# Spiritual California -- "The Clinic" Series

A third content line: evidence-based clinical treatments. EMDR and the rest of what the journal
keeps telling people to ask for.

*(Working title. Alternatives: "The Treatment Room", "First Line", "What Actually Treats This".)*

---

## 1. Why this line exists

The 50-article journal is mostly Tier C and D — complementary modalities with modest or absent
evidence. Across it, we repeatedly wrote sentences like:

> *"Trauma-focused CBT, prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy and EMDR have evidence
> bases orders of magnitude larger and appear in clinical guidelines."* — Somatic Experiencing, IFS
>
> *"If insomnia has lasted more than a month, ask your doctor about CBT-I by name."* — three separate
> articles

**We told readers what the real treatment is and then gave them nowhere to go.** This series
closes that loop.

**Strategic consequences, stated plainly:**

- This line is **mostly Tier A and B**. It changes the site's centre of gravity.
- It routes readers **toward licensed clinicians and away from the marketplace** more often than
  anything else we publish.
- It makes the journal's Tier C and D articles *more* credible, not less — a site that publishes
  the strong stuff has standing to publish the weak stuff honestly.

That trade should be made deliberately, not discovered after launch.

---

## 2. Format

Closer to the journal than to "What To Do." Same eight-beat structure and evidence-tier badge,
with three modifications:

- **"Is this available to me?"** replaces the journal's cost section. Insurance, waiting times,
  telehealth availability, California-specific access.
- **"What the sessions actually involve"** must be concrete enough to reduce fear. Most people
  avoid effective treatment because they imagine it wrongly.
- **"How to ask for it"** — the exact words to use with a GP or insurer. This is the most
  practically valuable section in the series and does not exist on any competitor site.

**Length:** 1,200-1,600 words. **Tier badge:** yes — this is where it earns its place.

## 4. The two volatile articles

Psychedelic-assisted therapy is moving fast enough that anything written today will be wrong
within months. As verified in July 2026:

- **MDMA:** FDA issued a Complete Response Letter in **August 2024** declining approval for
  Lykos Therapeutics' PTSD application and requiring an additional Phase 3 trial. MDMA remains
  Schedule I in the US. **Australia is the only country where it can be legally prescribed**,
  under the TGA Authorised Prescriber scheme since July 2023.
- **Psilocybin:** Schedule I, no FDA approval. Compass Pathways' COMP360 is furthest advanced,
  with a rolling NDA review granted in April 2026 and a decision plausibly late 2026 or early
  2027. State-level therapeutic programmes are live in **Oregon and Colorado**.
- **Context:** Executive Order 14401 (April 2026) directs expedited review; FDA finalized
  psychedelic trial guidance in July 2026 specifically addressing **functional unblinding** —
  participants correctly identifying whether they received the active drug, which biases
  outcomes.

**That last point deserves the article's spine.** Functional unblinding is precisely the problem
this journal identified across fifty modalities: you cannot conceal the intervention, so
expectancy contaminates the result. The FDA has now written formal guidance about it. That is a
remarkable convergence and it makes these two articles the most intellectually interesting in
the series.

**Editorial requirements for #26 and #27:**
- A visible `verifiedAsOf` date in the header, rendered on the page.
- A 90-day review cadence, enforced as a build warning.
- No prediction of approval dates. Report status, not forecasts.
- Explicit statement that state-legal programmes are **not** FDA-approved medical treatment, and
  that ketamine clinics are a different thing entirely.

## 5. Editorial hazards

**The EFT name collision.** *Emotional Freedom Techniques* (tapping — journal article #34, Tier C)
and *Emotionally Focused Therapy* (couples therapy — this series #20, Tier A/B) share an
acronym and nothing else. A reader searching "EFT therapy" could land on either. Both articles
need a disambiguation line in the first paragraph and a cross-link. Never use the bare acronym
in a title or slug.

**Scope discipline.** These articles describe treatments; they do not diagnose, recommend, or
suggest that any reader has a condition. Every one routes to a clinician for the decision.

**The marketplace tension.** Roughly 20 of these 33 articles will conclude, in effect, "see a
licensed clinician, not a practitioner on this site." That is correct and it is the point. But
you should count the cost before commissioning rather than after.

**Do not publish a self-assessment quiz.** For this content category it is the obvious growth
tactic and the wrong one.

## 7. One thing to decide before commissioning

**Is a wellness marketplace the right publisher for clinical psychotherapy content?**

The case for: readers are already arriving here confused about what treats what, and nobody is
giving them an honest map. The evidence-tier discipline that made the journal credible works
even better on material where the evidence is strong.

The case against: it stretches the brand from "spiritual and wellness practices, honestly
assessed" to "all mental health treatment," and you will be publishing authoritative content
about treatments none of your practitioners deliver.

**Our recommendation:** publish it, but frame the series explicitly as *navigation* rather than
*authority*. The reader-facing promise should be "here is what exists and how to ask for it,"
not "here is our clinical guidance." That framing is honest, it is defensible, and it is exactly
what the audience lacks.
