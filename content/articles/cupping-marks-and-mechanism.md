Michael Phelps arrived at the 2016 Olympics covered in dark circular bruises, and cupping
went from obscure to ubiquitous in about a week. It has been visible ever since -- on
athletes, in physiotherapy clinics, on Instagram.

The research since then has been substantial. Seventy-two randomized trials, more than five
thousand participants, several meta-analyses. And it has converged on a finding that is
genuinely awkward: cupping works well against nothing, and not measurably better than a
convincing fake.

The reason we may be stuck there for a long time is the same reason Phelps made it famous.
You cannot hide the marks.

## What it actually is

A cup -- glass, silicone or plastic -- is placed on the skin and suction applied, either by
briefly heating the air inside with a flame before placement or by a hand pump. The skin and
superficial tissue are drawn up into the cup, which stays for five to fifteen minutes.

**Dry cupping** is suction alone. **Wet cupping** (*hijama*, prominent in Middle Eastern
traditional medicine) involves small superficial incisions before suction so that a small
quantity of blood is drawn. **Moving or gliding cupping** slides an oiled cup across the
skin. **Pulsatile cupping** cycles the suction mechanically.

The characteristic circular marks are not bruises from impact. They are the result of
negative pressure drawing blood into the tissue beneath the skin, and they take three days
to two weeks to clear.

Proposed mechanisms include increased local blood flow, mechanical decompression of tissue,
stimulation of pressure and pain receptors modulating pain signalling, and a mild
inflammatory response. In traditional Chinese medicine the framing is the movement of
stagnant qi and blood.

## What the research shows

**Against no treatment, the effect is large and consistent.** The most careful synthesis is
Cramer and colleagues' systematic review and meta-analysis in *The Journal of Pain*, which
found
[large short-term effects on pain intensity compared with no treatment](https://www.jpain.org/article/S1526-5900(20)30003-1/fulltext),
with cupping superior for low back pain, neck pain and osteoarthritis, and dry, wet and
pulsatile cupping all outperforming no treatment. For functional disability the effect was
medium-sized against no treatment (SMD −0.66) and also significant against other active
treatments (SMD −0.52).

**Against sham cupping, it is not.** The same review found no evidence of statistically
significant effects on pain compared with sham cupping. For disability, the comparison
against sham gave SMD −0.26 with a confidence interval crossing zero (−0.57 to 0.05). Only
two sham-controlled trials existed to analyse -- which is the core problem, not a footnote.

**The wider literature is large and uniformly low-quality.** A 2025 update pooling
[72 trials and 5,720 participants assessed every single included trial as having high risk of bias](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11955767/).
Across fourteen separate meta-analyses on pain scores, cure rate, quality of life, sleep and
adverse events, all the evidence was graded low or very low quality, citing selection bias,
detection bias, inconsistency between trials and potential publication bias. The trials
mostly reported benefit -- average VAS reductions ranging from 0.16 to 7.0 cm, which is
itself a suspiciously wide spread.

**One comparison is genuinely interesting.** A 2025 network meta-analysis ranking
interventions for chronic non-specific low back pain placed
[cupping at 77.7% on the SUCRA ranking, ahead of diclofenac at 68.8% and paracetamol at 24.5%, with usual care last at 3.8%](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0965229925000421).
The authors' conclusion is the useful part: they recommend future trials use objective
outcomes and control interventions with "physiological inertia" specifically in order to
isolate cupping's true effect from psychological bias. Even the researchers ranking it
favourably are saying the design cannot yet separate the two.

**Where the evidence stands.** A large body of consistently positive but high-risk-of-bias trials, with the
sham-controlled comparison -- the one that would tell us whether the suction matters -- showing
no significant difference in the only two studies available.

## The blinding problem, which deserves its own section

In the trials reviewed, none reported blinding of the therapists. Only two attempted to blind
patients. And the reason is obvious once stated: cupping leaves conspicuous circular marks for
days. The patient knows. Anyone assessing them knows.

Sham cupping exists -- cups with minimal suction, or valves that release pressure -- but it
either fails to convince or fails to be inert. Every ingredient other than the suction
survives in the sham: the practitioner's time, the ritual, the sensation of pressure, the
strong expectation set by the visible marks.

This is not a reason to dismiss cupping. It is a reason to be honest that the question
"does the suction itself do anything?" may not be answerable with current methods, and that
anyone claiming it has been settled in either direction is overstating.

## What it does not do

**It does not remove toxins from your body.** This is the most common explanation given and
it has no basis. Nothing is being extracted in dry cupping. In wet cupping, a small amount of
blood is removed; there is no evidence that blood contains a toxin load whose removal helps.

**It does not "break up fascial adhesions."** Suction lifts tissue temporarily. Whether that
produces durable structural change has not been demonstrated.

**It has not been shown to improve athletic performance or recovery** -- the use that made it
famous. The evidence base is in chronic pain, not sport.

**And it has not been shown to outperform a convincing sham.** That is the honest headline,
and no amount of positive no-treatment comparisons changes it.

**Adverse events are real and more frequent than no treatment.** The Cramer review found
cupping associated with more adverse events than no treatment or other active treatments,
though not more than sham. The marks themselves are expected. Documented complications include
burns from fire cupping, skin infection, and -- with wet cupping -- bloodborne infection risk if
sterile single-use equipment is not used. Anaemia has been reported with excessive repeated wet
cupping.

**Avoid it** if you take anticoagulants or have a bleeding disorder, over broken or infected
skin, over varicose veins, during pregnancy on the abdomen or low back, and with significant
anaemia. Wet cupping specifically should be avoided if you are immunocompromised.

## What a session is like

Usually an add-on to acupuncture or massage rather than a standalone appointment. You lie face
down with the area exposed and oiled. Cups are placed -- typically four to eight along the
back -- and you feel a strong pulling sensation as suction takes hold. It is a distinctive
feeling: tight and slightly alarming for the first minute, then oddly comfortable.

Cups stay five to fifteen minutes. Some practitioners then slide them, which is sharper. On
removal the skin underneath is dark red to purple in a clean circle.

Afterwards the area feels warm and loose, and often mildly tender the next day. The marks are
darkest at 24 to 48 hours. Plan around anything involving a backless dress or a swimming
pool.

Expect $30-$60 added to another treatment, or $60-$100 standalone in California.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often use cupping for persistent upper back and neck tension, for chronic low back
pain, and as an adjunct within an acupuncture course. The chronic-pain evidence is where the
research sits, and the network meta-analysis result -- however cautiously it should be read --
is not trivial.

It suits people who like strong sensation and are unbothered by visible marks. It suits poorly
anyone on blood thinners, anyone who needs to keep their back unmarked, and anyone who wants
to know that the specific mechanism has been validated.

## How to choose a practitioner

Cupping is generally performed within another licensed scope, which is what you should look
for.

**Prefer an L.Ac.** In California, licensed acupuncturists are trained in cupping and are
accountable for it. Physical therapists and CMTs may also practise it depending on scope.

**For wet cupping, ask about single-use sterile equipment and sharps disposal, and expect a
confident answer.** This is the one form with genuine infection risk, and the answer should be
immediate and specific.

**For fire cupping, ask about their burn protocol.** Burns are the most commonly documented
complication.

**And ask what they claim.** A practitioner who says people often find it eases muscular
tension and that the research is early and mixed is being accurate. One who says it draws out
toxins, or that it has been proven superior to sham treatment, is not.

If your goal is well-evidenced treatment for chronic musculoskeletal pain from within the same
tradition, read our acupuncture article -- the individual patient data there is considerably
stronger than anything available on cupping.
