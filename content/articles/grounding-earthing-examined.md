Walking barefoot on grass is free, pleasant, and almost certainly good for you in the
ordinary ways that being outdoors and moving are good for you. Nothing in this article argues
otherwise.

What this article is about is the specific claim layered on top: that direct electrical
contact with the earth transfers electrons into the body, neutralizing free radicals and
reducing inflammation -- and that you should therefore buy a conductive bedsheet.

That claim has a literature. It also has a problem that appears in the disclosure statements
of the papers themselves.

## What it actually is

Grounding, or earthing, means making direct conductive contact between skin and the earth's
surface -- barefoot on soil, grass, sand or unsealed concrete -- or simulating it indoors with
mats, sheets, patches and wristbands connected to the ground pin of an electrical outlet.

The proposed mechanism is electron transfer. The earth carries a negative surface charge; the
argument is that contact allows free electrons to enter the body, where they neutralize
positively charged free radicals and reduce oxidative stress and inflammation. Downstream
claims include improved sleep, normalized cortisol rhythm, reduced pain, improved heart rate
variability, and reduced blood viscosity.

Protocols in the studies typically involve 20 to 60 minutes daily, or grounded sleeping over
several weeks.

## What the research shows

**The literature is real, and it is small and concentrated.** The most-cited paper is a 2015
review by Oschman, Chevalier and Brown in the *Journal of Inflammation Research*, arguing that
grounding affects inflammation, immune response and wound healing. Chevalier and colleagues
have also published randomized double-blind pilot work reporting that one hour of contact with
the earth's surface improves inflammation markers and facial blood flow, using thermal and
laser speckle contrast imaging.

The individual studies are typically small pilots. Sample sizes in the tens, short durations,
surrogate outcomes -- imaging, viscosity, cortisol, heart rate variability -- rather than
clinical endpoints. That is normal for an early field, and on its own would leave grounding
as an unremarkable early-stage evidence base.

**Two problems make it more complicated than that.**

**First, the disclosed financial interests.** The conflict-of-interest statements in the 2015
*Journal of Inflammation Research* paper disclose that two of its authors are independent
contractors for Earth FX Inc. and hold shares in the company, which sells grounding products.
The peer-reviewed pilot on delayed-onset muscle soreness lists an author's affiliation on the
title page as Earth FX Inc., Palm Springs, California. Clinton Ober, who popularized the
concept and funded much of the early research, runs a company selling grounding equipment.

None of this is concealed -- it is printed in the papers, which is exactly how disclosure is
supposed to work. And industry-funded research is normal across medicine; pharmaceutical
companies fund drug trials. But the standard remedy in those fields is independent
replication by researchers with no stake in the outcome, and in this field that remedy has
largely not arrived. A body of evidence produced almost entirely by people who sell the
product is not disqualified. It is provisional until someone else repeats it.

**Second, the blinding problem is severe.** Sham grounding uses a disconnected wire. But
participants can sometimes tell -- through faint tingling, static discharge, or temperature
differences at the contact point. Where blinding fails in a study measuring self-reported
sleep, pain and mood, expectation is a live explanation for the findings. This has been raised
repeatedly in critical appraisals of the field.

An advocacy review in *Explore* set out the case for including earthing in integrative and
lifestyle medicine, summarizing the proposed mechanisms across
[blood fluidity, autonomic regulation, cortisol normalization and inflammation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1550830719305476).
It is a useful map of the claims, and it is written by proponents -- the honest way to read it
is as the strongest version of the argument rather than as a neutral assessment.

**Where the evidence stands.** A coherent proposed mechanism, a consistent set of small positive findings, and an
evidence base whose independence and blinding are both compromised in ways that could
plausibly account for the results.

## What it does not do

**It has not been shown to reduce clinical inflammation in a way that changes health
outcomes.** Surrogate markers moving in small pilots is not the same as disease risk changing.

**It has not been shown to treat any condition** -- not fibromyalgia, not autoimmune disease,
not cardiovascular disease, whatever the marketing says.

**"Free radical neutralization by earth electrons" is a hypothesis, not an established
mechanism.** The physics of surface charge is real; whether meaningful quantities of electrons
enter the body and reach sites of inflammation is not demonstrated.

**And the products have not been shown to outperform the ground.** If the mechanism is
conductive contact, standing barefoot on damp grass costs nothing.

**Safety, briefly and importantly.** Outdoor barefoot grounding is very safe -- watch for
glass, sharps and, for anyone with diabetic neuropathy, unnoticed foot injury. Indoor
grounding products connect you to a building's electrical ground, and while properly wired
outlets make this safe, faulty wiring makes it a genuine hazard. If you use a grounded sheet,
have the outlet checked. Anyone with an implanted electrical device -- pacemaker, defibrillator,
neurostimulator -- should ask their cardiologist first.

## What it is like to do

Take your shoes off and stand or walk on grass, soil, sand or unsealed concrete for twenty
to forty minutes. Wet grass conducts better than dry, which is one reason morning is often
recommended.

What people report is unremarkable and pleasant: the temperature and texture of the ground, a
gradual settling, and the specific effect of being outdoors without a task. The characteristic
experience is difficult to separate from the experience of being outside barefoot, which is
also the central research problem.

Cost: free outdoors. Grounding mats and sheets run $30-$300 and up, and this is where our
honest advice is firmest -- given the state of the evidence and who produced it, we would not
spend meaningfully on products before independent replication exists.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often come to grounding for sleep, for general depletion, and as part of a broader
attempt to spend more time outside. The last of those is worth taking seriously as the actual
mechanism candidate: our forest bathing article covers a related literature with independent
funding and better controls.

It suits people who want a free, low-risk daily habit that gets them outdoors and barefoot.
It suits poorly anyone buying an expensive sleep system on the strength of this evidence, and
anyone hoping to treat an inflammatory condition.

Our honest framing: do the free version enthusiastically. Treat the paid version as unproven,
and revisit if independent groups replicate the findings.

## How to evaluate what you are told

There is no practitioner here, so the judgment is about claims and products.

**Check who funded the study you are shown.** This field makes it easy -- the disclosures are
in the papers. If every citation traces back to the same small group with commercial ties,
that is the relevant fact.

**Be suspicious of specificity.** Claims about precise cortisol curves, viscosity percentages
or immune markers rest on pilot studies with very small samples.

**Ignore anything invoking Schumann resonance** as a mechanism for health effects. The
resonance is a real electromagnetic phenomenon in the earth-ionosphere cavity; its
appropriation as a biological healing frequency is not supported.

**And if a seller cannot tell you who paid for the research, assume they know.**
