Gratitude is the most heavily promoted practice in the wellbeing industry. It costs nothing,
requires no equipment, fits in three minutes, and every app, corporate wellness programme and
self-help book has a version of it.

The research is genuinely interesting, and it hinges almost entirely on one methodological
question: what do you compare gratitude journaling *against*?

Compare it against a waiting list and the effects look solid. Compare it against writing about
anything else at all, and most of the effect disappears. Both results come from the same
meta-analyses, and the honest version of this practice lives in the gap between them.

## What it actually is

Gratitude interventions come in three main forms, and the research treats them separately.

**Gratitude lists** -- writing down three to five things you are grateful for, daily or weekly.
The most-tested and most-promoted format.

**Gratitude letters** -- writing a detailed letter of thanks to someone who has helped you,
sometimes delivered and read aloud.

**Gratitude reflection** -- structured noticing without writing.

The underlying proposition is that attention allocation is habitual and modifiable: that people
default to scanning for problems, and that deliberately directing attention toward benefits
shifts the balance over time.

## What the research shows

**The pooled effects are real but small.** Cregg and Cheavens meta-analyzed 27 studies with
3,675 participants and found gratitude interventions had a
[small effect on symptoms of depression and anxiety at post-test (g = −0.29) and at follow-up (g = −0.23)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10902-020-00236-6).
A separate meta-analysis of 25 randomized controlled trials with 6,745 participants found
expressed gratitude interventions produced
[a significant effect on psychological wellbeing relative to neutral comparison groups, Hedges' g = 0.22](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41042-023-00086-6).

Small, positive, replicated across large samples. So far, a modest but respectable practice.

**Now the comparison that matters.** Cregg and Cheavens found a medium effect when gratitude
interventions were compared with waitlist-only conditions -- but
[only a trivial effect when compared with putatively inert control conditions involving any kind of activity](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/339431121_Gratitude_Interventions_Effective_Self-help_A_Meta-analysis_of_the_Impact_on_Symptoms_of_Depression_and_Anxiety).

Read that again, because it is the whole article. Gratitude journaling beats doing nothing. It
barely beats writing about your day, your errands, or a neutral topic for the same three
minutes.

Davis and colleagues' earlier meta-analysis found the same structure with more granularity:
gratitude interventions outperformed measurement-only controls on wellbeing with a small effect
size, and outperformed alternative-activity conditions on measures of *gratitude* with a
medium effect and on wellbeing with a small effect --
[but not on anxiety](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/08870446.2024.2336042).
Those authors closed by arguing that enthusiasm for gratitude interventions should be tempered
until longer and more powerful versions demonstrate stronger evidence.

**The placebo hypothesis is explicitly on the table.** Cregg and Cheavens open by noting that
previous reviews suggest wellbeing improvements associated with gratitude interventions may be
attributable to placebo effects -- citing Davis and the earlier theoretical review by Wood and
colleagues. That is not a sceptic's framing imported from outside; it is how the field
introduces its own literature.

**Interpersonal versions are not clearly better.** Despite participants reporting that the
relational elements feel most valuable, meta-analytic evidence does not support additional
benefits from those designs as a category.

**Where the evidence stands.** Small, consistent, replicated effects on wellbeing and mood, with the caveat that
most of the advantage over active alternatives is modest -- and, for anxiety, may not exist.

## What it does not do

**It does not treat depression or anxiety.** A small effect on symptoms is not a treatment, and
gratitude interventions have consistently underperformed relative to their promotion in
clinical populations.

**It is not clearly better than writing about anything else.** If the mechanism is the
structured act of reflective writing rather than the gratitude content specifically, that is a
different -- and cheaper -- claim.

**It does not require positivity about your circumstances**, and the version that does is
actively harmful. Gratitude practice used to suppress legitimate grievance, grief or anger --
"be grateful, it could be worse" -- is not what any of these studies tested, and it has a real
cost for people in difficult situations. Nothing in this literature supports telling someone
in a bad situation to feel thankful about it.

**And more is not established as better.** Some evidence suggests weekly lists may work as well
as daily ones, with the daily version losing potency as the same items recur.

## How to do it, if you do

The protocol as tested is unglamorous and takes three minutes.

- **Write, do not just think.** Nearly all the tested versions involve writing.
- **Be specific.** "My colleague stayed late to help with the deck" rather than "my job."
- **Go for depth over breadth.** Three items elaborated appear to work better than ten listed.
- **Weekly may beat daily.** Novelty seems to matter, and daily lists tend to repeat.
- **Include people, not only circumstances**, and where a letter is possible, deliver it -- the
  interpersonal versions are not clearly superior in the data, but they are the ones people
  find most meaningful.

Cost: nothing. Which, given a small but real effect size and no plausible downside, makes this
one of the better ratios in the journal even after the deflation above.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often take up gratitude practice during periods of low-grade dissatisfaction rather than
acute distress -- the state of having a good life and not feeling it. The evidence fits that
use better than it fits clinical use.

It suits people who will actually write for three minutes rather than intend to. It suits
poorly anyone in acute grief or crisis, where the practice can feel like an instruction to
minimize what is happening, and anyone hoping it will substitute for treatment.

Our honest framing: this is a small, free, low-risk practice with genuinely replicated modest
benefits, that has been marketed as a transformation. Do it as the small thing it is and it
holds up. Expect what the marketing promises and it will not.

## What to watch for in how it is sold

Gratitude has no practitioners and no credential, so the caution here is about programmes and
claims rather than people.

**Be sceptical of large claims.** Anyone citing gratitude research to promise substantial
changes in happiness, health or income is not describing g = 0.22.

**Be wary of gratitude used as a management tool.** Workplace gratitude programmes that arrive
in place of addressing workload or pay are using a psychological intervention to substitute
for a structural fix, and employees generally notice.

**And be alert to the toxic-positivity version.** A programme that discourages complaint, or
frames difficulty as a failure of appreciation, has departed from the evidence and into
something else. The studies asked people to notice good things. They did not ask them to stop
noticing bad ones.

If you want the practice with a larger evidence base in this neighbourhood, expressive writing
and structured awe practice are both covered separately in this journal -- with, in each case,
their own deflations honestly reported.
