Between 2018 and 2020, researchers at three American medical centers recruited 276
adults with diagnosed anxiety disorders and split them into two groups. One group
received escitalopram, a standard first-line antidepressant prescribed for anxiety
millions of times a year. The other group was sent to a meditation class.

Not a wellness weekend. A specific, structured, eight-week course: two and a half
hours in a room every week, a full-day retreat somewhere in week five or six, and
forty-five minutes of practice at home, every day. Then blinded clinicians -- who did
not know which group anyone was in -- assessed everybody.

The meditation group did not lose.

## What it actually is

Mindfulness meditation, as it appears in clinical research, is almost always
Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction: MBSR. It was assembled in 1979 at the University
of Massachusetts Medical Center by Jon Kabat-Zinn, who stripped the Buddhist framing
out of vipassana practice and rebuilt what remained as a secular skills course for
patients whose chronic pain medicine had run out of answers for.

The practice itself is unglamorous. You attend to the breath. You notice, in some
detail, the sensations moving through the body. You watch a thought arrive, and
instead of arguing with it or obeying it, you register it as a passing event and
return to the breath. Then you do that again, several hundred times, and the
returning *is* the practice -- not the staying.

What matters for anyone evaluating the evidence is that MBSR is standardized. It has a
curriculum, a duration, a certified-instructor pathway and a home-practice dose. That
is precisely why it can be tested against a drug, and why most of the good research
on "meditation" is really research on this one protocol.

## What the research shows

The trial described above is called TAME, and its results were published in *JAMA
Psychiatry* in January 2023. It was designed as a noninferiority trial -- a study that
asks not "is this better?" but the more practically useful "is this at least not
meaningfully worse?" -- with the margin fixed in advance.

MBSR met it. Across the primary analysis of 208 patients, the eight-week course was
[noninferior to escitalopram on clinician-rated anxiety severity](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2798510),
and the authors concluded it represented a well-tolerated treatment option with
comparable effectiveness to a first-line medication.

A [secondary analysis published in *JAMA Network Open* in 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39382900/)
went back to the same trial and looked at what patients themselves reported --
anxiety, depression, quality of life. The pattern held, with outcomes similar to
antidepressant pharmacotherapy and, the authors noted, potentially fewer side effects.

None of this arrived from nowhere. A meta-analytic review in the *Journal of
Consulting and Clinical Psychology* had already pooled the mindfulness-based therapy
literature and found
[moderate effects on both anxiety and depressive symptoms](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018555)
more than a decade earlier. TAME's contribution was to put the practice in a head-to-head
against the actual standard of care, which is a much harder test and a much more
useful one.

That combination -- replicated moderate effects, plus a properly powered
noninferiority trial against an active comparator -- is why the evidence here is stronger
than for almost anything else on this site.

## What it does not do

Several things, and they are worth being blunt about.

**It is not a light-touch practice.** The dose in TAME was forty-five minutes a day
plus a weekly class plus a retreat day. That is roughly forty hours over eight weeks.
Ten minutes on an app during a commute is a reasonable habit, but it is not the
intervention that produced these results, and it should not be sold as though it were.

**Noninferior is not superior.** The trial showed the course was not meaningfully
worse than the drug. It did not show it was better. For some people medication will be
the faster, safer or only workable option, and nothing here argues otherwise.

**The control-group problem is real.** Researchers wrote to *JAMA Psychiatry* after
publication to
[raise exactly this point](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/article-abstract/2802426):
an eight-week course with a warm instructor, a committed group and a weekend retreat
delivers a great deal of nonspecific benefit -- structure, attention, belonging -- and
disentangling that from the meditation itself is genuinely difficult. This is an open
question in the field, not a settled one.

**And it is not universally benign.** A minority of people find intensive practice
destabilizing, particularly those with a history of trauma or dissociation. A
competent teacher will ask about this before you start. If yours does not, that tells
you something.

## What a session is like

The first class usually disappoints people, which teachers tend to regard as a good
sign. There is no incense and very little talk of consciousness. You will likely eat a
single raisin with absurd slowness, lie on a floor while your attention is walked from
your left little toe to the top of your skull, and then be asked to report what you
noticed -- not what you achieved.

Weeks two through four are where most people want to quit. The mind is loud, the
forty-five minutes feel long, and there is no obvious reward. Around week five
something shifts for many people: not calm, exactly, but a small, useful gap between a
feeling arriving and the reaction to it.

In the Bay Area, an eight-week MBSR course typically runs $400-$700 through a clinic,
university program or independent certified instructor. Wear whatever you can sit
still in for an hour. Bring nothing.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often seek mindfulness training for anxiety, for chronic stress, for insomnia,
and for the specific occupational hazard of a mind that will not stop preparing for
meetings that have not happened yet. It is also frequently sought by people who have
found talk therapy useful for understanding a pattern but not for interrupting it in
the moment.

It suits people who are willing to do something dull, daily, for two months without
early evidence that it is working. That is a real filter, and an honest teacher will
say so at the outset.

## How to choose a practitioner

Ask three questions.

**Where did you train?** For MBSR specifically, look for teachers trained through a
recognized program -- Brown University's Mindfulness Center and the University of
Massachusetts lineage are the established pathways. "Certified mindfulness coach" from
an unnamed online course is not the same credential.

**How long is your course, and what is the daily home practice?** If the answer is
substantially less than eight weeks and forty-five minutes, that is fine -- but it is
not the protocol the research tested, and the teacher should say so plainly rather
than borrowing the evidence.

**What do you do if a student becomes distressed?** A good answer involves screening
beforehand, a referral network, and a clear statement that they are a teacher and not
a therapist. A vague answer is the red flag.

Anyone promising that meditation will cure your anxiety is telling you something
untrue about the research and something worrying about their judgment.
