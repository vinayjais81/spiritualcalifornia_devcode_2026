Most arguments about acupuncture are conducted by people quoting different studies at
each other. There is a reason for this: for thirty years the literature was a mess of
small trials with incompatible designs, and you could assemble a convincing case for
almost any position you already held.

In 2012, a group of researchers led by Andrew Vickers at Memorial Sloan Kettering
decided to end that particular argument by force. Rather than pooling published
summaries -- the usual method, and a fragile one -- they went back to the trialists
themselves and asked for the raw data on every individual patient. Twenty-nine trials
handed it over. Just under eighteen thousand patients. In 2017 they updated the whole
thing again.

The result is the most serious body of evidence anyone has assembled on this question,
and it does not say what either camp wants it to say.

## What it actually is

Acupuncture involves the insertion of very fine needles -- typically 0.16 to 0.30 mm,
thinner than a hypodermic and usually not felt as a puncture -- into specific points
on the body, where they remain for fifteen to thirty minutes.

The traditional explanatory framework is qi circulating through channels, or meridians,
which can be blocked or depleted and which needling regulates. This framework is
roughly two thousand years old, internally coherent, and not a physiological model in
the modern sense. No structure corresponding to a meridian has been identified.

That is a genuine problem for the theory, and not automatically a problem for the
practice. Aspirin worked for seventy years before anyone understood prostaglandins.
Whether needling produces a real effect and whether qi exists are two entirely
separate questions, and the research addresses only the first.

## What the research shows

The Acupuncture Trialists' Collaboration restricted itself to four conditions where
the trial evidence was strongest: chronic back and neck pain, shoulder pain, chronic
headache, and osteoarthritis.

The [2018 update in *The Journal of Pain*](https://www.jpain.org/article/S1526-5900(17)30780-0/fulltext)
reached four conclusions, stated by the authors in unusually direct language: that
acupuncture has a clinically relevant effect on chronic pain which persists over time;
that this effect cannot be explained by placebo alone; that factors beyond the specific
act of needling are nonetheless important contributors; and that referral for
acupuncture is a reasonable option for patients with chronic pain.

Read that second and third point together, because they are the whole story. Something
real is happening that is not purely expectation. And something substantial is also
happening that is not the needles.

The [original 2012 analysis in *Archives of Internal Medicine*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK100553/)
sharpened the point. Acupuncture outperformed standard care alone, and it also
outperformed sham acupuncture -- but the gap between real and sham needling was, in the
reviewers' own assessment, relatively modest. The larger share of the benefit came from
the comparison against doing nothing in particular.

A companion analysis of 17,922 patients in *PLOS ONE* then asked which features of
treatment predicted better outcomes, and found that
[characteristics of the acupuncture and the acupuncturist were associated with results](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0077438).
The person, the setting and the protocol mattered measurably -- which is exactly what
you would expect of a treatment whose effect is partly specific and partly contextual.

This is the honest middle position, and it satisfies neither camp. The effect is
real and replicated. It is also smaller than its advocates claim and larger than its
critics allow.

## What it does not do

**It does not work by unblocking meridians, as far as anyone can demonstrate.** A
practitioner may use that language as a clinical map, and it may be a useful one. It
is not a mechanism that has been shown to exist, and any article telling you otherwise
is not being straight with you.

**It is not a treatment for the conditions it is often marketed for.** The strong
evidence is for four chronic pain presentations. Claims about fertility, allergies,
weight loss, addiction, or "boosting immunity" rest on far weaker ground, and some
rest on nothing.

**The sham-controlled effect is modest.** If your standard for a worthwhile
intervention is a large advantage over convincingly faked needling, acupuncture does
not clear it. If your standard is a meaningful improvement over usual care that lasts,
it does. Both readings are defensible from the same data set.

**It does not replace a diagnosis.** New, escalating or unexplained pain needs a
medical workup before it needs needles.

Acupuncture is generally safe in trained hands, with serious adverse events rare.
Bruising and brief lightheadedness are the common ones.

## What a session is like

The first appointment is mostly conversation -- often forty-five minutes of it. A
licensed acupuncturist will ask about sleep, digestion, temperature, appetite and
menstrual cycle in a level of detail that can feel unrelated to your shoulder. They
will likely take your pulse at three positions on each wrist and look at your tongue.
This is diagnostic within the traditional system.

Then you lie down, usually partially clothed with towels arranged for access, and
somewhere between six and twenty needles go in. Insertion registers as a faint tap or
nothing at all. What people notice more is the deep, slightly heavy, faintly
electrical sensation that spreads afterwards -- traditionally called *de qi*. Many
people fall asleep. You are left alone for twenty to thirty minutes in a quiet room.

Expect $90-$180 per session in Northern California, with the initial visit higher.
Most practitioners will propose a course of four to six sessions before evaluating,
which is a reasonable structure and also worth pushing back on if nothing has shifted
by the end of it. Many California plans now cover acupuncture for pain -- worth
checking before you pay out of pocket.

## Who tends to find it useful

People most often seek acupuncture for persistent musculoskeletal pain -- the low back
that never fully resolved, the neck that seizes under deadline, the shoulder that has
outlived three courses of physiotherapy -- and for chronic headache and
osteoarthritis. These are the areas where the evidence is strongest, and the overlap is
not a coincidence.

It also tends to suit people who have exhausted the obvious options and are looking for
something to add rather than something to replace. The trial evidence supports exactly
that framing: acupuncture *added to* standard care beat standard care alone.

## How to choose a practitioner

In California, look for **L.Ac.** -- a state-licensed acupuncturist. California
licensure is among the most demanding in the country, requiring a lengthy masters-level
program and a state exam. Nationally, **Dipl.Ac. (NCCAOM)** is the equivalent signal.
A physician or chiropractor with a weekend certification in "dry needling" is doing
something related but not the same, and should say so.

Ask what conditions they most commonly treat, and how many sessions before they would
tell you it is not working. A practitioner who has a clear answer to the second
question is one worth booking.

Two red flags: anyone who advises you to stop a prescribed medication, and anyone who
sells a twenty-session package before the first needle.
