In 2014, researchers at the University of Vienna designed the study this practice needed. They
took 39 participants and split them four ways: repetitive drumming with shamanic journeying
instructions, drumming with relaxation instructions, instrumental meditation music with
journeying instructions, and meditation music with relaxation instructions.

Two variables, cleanly crossed. The drum, and the story you are told about what the drum is
for.

The result is the most useful thing anyone has established about shamanic journeying, and it
cuts in both directions at once.

## What it actually is

A shamanic journey is a guided visionary practice. You lie down, usually in the dark, while a
drum is beaten at a steady four to five beats per second. Following instructions, you visualize
descending through an opening in the earth -- a cave, a hole, a lake -- into what practitioners
call the Lower World, where you may meet a power animal or other figures. The experience is
frequently described as resembling a lucid dream. A change in the drumbeat, or a rattle, signals
the return.

The version taught across most of the Western world is **core shamanism**, codified by the
anthropologist Michael Harner in the 1970s and 80s: a deliberately stripped-down synthesis
distilled from practices found across many indigenous cultures, with the culturally specific
elements removed.

That is worth stating clearly rather than glossing. Core shamanism is a modern Western
construction assembled from other people's traditions. Whether that constitutes respectful
transmission or appropriation is a live and legitimate argument, and readers deserve to know
they are entering it. What it is not is an unbroken indigenous lineage, and practitioners who
present it as one are misrepresenting its history.

## What the research shows

**The Vienna study is the anchor.** Gingras, Pohler and Fitch published their four-condition
experiment in *PLOS ONE*. Salivary cortisol was measured before and after fifteen minutes of
listening.

Cortisol fell significantly in every group. And
[no significant differences were observed across conditions](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0102103).
Listening to music reduced cortisol regardless of whether it was shamanic drumming or
instrumental meditation music, and regardless of whether participants were told to journey or
simply to relax.

**But the subjective experiences were not equivalent.** Participants exposed to repetitive
drumming combined with shamanic instructions reported
[heaviness, a decreased sense of heart rate, and dreamlike experiences significantly more often](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0102103)
than those given the same drumming with relaxation instructions.

That is the finding in full. The combination of rhythm and framing produces a specific and
reproducible kind of experience. It does not produce a distinctive physiological signature --
at least not one visible in cortisol.

The authors' own conclusion is admirably direct: the subjective effects attributed to
repetitive drumming and shamanic journeying may not be reflected in differential endocrine
responses.

**Experienced practitioners look different from novices.** Later EEG work found that shamanic
practitioners showed
[increased gamma power during shamanic drumming that correlated with self-reported visual alterations, and increased criticality in low beta and gamma bands](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0149763426001636)
-- and these findings were exclusive to the drumming condition, suggesting trance-specific
neural activity. Practitioners also scored higher than controls on eight of eleven domains of a
validated altered-states questionnaire, including complex imagery, unity, spiritual experience
and disembodiment.

**And phenomenological research is genuinely substantial.** Studies have mapped the imagery
reported by naive participants during journeying, and a phenomenological study of
inexperienced students found enhanced perceptual phenomena, vivid visual imagery, and the
perception of the drum sound as a source of healing that facilitated the journey.

**Where the evidence stands, and it requires explanation.** There *is* real research here -- more than we
expected. But it is research on the *experience*, not on outcomes. Beyond a small feasibility
study of shamanic treatment for temporomandibular joint disorder, there are essentially no
clinical trials testing shamanic journeying as a treatment for anything. What has been
demonstrated is that the practice reliably produces a particular state of consciousness. What
that state does for a person's health has not been studied.

## What it does not do

**It has not been shown to treat any condition.** No trials, no outcomes, no evidence base.
Anyone presenting it as therapy for illness, addiction or mental health conditions is going
well beyond what exists.

**It does not produce a distinctive stress response.** The Vienna study is clear: fifteen
minutes of instrumental meditation music with relaxation instructions dropped cortisol just as
much. If the physiological claim matters to you, it did not survive its cleanest test.

**"Brainwave entrainment at 4 Hz" is an explanation, not a finding.** The drumming tempo is
often described as corresponding to theta frequency, with entrainment offered as the mechanism.
The EEG findings above are more complex than that and concern gamma-band activity in trained
practitioners.

**And the framing does a great deal of the work.** This is the study's most interesting
implication and its most uncomfortable one. The same fifteen minutes of drumming produced
markedly different experiences depending on what participants were told to expect. That does not
make the experiences unreal. It does mean the practice is at least partly a technology of
expectation.

**Safety.** Journeying is physically harmless. The genuine caution is psychological: deliberately
induced altered states can destabilize people with a history of psychosis, dissociation or
recent severe trauma, and there is no clinical screening in most settings. If this describes
you, talk to a clinician before attending a workshop -- and be aware that some practitioners
also work with plant medicines, which is an entirely different risk category not covered here.

## What a session is like

You lie on the floor with a blanket and cover your eyes. The facilitator explains the map --
Lower World, Middle World, Upper World -- and sets an intention or a question with you.

Then the drumming starts, and it does not vary. Fifteen to thirty minutes of the same steady
beat, fast enough to be insistent and monotonous enough to stop being interesting. Somewhere in
the first several minutes most people stop tracking it.

What people report is a good deal more vivid than ordinary daydreaming and less controllable:
imagery that arrives rather than being constructed, encounters with animal figures, a sense of
place. Many people are surprised by the specificity. Some experience nothing much beyond
relaxation, which facilitators typically treat as normal.

The callback rhythm brings you out. Then, usually, people describe what they saw -- to a
partner or the group -- and that telling is treated as part of the practice.

Sessions run $30-$80 in a group and $100-$200 individually in California. Recordings of
journeying drumbeats are freely available and the practice is often done solo at home.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often come to journeying for questions rather than symptoms -- decisions they cannot
think their way through, grief they cannot articulate, or a general sense of being
disconnected from anything larger. It draws people who are curious about altered states without
substances, and people who find imagery a more natural language than analysis.

It suits people who can hold an experience as meaningful without requiring it to be literally
true. That is the whole skill, and people who have it tend to get a great deal from this.

It suits poorly anyone seeking treatment for a diagnosed condition, anyone with the psychiatric
histories noted above, and anyone who needs the metaphysics to be verified before they will
participate -- because it will not be.

## How to choose a practitioner

There is no licence and no regulation. What varies enormously is honesty and judgment.

**Ask where they trained.** The Foundation for Shamanic Studies is the established core shamanism
training organization. Others exist. A practitioner should be able to name theirs and describe
what it involved.

**Ask how they describe what they do.** "This is a guided visionary practice that many people
find meaningful, and there is very little research on it" is honest. "This will heal your
illness by retrieving your soul" is not, and it is the framing under which people delay
medical care.

**Ask about screening and about what happens if someone becomes distressed.** A thoughtful
facilitator asks about psychiatric history before a session and has a plan.

**And ask directly about lineage claims.** A practitioner who is clear that core shamanism is a
modern Western synthesis is being straightforward with you. One claiming initiation into a
specific indigenous tradition should be able to say which, from whom, and with what
relationship to that community -- and the answer will tell you a great deal.

**Two firm lines.** Anyone suggesting this replaces medical or psychiatric treatment. And anyone
offering plant medicines alongside it, which is a different practice with different legality and
substantially different risk.
