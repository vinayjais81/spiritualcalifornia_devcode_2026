Everything else in this journal asks you to add something to your week -- a class, a session, a
practice, a practitioner. This one asks you to change where you point your attention on a walk
you were going to take anyway.

That is the entire intervention in the study that made awe research famous, and it is the
reason this article is here.

## What it actually is

Awe, in the research definition established by Dacher Keltner and Jonathan Haidt, is the
emotion produced by encountering something vast that does not fit your existing mental
frameworks -- vast in scale, in beauty, in skill, in moral quality. Nature and art are the
common triggers; so is music, so is collective experience like a ceremony or a crowd.

Its distinguishing feature in the literature is what researchers call the **small self**: awe
reduces self-focus, shrinks the felt importance of one's own concerns, and orients attention
outward. That is the mechanism, and it is what separates awe from ordinary pleasure. Pleasant
experiences make you feel good. Awe makes you feel small, and it turns out that this is often
the useful part.

An "awe practice" simply means deliberately seeking those experiences and attending to them
rather than waiting for them to arrive.

## What the research shows

**The awe walk trial is the anchor.** Sturm, Keltner and colleagues at UCSF and Berkeley
randomized about sixty healthy adults aged 75 and over to eight weeks of weekly fifteen-minute
outdoor walks. The awe group was instructed to
[pay attention to the vastness and novelty of the environment around them](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/44/NCT03550144/Prot_000.pdf)
-- to tap into a childhood sense of wonder, notice the scale of things, and go somewhere new.
The control group was told to walk, with no additional instruction.

The awe group reported increased daily prosocial positive emotions -- gratitude, compassion --
and less daily distress across the eight weeks. The study, published in *Emotion* under the
title *Big smile, small self*, also included a detail that is unusual and rather lovely: it
tracked selfies participants took on their walks, and found the awe group's photographs
increasingly gave more of the frame to the surroundings and less to themselves, with bigger
smiles.

The population choice was deliberate. As the researchers noted, loneliness and negative
emotion carry well-documented health costs in adults over 75, and an intervention that turns
attention outward speaks directly to that.

**A clinical trial has since followed.** A randomized controlled clinical trial published in
*Scientific Reports* in 2025 reported that
[awe reduced depressive symptoms and improved wellbeing](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-96555-w)
-- moving the field from prosocial emotion into clinical outcomes, which is a meaningful step
up.

**The observational and field literature is broad.** Awe has been linked to
[elevated life satisfaction and reduced daily stress](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000267),
and a longitudinal study during COVID-19 examined daily experiences of awe against stress,
somatic health and wellbeing. Anderson, Monroy and Keltner studied awe in nature across
military veterans, at-risk youth and college students. Separate work has found that contact
with nature reduces rumination, with awe and mood as mediators.

**Where the evidence stands: and honestly assessed.** The awe walk trial has a real limitation: the control group
received *no instruction*, only a walk. The awe group received an instruction, a framing and an
intention. Some of the difference may be attributable to having any deliberate focus at all
rather than to awe specifically -- the same active-control problem that deflated gratitude
research elsewhere in this journal. The samples are also small and the outcomes are
self-reported.

What holds up: the effect direction is consistent across trial, longitudinal and field
studies, the mechanism is well specified, and the newer clinical trial is a genuine
strengthening of the base.

## What it does not do

**It is not a treatment for depression**, notwithstanding the 2025 trial. One clinical RCT is a
promising start, not a clinical recommendation.

**It does not require a mountain.** This is the most common misreading. The research
consistently finds awe in ordinary settings -- a single old tree, a wide view from a familiar
street, the pattern of light on water. Treating awe as something requiring travel is both
inaccurate and self-defeating.

**And it cannot be manufactured to order.** Awe is a response to something genuinely
encountered. Instructing yourself to feel it does not work; instructing yourself to *go
somewhere new and look outward* is what the trial actually did, and that is a behavioural
instruction rather than an emotional one.

**A note on the small self.** Feeling small is beneficial in the context of these studies --
it correlates with generosity, connection and reduced self-preoccupation. It is not the same
as feeling insignificant or diminished, and for people in a depressive state the distinction is
not always easy to hold. If awe practice reliably leaves you feeling worthless rather than
connected, that is a signal to stop and talk to someone.

## How to do it

The protocol, as tested, is almost embarrassingly simple.

- **Fifteen minutes, once a week**, outdoors, on foot.
- **Go somewhere new**, or a familiar route in an unfamiliar direction. Novelty is part of the
  instruction.
- **Attend to vastness and to scale** -- the age of a tree, the distance of a hill, the size of
  the sky, the intricacy of something small enough that its complexity is surprising.
- **Leave the phone alone**, except perhaps for one photograph at the end.
- **Do not try to feel anything.** Look outward; the emotion is a response, not a task.

Cost: nothing. Along with slow breathing, this is one of two practices in this journal that
requires no practitioner, no purchase and no appointment.

For the Bay Area specifically, the ingredients are unusually available -- coastal edges,
redwood stands, fog moving over hills, the scale of the bay itself. The research suggests you
do not need any of them, but they do not hurt.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often arrive at this after noticing that their attention has narrowed -- that weeks pass
in a corridor of work, screens and logistics, and nothing registers as large. Awe practice is
specifically an antidote to that narrowing, and the small-self mechanism is why: it works by
reducing the relative size of your own concerns rather than by solving them.

It suits people who are functioning and depleted rather than acutely unwell. It suits people
who spend most of their attention on problems that are, by any external measure, not enormous
-- which describes most professionals most of the time.

It suits poorly anyone in acute depression, where the diminished-self experience can land
badly, and anyone hoping for something more structured to do.

## Where this fits with the rest of the journal

Awe is the cheapest thing here after slow breathing, and it is worth noticing what it shares
with the more expensive items. Forest bathing, sound baths, silent retreats, pilgrimage --
several of the practices covered in this journal plausibly work in part by producing exactly
this state: attention turned outward, self-concern briefly reduced, scale re-established.

If that is the shared active ingredient, then a fifteen-minute walk with a specific instruction
is the minimum viable version of a great deal of what this site sells. We would rather tell you
that than not.

Which is not an argument against the rest of it. Some people will find awe reliably at a sound
bath and never on a walk, and there is nothing wrong with paying for a reliable route to
something that matters. But start with the free version. If it does nothing for you, that is
useful information about which of the others to try.
