Almost everything on this website costs money and requires a practitioner. This does not.

Breathe at roughly six cycles a minute -- about five seconds in, five seconds out -- for
five minutes. That is the whole intervention. It requires no app, no device, no training
and no belief, and it produces a measurable change in the autonomic nervous system that
has now been documented across dozens of studies and confirmed by meta-analysis.

Which raises a fair question about why it is not more widely prescribed. Part of the
answer is that a free intervention has no marketing budget. Part of it is that what slow
breathing reliably changes is a physiological index, and what it changes in daily life is
harder to pin down.

## What it actually is

Slow-paced breathing means voluntarily reducing your respiratory rate to somewhere near
six breaths per minute -- against a resting adult norm of roughly twelve to twenty.
Researchers call this the resonance frequency, because at approximately 0.1 Hz the
oscillations in heart rate driven by breathing come into phase with the baroreflex, the
blood-pressure regulating loop. The result is a large, visible swing in heart rate across
each breath.

The proposed mechanism is reasonably well specified, which is unusual in this field.
Slowing the breath is thought to stimulate baroreceptors and pulmonary afferent receptors
that project via the vagus nerve to the nucleus of the solitary tract in the brainstem,
which in turn regulates the cardiac vagal neurons governing the heart's parasympathetic
control. The measurable output is heart rate variability, and specifically the
vagally-mediated indices such as RMSSD.

This is what people are gesturing at when they say a practice "activates the
parasympathetic nervous system." In this one case, the phrase is doing real work.

## What the research shows

The definitive synthesis is a systematic review and meta-analysis by Laborde and
colleagues in *Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews*, which pooled the studies of
voluntary slow breathing and concluded that the technique
[increases parasympathetic control of the heart](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0149763422002007),
evidenced by increased RMSSD both during the practice and at multiple points immediately
afterwards.

Two follow-up findings make the practice genuinely usable.

First, you do not need equipment. Slow-paced breathing is usually studied with a
heart-rate-variability biofeedback device, and Laborde's team ran a study in
*Psychophysiology* specifically to test whether the device was doing the work. It was
not: a
[short five-minute session realized without any biofeedback device](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/psyp.13952)
produced mostly similar effects.

Second, longer is not better. A dose-response study had fifty-nine participants breathe
at six cycles per minute for 5, 10, 15 and 20 minutes on separate lab visits, plus a
control condition. RMSSD rose in every breathing condition relative to control -- and
[no differences were found between the durations](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph182312478).
Five minutes did what twenty minutes did.

There is also a mechanistic literature worth knowing exists. Sevoz-Couche and Laborde's
review on
[resonance and coherence](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0149763422000653)
sets out the pathway from vagal afferent stimulation to limbic and interoceptive brain
regions, and a separate study found that
[lengthening the exhalation relative to the inhalation](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.13905)
further enhances high-frequency heart rate variability -- which is the actual, unglamorous
basis for the widespread advice to make your out-breath longer than your in-breath.

**Where this stops short.** The physiological effect is about as well established
as anything in this journal. What remains less settled is the size and durability of the
*clinical* benefit -- how much a repeated shift in vagal activity translates into
measurable improvement in anxiety, sleep or blood pressure over months. Reviews of the
psychological outcomes report positive effects on stress, anxiety and wellbeing, but with
smaller samples and more heterogeneity than the HRV work.

## What it does not do

**It does not "reset your nervous system."** It produces a transient increase in cardiac
vagal activity, most pronounced during practice and for a period afterwards. That is a
real effect and a modest one, and the language of resetting oversells it considerably.

**Higher heart rate variability is not a health outcome in itself.** HRV is associated
with cardiovascular risk across large populations, which is why researchers use it. Moving
your HRV for five minutes is not the same as changing your risk, and no trial has shown
that it is.

**It is not a treatment for panic disorder, hypertension or insomnia**, though it appears
as a component in interventions for all three.

**And it is not for everyone at every moment.** Deliberate breath control makes some
people acutely anxious -- particularly people with panic history, for whom attending to
respiration is itself a trigger. Anyone with significant cardiopulmonary disease should
raise it with a clinician first. If slow breathing makes you feel worse, breathing harder
at it is the wrong response.

## What it is like to do

Sit upright, or lie down. Inhale through the nose for about five seconds, exhale for
about five seconds, and continue for five minutes. Do not force the depth -- the target is
the *rate*, not lung capacity, and over-breathing deeply at a slow rate makes people
lightheaded.

Two adjustments, both supported above. Lengthen the exhale if it is comfortable: four in,
six out is a common and evidence-consistent pattern. And breathe with the diaphragm
rather than the upper chest -- your abdomen should move more than your shoulders.

The first minute usually feels like effort and mild air hunger. Somewhere in the second or
third minute most people notice the effort drop away. What follows is not drama: a
slight heaviness, a slowing, a sense of having more room than you did. Then you stop.

It costs nothing. Free timers and audio pacers are adequate; HRV biofeedback devices run
$100-$300 and, on this evidence, are optional for the acute effect -- though some people
find the feedback usefully motivating.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often use it in the ninety seconds before a difficult conversation, on the drive
home, in the middle of a night they cannot sleep through, or as a fixed five minutes at
the start of the day. Its particular advantage is portability: it works in a chair, on a
plane, in a bathroom before a board meeting.

It suits people who want something to do with their body rather than their thoughts, and
people who have bounced off meditation because sitting with the mind felt like the
problem rather than the solution.

## How to learn it well

You do not need a practitioner for this. That is the point of the article. But if you
want structured teaching, look for HRV biofeedback training with a clinician or
psychophysiologist, or a breathwork teacher who can explain what resonance frequency
means without invoking energy.

The distinction worth drawing: slow-paced breathing is the well-evidenced, low-risk
practice described here. It is not the same as intense circular or holotropic breathwork
sessions, which involve fast breathing to the point of physiological change, carry
documented adverse effects, and rest on a much thinner evidence base. Anyone offering
them as though they were interchangeable is conflating two very different things.
