Almost everything on this website has the same research problem: you cannot hide the
treatment from the patient. You know when you have been needled, cupped, massaged or laid
under a gong.

Binaural beats are the exception, and it makes them unusually interesting. Two tones, one in
each ear, differing by a few hertz. The listener perceives a third pulsing tone that exists
nowhere in the air -- it is constructed in the brainstem from the discrepancy. Swap the
frequencies so that no beat is generated, and the audio sounds essentially the same. Nobody
can tell which they received.

An honest double-blind is available here. So what happened when the field used it?

## What it actually is

Present 200 Hz to the left ear and 210 Hz to the right, and you perceive a 10 Hz pulsation.
The effect requires headphones -- played through speakers the tones mix in the air and the
phenomenon disappears.

The claim built on this is **entrainment**: that the brain's oscillatory activity synchronizes
to the beat frequency, so a 10 Hz beat should push the brain toward alpha activity and
relaxation, a 5 Hz beat toward theta and drowsiness, a 40 Hz beat toward gamma and focus.
Commercial apps sell tracks labelled accordingly -- focus, sleep, anxiety, meditation.

The underlying auditory phenomenon is well established and uncontroversial. The entrainment
claim is the part under test.

## What the research shows

**The headline synthesis is positive and moderate.** Garcia-Argibay, Santed and Reales
published a meta-analysis in *Psychological Research* covering 22 studies and 35 effect
sizes, and reported an
[overall medium, significant and consistent effect (g = 0.45)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00426-018-1066-8)
across memory, attention, anxiety and pain perception. Their meta-regression produced two
practical findings: masking the beats with white or pink noise is unnecessary, and exposure
*before* a task -- or before and during -- outperforms exposure during the task alone.

**The anxiety result is the most-quoted and the thinnest.** That same meta-analysis found a
medium-to-large effect on anxiety in the theta/delta range, Hedges' *g* = 0.69 -- based on
five effect sizes drawn from four studies, with a total of 159 participants. A number that
size resting on four small studies is a signal worth following up, not a conclusion.

**Some of the best-powered individual studies are negative.** Work testing whether
beta-frequency binaural beats improve sustained attention produced, in the authors' framing,
rather strong evidence *against* the hypothesis -- no general speeding of responses and no
mitigation of the vigilance decline. Other work found no improvement in attention with gamma
beats and no effect on self-rated anxiety. These are exactly the well-controlled studies the
commercial claims most depend on, and they did not deliver.

**One properly blinded clinical trial stands out.** Gkolias and colleagues ran a double-blind
randomized controlled cross-over trial of acoustic binaural beats therapy in chronic pain and
reported reduced pain and reduced analgesic use, published in the *European Journal of Pain*.
Cross-over, double-blind, an objective-adjacent outcome in analgesic consumption -- this is a
better design than almost anything else cited on this site.

**And there is a physiological trace.** Solcà and colleagues found in *Hearing Research* that
binaural beats increase interhemispheric alpha-band coherence between the auditory cortices.
Something measurable is happening in the brain. Note what it is, though: coherence between
*auditory* cortices, which is a good deal more modest than global entrainment of brain state.

**Where the evidence stands.** A real perceptual phenomenon with a measurable cortical signature, a moderate
pooled effect across a heterogeneous literature, at least one well-designed clinical trial --
and pointed negative findings from some of the better-powered studies. Reviews of dental
anxiety applications report heterogeneity as high as I² = 94%, which is the field in a
number.

## What it does not do

**It does not reliably "entrain your brainwaves" to a chosen state.** The strong version of
entrainment -- that a 40 Hz track puts your brain into a gamma state on demand -- is not what
the evidence shows. Localized auditory-cortex coherence is not a global brain state.

**It is not a substitute for sleep medication, anxiolytics or treatment.** No regulator has
approved any binaural beat product for any condition, and the trials are small.

**Frequency-specific marketing outruns the data considerably.** Apps offer precisely labelled
tracks for creativity, manifestation, immune function and much else. The meta-analytic
evidence distinguishes broad frequency bands at best, and inconsistently.

**And it is not the same thing as a sound bath**, despite frequent conflation. Binaural beats
require headphones and depend on a perceptual illusion. A room full of gongs is an entirely
different intervention with an entirely different evidence base.

**Safety** is straightforward. The main caution is volume -- these are typically listened to
on headphones for extended periods, and hearing damage is cumulative and irreversible. Keep
it low. As with any rhythmic auditory stimulation, anyone with a seizure disorder should ask
a doctor first. And do not use tracks marketed as replicating drug effects; those are
marketing, not pharmacology.

## What using it is like

You need stereo headphones -- over-ear or in-ear, not a speaker, not one earbud. Tracks
typically run 15 to 60 minutes and sound like a low continuous hum, often layered under rain,
ambient music or nothing at all.

The perceptual effect is subtle. Most people notice a soft pulsing quality in the tone,
easier to hear in silence than under music. Some people find it pleasant and some find it
faintly irritating, which is worth knowing before committing to a subscription.

Based on the meta-regression: use it *before* the task you want to affect, or before and
during, rather than only during. Masking it with noise is not necessary. And the free
versions are, as far as the evidence goes, indistinguishable from the paid ones -- there is no
proprietary frequency that has been shown to outperform another.

Cost: free to about $100 a year for app subscriptions. Given the state of the evidence, we
would suggest starting with a free track and seeing whether you like the experience at all.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often use binaural beats for focus during work, for winding down at night, and to make
meditation easier by giving the ears something to hold. Anecdotally, the group who report the
most benefit are people who find silence uncomfortable and ordinary music too distracting --
the tracks occupy a useful middle space.

It suits people who want a zero-risk, near-zero-cost experiment. It suits poorly anyone
buying it as a treatment, and anyone who will be disappointed to learn the effect is modest
and contested.

Our practical suggestion: try it for two weeks. It is one of the few things on this site
where you can run your own uncontrolled experiment for nothing, and where the honest expected
effect is "possibly a bit."

## How to choose what to use

There is no practitioner and no credential, so this reduces to product judgment.

Prefer plain tracks over elaborate frequency claims -- a track offering a specific band for
relaxation is consistent with what has been tested; one promising DNA repair or manifestation
is not. Prefer apps that state their frequencies openly. Ignore anything invoking 432 Hz or
528 Hz "healing frequencies", which are a separate and unsupported set of claims that have
become tangled up with binaural beats in marketing.

And keep the volume low enough that you could hold a conversation over it. On a practice you
might use daily for an hour, that is the only recommendation here with genuinely high-quality
evidence behind it.
