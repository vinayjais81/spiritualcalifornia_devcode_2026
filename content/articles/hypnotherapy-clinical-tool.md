Almost nothing on this website has managed what gut-directed hypnotherapy has managed. It is
named in European and North American gastroenterology guidelines as a second-line treatment
option for irritable bowel syndrome -- not as complementary care, not as an adjunct to be
tolerated, but as a recommended therapy in mainstream clinical practice.

This is the same modality most people associate with a man in a waistcoat persuading
volunteers to cluck like chickens. The gap between those two things has cost hypnotherapy
thirty years of credibility, and it is worth closing.

## What it actually is

Clinical hypnosis is a procedure in which a trained practitioner guides you into a state of
focused attention with reduced peripheral awareness, and then offers structured suggestions
directed at a specific clinical target.

Several things it is not. You are not asleep or unconscious. You are not under anyone's
control, and you cannot be made to do something contrary to your values. You will remember it.
And you are not being deceived -- the state is voluntarily entered and you can leave it at any
moment.

**Gut-directed hypnotherapy** is the most-studied protocol: typically seven to twelve weekly
sessions in which suggestions are aimed specifically at normalizing gut function, reducing the
salience of gut sensations, and altering how the brain interprets signals from the digestive
tract. The Manchester protocol is the reference version and appears repeatedly in trials.

Susceptibility varies. Some people enter hypnotic states easily and some barely at all, and
this is a stable individual trait -- which turns out to matter less for outcomes than you
would expect.

## What the research shows

**IBS is where the evidence lives.** A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis in
*Neurogastroenterology & Motility* found that
[all twelve included studies reported gut-directed hypnotherapy superior to the comparator, with nine reaching statistical significance](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nmo.70037).
On meta-analysis the pooled effect on global IBS symptoms was SMD 0.73 -- with a 95%
confidence interval of −0.09 to 1.55 and heterogeneity at I² = 93%. That interval crosses
zero, and a fact-first reading has to say so: the direction is highly consistent, the pooled
estimate is not statistically robust, and the studies differ enormously from one another.

An earlier meta-analysis in the *Journal of Neurogastroenterology and Motility* found
hypnotherapy
[significantly improved abdominal pain at short-term follow-up (SMD −0.83, 95% CI −1.65 to −0.01)](https://www.jnmjournal.org/journal/view.html?doi=10.5056%2Fjnm.20.2.152),
with three of four trials showing greater improvement in overall gastrointestinal symptoms --
and stated plainly that evidence for long-term efficacy is lacking due to too few studies.

**The guideline status is the strongest signal.** A 2024 mini-review notes that of the
brain-gut behavioural therapies, cognitive-behavioural interventions and gut-directed hypnosis
have the largest evidence base for short- and long-term efficacy, and are
[recommended by current European and North American gastroenterology guidelines as second-line treatment](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1389911/full).
The same review reports a randomized trial of 378 participants in which 70% achieved a
clinically relevant reduction in IBS symptoms versus 30% of controls.

**And here is the finding that keeps this from being stronger.** A US study compared
digital gut-directed hypnosis with digital progressive muscle relaxation in 362 patients. A
similar proportion met the primary endpoint in each arm -- 30.4% versus 27.1%. Against an
active relaxation comparator, the specific hypnotic component did not clearly add anything.

**Beyond IBS**, an overview of meta-analyses using AMSTAR 2 methodology assessed hypnosis
across mental and somatic health problems, providing the broadest available picture of both
efficacy and safety. Hypnosis also has a substantial evidence base in procedural pain and
anxiety, and Cochrane has reviewed hypnotherapy for IBS separately.

**Where the evidence stands.** Consistent direction, guideline recognition, and a genuinely impressive record in
one condition -- undercut by high heterogeneity, a pooled estimate that does not reach
significance, and at least one good comparison where relaxation performed just as well.

## What it does not do

**It does not give anyone control over you.** The stage-show model is a performance built on
selecting highly suggestible volunteers who are willing participants. It has nothing to do
with clinical practice.

**It does not recover reliable memories.** This is the most important warning in the article.
Hypnosis increases confidence in recalled material without increasing accuracy, and
hypnotically "recovered" memories are notoriously unreliable. Any practitioner offering to
uncover forgotten trauma or past lives is doing something that can genuinely harm people, and
courts in many jurisdictions restrict hypnotically-refreshed testimony for exactly this
reason.

**It has not been shown to be better than good relaxation training** in at least one
well-powered head-to-head. If the mechanism is focused attention plus therapeutic suggestion,
some of that may be available more cheaply.

**Long-term durability is not established** in the IBS literature -- the reviews say so
directly.

**And smoking cessation and weight loss, the two things it is most commercially marketed for,
have a far weaker evidence base than IBS.** The gap between what hypnotherapy is advertised
for and what it is evidenced for is wide.

**Safety.** Clinical hypnosis is low-risk in trained hands. It is not appropriate without
specialist assessment for people with psychosis, and it should be approached carefully by
anyone with dissociative symptoms.

## What a session is like

The first session is assessment and explanation, and a competent practitioner spends real time
dismantling the misconceptions above before doing anything else.

Induction is undramatic. You sit or recline, eyes usually closed, and the practitioner talks --
slowly, repetitively, guiding attention to the breath, to the body, to an image. There is no
pocket watch. Most people describe it as similar to the state just before sleep, or to being
absorbed in a film: aware, unbothered, with time behaving strangely.

Then come the suggestions, which in gut-directed work are specific and often surprisingly
concrete -- imagery of a calm, smoothly functioning digestive system, warmth placed on the
abdomen, the volume of gut sensation being turned down.

Sessions run 45 to 60 minutes over seven to twelve weeks, and home practice with recordings is
standard. That home practice is part of the protocol, not an optional extra.

Expect $120-$250 per session in California. Some medical hypnotherapy delivered by licensed
clinicians is billable; standalone hypnotherapy generally is not.

## Who tends to find it useful

People most often seek it for IBS and functional gut disorders -- where the evidence is
strongest and where conventional options frequently disappoint -- and for procedural anxiety,
chronic pain, and childbirth. It is also widely used for phobias and habit change, with
weaker support.

It suits people who are comfortable with structured suggestion and can commit to a
multi-week course plus daily home practice. It suits poorly anyone hoping for a single
dramatic session, and anyone wanting to explore memory.

If you have IBS and have not discussed brain-gut behavioural therapy with your
gastroenterologist, that conversation is the single most actionable thing in this article.

## How to choose a practitioner

This is the modality where credential-checking matters most, because "hypnotherapist" is not a
protected title in California and weekend certifications are common.

**Strongly prefer a licensed healthcare professional who also practises hypnosis** -- a
psychologist, LMFT, LCSW, physician or nurse with additional hypnosis training. That underlying
licence means someone accountable, insured, and trained to recognize what is outside their
scope.

**Look for ASCH or SCEH certification** -- the American Society of Clinical Hypnosis and the
Society for Clinical and Experimental Hypnosis both restrict membership to licensed
professionals and are the meaningful credentials in this field.

**For IBS specifically, ask whether they use a standardized gut-directed protocol** such as the
Manchester or North Carolina protocols, and how many sessions. If the answer is vague, find
someone else.

**Ask what they will not treat.** A good answer is immediate and specific.

**Three hard stops.** Anyone offering past-life regression. Anyone offering to recover
repressed memories. And anyone claiming to treat a serious medical condition with hypnosis
alone.
