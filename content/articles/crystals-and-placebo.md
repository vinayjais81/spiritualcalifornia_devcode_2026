We are going to do something unusual with this article, and it is worth saying so at the
start. We are going to tell you that the central claim about crystals is not supported by
any evidence -- and then spend most of the remaining words on why the thing that *does*
happen is more interesting than the thing that does not.

If you came for validation, this is not that. If you came for a debunking that treats you
as foolish, it is not that either.

## What it actually is

Crystal healing involves placing stones -- quartz, amethyst, rose quartz, obsidian -- on or
around a clothed person, or carrying them, or holding them during meditation. Different
stones are held to carry different properties, and the practice is frequently combined with
chakra work, with a stone assigned to each position on the body.

The stated mechanism is that crystals emit, focus or balance a form of energy that interacts
with the body's own. The lattice structure of quartz and its piezoelectric properties are
sometimes invoked as a physical basis.

Piezoelectricity is real -- quartz generates a small voltage under mechanical stress, which
is why it appears in watches and sensors. A crystal sitting on a sternum is not under
mechanical stress, and nothing has been shown to be emitted from it.

## What the research shows

There is very little research, and what exists points one way.

The best-known study was conducted by Christopher French and colleagues at the Anomalistic
Psychology Research Unit at Goldsmiths, University of London. Eighty volunteers were asked to
meditate holding either a genuine quartz crystal or a
[placebo stone indistinguishable from quartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_healing).
Many participants reported the sensations they had been primed to expect -- tingling, warmth,
heightened energy, improved concentration, greater wellbeing -- and reported them irrespective
of whether their stone was real. The conclusion presented to the British Psychological Society
Centenary Annual Conference was that there is no evidence crystal healing works over and
above a placebo effect.

Two details matter more than the headline. Participants who believed in crystal power before
the study were roughly twice as likely to report sensations as those who identified as
sceptics. And this was a replication -- the earlier version had not been double-blind, so the
team ran it again with the experimenter also blinded, and got the same result.

**One caveat we should state plainly:** French's crystal study was presented at conferences
in 1999 and 2001 and does not appear to have been published in a peer-reviewed journal. That
is a real limitation, and the sort of thing this journal will not paper over even when the
finding is convenient.

More recently, a peer-reviewed study examined
[placebo effects in alternative treatments for anxiety](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40855750/)
and reported that healing crystals showed no anxiolytic effects beyond placebo, with Bayesian
estimates favouring the null hypothesis for any specific treatment effect. Pre-existing belief
strongly predicted both perceived efficacy and symptom change. Non-believers showed no
reliable improvement at all.

**Where the evidence stands.** No demonstrated mechanism, and the two controlled tests that exist found the real
stone and the fake stone performed identically.

## So what is actually happening?

This is the part worth your time.

The placebo response is not nothing, and it is not imagination. It is one of the most
reliably measurable phenomena in medicine -- expectation and ritual produce real changes in
reported symptoms, and in some cases in physiology. It is strongest precisely for
subjectively-rated outcomes: pain, anxiety, mood, energy. Which is exactly the list of things
crystal users report improving.

Notice what the second study found: symptom change was mediated by expectancy and
conditioning, and non-believers improved not at all. That is a coherent picture of a
mechanism. It is simply not the mechanism being advertised.

There is also something in crystal practice that has nothing to do with minerals. Choosing a
stone for a specific intention, carrying it, and touching it during the day is a **portable
attentional cue**. Each time your fingers find it in a pocket, you are reminded of a thing
you decided mattered. Structured self-reminding of an intention is not a fringe idea; it is
close to how habit cues and implementation intentions are understood to work.

So the honest formulation is this. A crystal does not do anything. A crystal *practice* --
choosing, intending, carrying, remembering -- can plausibly do something, through mechanisms
that are well understood and that do not require the stone to be special. The stone is a
handle for attention.

Whether that is disappointing or rather remarkable is up to you. Our view is that
transferring the credit from the mineral to the person holding it makes the practice more
interesting, not less.

## What it does not do

**It does not treat any medical condition.** This is the one point where crystal practice
stops being harmless. Crystals are non-toxic and physically inert, so the risk is never the
stone -- it is substitution. Any use that delays diagnosis or replaces treatment for a serious
illness converts a benign practice into a dangerous one. Documented cases of families
pursuing crystal therapy for terminal illness in place of care are the reason this paragraph
exists.

**Its properties are not traditional in the way they are usually presented.** Modern
attributions of specific meanings to specific stones largely date from twentieth-century New
Age literature, not from an unbroken ancient lineage. Stones have carried protective and
symbolic significance across many cultures for millennia; that is a different and more
accurate claim.

**And no stone has been shown to differ from any other**, including from a piece of moulded
plastic.

**Sourcing deserves a mention.** The crystal trade has documented problems with mining
conditions, environmental impact and opaque supply chains. If a practice is about intention,
provenance is arguably part of it.

## What a session is like

You lie clothed on a table. Stones are placed on and around the body -- commonly along the
midline at the chakra positions -- and you rest for thirty to sixty minutes, often with music.
The practitioner may move stones, hold them above you, or place them at the head and feet.

What people report is the weight of the stones, warmth where they sit, drowsiness, and the
particular quality of an hour spent lying still while somebody attends to you. Those reports
are accurate descriptions of experience and we have no reason to doubt them.

Sessions run $80-$150 in California. Stones themselves range from a few dollars to a great
deal more, and price correlates with rarity and aesthetics rather than with anything else.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often come to crystals as an entry point -- the least demanding, least intimidating
thing in this whole category, requiring no class, no undressing, no belief system at the
door. Many people use them as objects of focus during meditation or as a physical anchor for
an intention.

Our honest suggestion: treat a crystal as a beautiful object that reminds you of something
you decided. That framing is defensible, costs little, and puts the agency where the evidence
says it is. Spending significantly on crystals for their properties is spending on a claim
that two controlled tests have failed to support.

## How to think about a practitioner

Since there is no mechanism to be trained in, the question is what they claim.

A practitioner who describes crystal work as a ritual for focus, intention and rest is being
straightforward, and that session may genuinely be restful.

A practitioner who claims to diagnose you, to detect energy imbalances, or to treat a
physical or psychiatric condition has moved from a harmless practice to a harmful one. If
anyone suggests crystals in place of medical treatment for a serious illness -- particularly
cancer -- leave, and say so to whoever referred you.

Finally, if a session helps you, that is real. What the evidence says is that *you* helped
you, with the assistance of a quiet room, an hour of attention, and an object you had decided
to take seriously. That is not a smaller thing.
