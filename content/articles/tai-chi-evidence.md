In 2010, the *New England Journal of Medicine* -- the most conservative general medical
journal in the world, the place where cardiac drugs and cancer protocols are adjudicated
-- published a randomized trial of classical Yang-style tai chi.

That sentence is worth pausing on. Very little in complementary medicine reaches that
venue. Tai chi did, and it did so because the evidence for it had become difficult to
dismiss.

Meanwhile, in Californian parks at seven in the morning, people are doing it slowly in
loose clothing, and it is filed in the public imagination somewhere between gentle
stretching and a hobby. It is the most robustly evidenced practice on this entire site,
and almost nobody treats it as clinical.

## What it actually is

Tai chi -- *taijiquan* -- is a Chinese internal martial art, several centuries old, in
which a fixed sequence of postures is performed continuously at low speed, with attention
on weight transfer, alignment and breath. The Yang style is the most widely taught; the
24-form simplified sequence is the usual entry point and takes five to six minutes to
perform once learned.

Structurally, it is three things at once: slow weight-bearing exercise, a continuous
balance challenge, and a sustained attentional task. Researchers have argued -- correctly
-- that this makes it a *complex multicomponent intervention*, which creates a genuine
methodological problem: when it works, it is hard to say which component worked.

That problem has not stopped it producing some of the cleanest results in the field.

## What the research shows

**Falls, first.** A systematic review and meta-analysis of ten randomized controlled
trials covering 2,850 participants found that tai chi
[significantly reduced the risk of falls, with an odds ratio of 0.70 (95% CI 0.59-0.84)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1873959816300746)
compared with controls. That is a roughly 30% reduction in the odds of falling, from a
pooled sample large enough to take seriously.

It has been replicated repeatedly and independently. Separate meta-analyses in the
*Journal of the American Geriatrics Society* and
[*BMJ Open*](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2016-013661)
reached consistent conclusions, and further
[systematic review work on falls and balance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10509476/)
has confirmed the effect on postural control and coordination. Falls are the leading cause
of injury death in older Americans, and interventions that move that number are not
common.

**Fibromyalgia, second.** The NEJM trial compared classic Yang-style tai chi against a
control intervention in patients with fibromyalgia in a single-blind randomized design,
and concluded that
[tai chi is potentially a useful therapy](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa0912611),
with the effect evident on the Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire -- a well-validated
multidimensional instrument rather than a single subjective rating.

**Chronic pain more broadly.** A meta-analysis of randomized trials in *Scientific
Reports* examined tai chi across chronic pain conditions, and a separate 2025 review of
qigong and tai chi for chronic low back pain -- eight RCTs, 729 adults -- found
[significant reductions in pain intensity (SMD -1.07) and disability (SMD -0.77)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12173434/),
with GRADE certainty rated moderate to high. Heterogeneity in that analysis was very high
(I² = 93%), which is a real limitation and the authors say so.

**And the caveat that keeps it honest.** A systematic review of tai chi and qigong for
stroke prevention pooled 21 trials and 1,604 patients with hypertension, hyperlipidaemia,
diabetes or metabolic syndrome -- and noted that
[no trials examined the effect on stroke incidence at all](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5690748/).
Risk factors improved. Whether events follow has not been tested.

**Where the evidence stands.** Strong -- but specifically for falls and balance in older adults,
with good supporting evidence in fibromyalgia and chronic pain. Not for everything tai chi
gets recommended for.

## What it does not do

**It does not do everything.** Tai chi appears in reviews for heart failure, cancer-related
fatigue, depression, COPD, cognitive aging and much else. Some of that work is
encouraging; much of it is small, unblinded, or conducted in a single centre. The falls
evidence is in a different class from the rest, and treating the whole literature as
uniformly strong is exactly the error this journal exists to avoid.

**It has not been shown to prevent strokes or cardiac events**, per the review above.

**Its mechanism is not established as anything to do with qi.** The traditional
explanatory framework describes cultivating and circulating internal energy. What can be
measured is improved postural control, lower-limb strength, reduced fear of falling, and
sustained attention. The practice works; the traditional explanation of *why* is a
tradition, not a finding.

**And the multicomponent problem cuts against the mystique.** If most of the falls benefit
comes from spending an hour a week shifting weight slowly onto one leg, then tai chi is a
very well-designed balance-training programme with two thousand years of refinement --
which is remarkable, and less romantic than the marketing.

## What a class is like

Everyone stands in loose rows. The teacher demonstrates and you follow, badly, for
several weeks. There is no stretching warm-up in the Western sense, no mirror, no count.

The physical experience surprises people. Held slowly, with knees bent and weight on one
leg, the postures are demanding -- thighs burn, and beginners are frequently sweating
within twenty minutes. It is not gentle in the way it looks.

Progress is slow in a way that is either the appeal or the deal-breaker. Learning the
24-form sequence takes most people three to six months of weekly classes. What people
report noticing first is not calm but *placement* -- an awareness of where their weight is
when they walk downstairs.

Classes run $15-$30 in California, and community centres, senior centres and parks
departments frequently offer them free or near-free -- which is worth knowing, because it
makes the best-evidenced modality on this site also among the cheapest. Wear flat shoes
and clothes you can bend in.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often come to tai chi for balance and for confidence on their feet, and the
evidence supports exactly that. It is also a reasonable choice for chronic pain,
particularly fibromyalgia, and for people who need weight-bearing exercise but cannot
tolerate impact.

It suits people who are willing to be a beginner in public for several months. It suits
people badly if they want either a workout with visible intensity or a meditative practice
with no learning curve -- tai chi is neither, and the middle ground is precisely the point.

If you are over sixty and have fallen once, or have started avoiding stairs, this article
is the one on the site to act on.

## How to choose a teacher

Tai chi is unregulated, and the field contains both serious martial lineages and
loosely-trained wellness instructors. What to look for:

- **A named style and lineage.** Yang, Chen, Wu, Sun -- and who they learned from. A
  teacher who cannot answer this is teaching something they picked up.
- **Experience with your goal.** Teaching balance to older adults is a distinct skill from
  teaching martial applications. Ask directly.
- **Class size and correction.** The falls benefit depends on doing the postures with
  accurate weight transfer. In a class of forty with no hands-on correction, you will
  learn the shape and miss the substance.
- **A trial class.** Any teacher confident in their work will let you attend one.

Two things to be wary of. A teacher who explains all outcomes through qi and dismisses the
physical mechanics is unlikely to correct your knee alignment, which is what actually
protects you. And anyone presenting tai chi as a treatment for a diagnosed disease has
overstated a literature that is already strong enough not to need it.
