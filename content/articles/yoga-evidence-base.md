Yoga has more randomized controlled trials behind it than anything else on this website, and it
is the only practice here that appears in an American College of Physicians clinical guideline.

It also has the most interesting problem in the journal, which is this: yoga is clearly better
than doing nothing, and probably no better than other exercise. Where you land on that
determines whether you read the evidence as impressive or deflating, and the honest answer is
that both readings are correct about different things.

## What it actually is

The yoga studied in Western clinical trials is almost entirely *asana*-based -- physical
postures, held or flowed through, usually combined with breath regulation (pranayama) and
often a period of relaxation or meditation. Iyengar, Viniyoga and Hatha styles appear most
frequently in the research, generally in structured 12-week programmes with weekly classes and
home practice.

That is a narrow slice of yoga as a tradition, which is a philosophical and ethical system with
physical practice as one of eight limbs. The research studies the exercise. It does not study
the philosophy, and it is worth being clear which one is being recommended.

## What the research shows

**Against no exercise, the evidence is strong.** Cramer and colleagues meta-analyzed ten
randomized trials covering 967 chronic low back pain patients, eight of which had low risk of
bias, and found
[strong evidence for short-term effects on pain (SMD −0.48), back-specific disability (SMD −0.59) and global improvement (RR 3.27)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23246998/?dopt=Abstract).
Long-term effects held at SMD −0.33 for pain and −0.35 for disability. No effect was found on
health-related quality of life in that analysis.

Cochrane's 2022 review reached compatible conclusions against non-exercise controls, finding
[low-certainty evidence of clinical improvement (RR 2.33) and moderate-certainty evidence of small improvements in physical and mental quality of life](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD010671.pub3/full).

**Against other exercise, the difference largely disappears.** In the same Cochrane review, across
eight trials and 912 participants, there was moderate-certainty evidence of
[little or no difference in back-specific function at three months compared with back-focused exercise](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD010671_yoga-chronic-non-specific-low-back-pain),
and very low-certainty evidence of no difference in pain, clinical improvement or quality of
life.

Cochrane's conclusion is admirably plain: decisions to use yoga instead of no exercise or
another exercise may depend on availability, cost, and participant or provider preference.

**Depression did not improve.** Low-certainty evidence from three trials showed little to no
improvement on the Beck Depression Inventory (MD −1.25, 95% CI −2.90 to 0.46). Yoga is
frequently recommended for mood; in the back pain trials at least, it did not move depression
scores.

**And here is the finding almost no yoga studio mentions.** Low-certainty evidence from eight
trials found that yoga **increased the risk of adverse events** compared with no exercise --
primarily increased back pain -- at six to twelve months, with a risk ratio of 4.76 (95% CI
2.08 to 10.89): roughly 43 per 1,000 with yoga versus 9 per 1,000 with no exercise.

That is not a contradiction of Cramer's finding that yoga was not associated with *serious*
adverse events. Both are true: serious harm is rare, and minor harm -- mostly back pain getting
temporarily worse -- is several times more common than doing nothing. Cochrane also notes yoga
may carry the same adverse event risk as other exercise, which puts it in ordinary company.

**Where the evidence stands.** Strong for chronic non-specific low back pain against
non-exercise comparators, with guideline recognition. But not superior to other exercise, which has never been shown.

## What it does not do

**It is not demonstrably better than other exercise for back pain.** If you already swim, lift
or walk regularly and enjoy it, this literature does not give you a reason to switch.

**It did not improve depression in the back pain trials.** Yoga's mood evidence exists
elsewhere and is weaker than its musculoskeletal evidence; treat claims about it accordingly.

**It is not risk-free.** The 4.76 risk ratio deserves to be known, particularly by people
starting yoga *because* their back hurts. Going too hard, too early, into deep forward folds
and twists with an irritable lumbar spine is a common and predictable way to make things worse.

**And the blinding problem is unavoidable.** Cochrane observes that since all studies were
unblinded and at high risk of performance and detection bias, blinded comparisons would be
unlikely to find a clinically important benefit. You cannot conceal from someone that they are
doing yoga.

## What a class is like

Enormously variable, which is the practical problem with a literature that says "yoga."

A therapeutic or Iyengar-style class -- closest to what the trials tested -- moves slowly, uses
props extensively (blocks, straps, bolsters, a wall), holds postures, and involves frequent
individual correction. A vinyasa or power class is a cardiovascular workout at pace with little
correction. A restorative class is largely lying supported on props. These are different
activities sharing a name.

The trial protocol was typically 12 weeks, one or two classes weekly, plus home practice. That
is the dose associated with the results above.

Expect $20-$35 per class in the Bay Area, $120-$200 monthly unlimited, and $100-$180 for
private sessions. Community and donation-based classes are widespread here, and many studios
run free introductory weeks.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often come to yoga for back pain, for flexibility, for stress, and for a form of exercise
that does not feel like the gym. The back pain evidence supports the first directly.

It suits people who will attend consistently for at least twelve weeks, and who will take a
slower class rather than the hardest one available. It suits particularly well anyone who has
been advised to exercise but finds conventional exercise unappealing -- given that yoga performs
comparably to other exercise, the one you will actually do is the right one.

It suits poorly anyone in acute back pain without medical assessment, and anyone whose
competitive instincts will push them past what their spine currently tolerates.

## How to choose a class and a teacher

**For back pain specifically, seek out therapeutic, Iyengar or gentle Hatha classes.** These are
closest to what was tested. Avoid hot yoga and power vinyasa as a starting point.

**Ask about the teacher's training.** Yoga Alliance registration (RYT-200, RYT-500) is the
common baseline and is a training-hours record rather than a competency examination. For a
back problem, look for additional therapeutic training -- C-IAYT (Certified Yoga Therapist,
through the International Association of Yoga Therapists) is the substantive credential, and it
is considerably more demanding.

**Tell the teacher before class, not during.** A good one will offer modifications and check in.

**Watch for the two red flags.** A teacher who pushes students into deeper positions with hands
rather than offering options. And any class culture in which the modified version is treated as
the lesser one -- that is precisely the culture in which the 43-per-1,000 adverse events happen.

**And be sceptical of therapeutic claims beyond the evidence.** Yoga for chronic low back pain is
well supported. Yoga for detoxification, for organ massage, for immune function or for curing
disease is not.

## A closing note on what this evidence means

The most useful thing this literature says is not about yoga specifically. It is that structured,
regular, moderate movement helps chronic back pain, and that yoga is one good delivery
mechanism for it -- available, sociable, gentle on joints, and easier to sustain than most
alternatives for a lot of people.

That is a smaller claim than the one usually made. It is also, given how many people cannot
sustain any exercise programme at all, a genuinely important one.
