There is a particular kind of person who reads about the benefits of meditation, buys
the app, sits down with real intent, and quits inside a fortnight. They are not lazy.
They are usually the opposite -- capable, busy people who have concluded, on evidence,
that they are simply not built for stillness.

The standard advice is to try harder and start smaller. The research suggests something
more interesting, and rather more forgiving: the relationship between how much you
meditate and how much good it does you is much weaker than almost anyone assumes.

## What it actually is

Formal mindfulness practice, as tested in the clinical literature, means sitting still
for a set period and returning attention to a chosen anchor -- usually the breath -- each
time it wanders. The canonical dose comes from Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction, which
asks for forty to sixty minutes of daily home practice across eight weeks, plus weekly
group sessions and a full-day retreat.

That is a serious commitment, and the field has known for years that it costs them
participants. Reviews of MBSR-style programs report average attrition around sixteen
percent, and researchers have argued openly that the time demand stops many people
from starting at all.

So the field began asking a practical question. What happens if you shrink the dose?

## What the research shows

The most useful single paper here is a dose-response meta-regression by Simon
Strohmaier, published in *Mindfulness* in 2020, which pooled randomized controlled
trials of mindfulness-based programs and asked whether more delivered more. Its finding
is the reason this article exists: programme length, the amount of contact with the
teacher, and the amount of home practice were
[not significantly associated with psychological outcomes](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12671-020-01319-4).

That result has held up under direct experimental test. A 2023 study in *Scientific
Reports* randomized participants to
[ten versus twenty minutes of daily practice](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-46578-y)
and looked for a difference in state mindfulness and affect. Researchers reviewing the
broader picture note that of the handful of trials that have deliberately manipulated
session length, most failed to detect a dose-response effect at all -- a summary set out
in the protocol for a
[large ongoing trial designed to settle the question properly](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12344385/),
which is randomizing hundreds of adults across 10, 20 and 30-minute daily doses against
a three-to-four minute minimal control.

Meanwhile, low-dose programs have been built and tested on exactly this logic. A
[Mindfulness-Based Integrative Programme trial](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0165032724020652)
was designed explicitly as a lower-dose alternative, citing the dose-response findings
as its rationale, and reported effects broadly comparable to standard-length
interventions in healthy adults.

None of which means the underlying practice is weak. A large meta-analysis by Goldberg
and colleagues found mindfulness-based interventions produced moderate-to-large
between-group effects against no-treatment controls -- around *d* = 0.59 for depression
and *d* = 0.89 for anxiety. The effect is real. It just does not appear to scale with
minutes the way a training effect would.

**Why this isn't stronger than it looks.** These are analyses of heterogeneous trials with
inconsistent measures, and absence of a detected dose-response is not proof that none
exists. It may be that the studies are too short, the dose range too narrow, or the
measures too blunt to see it. The large trial now underway exists precisely because the
question is unresolved.

## What it does not do

**A shorter practice does not make you a meditator in the traditional sense.** The
contemplative traditions are describing something that emerges over years of long
sitting, and they are not describing a mood improvement. If that is what you are after,
the dose almost certainly matters, and no trial has measured it.

**It does not mean any practice is as good as any other.** The same review that found
duration irrelevant also found that programs lacking a didactic component -- teaching,
explanation, discussion -- produced smaller effects. What you are doing appears to
matter more than how long you do it. An unguided ten minutes is not the same
intervention as a taught ten minutes.

**And it does not license the version most people default to**, which is not ten
consistent minutes but three sporadic ones followed by a fortnight of nothing.
Frequency and continuity are not what the dose research put to the test.

## What a practice like this looks like

The version that survives contact with a real schedule tends to have four features.

It is **attached to something existing** -- after the shower, before the first email, in
the parked car before walking into the building. Not scheduled in isolation, where it
competes with everything.

It is **short enough to be non-negotiable.** Ten minutes is well supported. Even shorter
practices show effects on immediate state measures, though the durable-outcome evidence
thins as you go down.

It is **guided, at least at first.** This is the didactic-component finding in practical
form.

And it is **often not sitting at all.** Mindful movement is a component of MBSR itself,
not a concession to the restless -- a slow walk with attention on the soles of the feet
is formal practice, and for some people it is the only version that ever becomes a
habit.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often come to shorter-form practice after failing at the long form, and arrive
with the assumption that they are getting a diluted product. The dose literature is
reasonably reassuring on that point.

It suits people whose obstacle is starting rather than believing -- and people whose
schedules genuinely cannot absorb forty-five minutes without something else breaking.
It suits less well anyone using "the research says duration does not matter" as
permission to practise almost never.

## How to choose a teacher or a program

Ask what the daily commitment is and *why that number*. A teacher who has read the dose
literature will give you a considered answer. One who insists that forty-five minutes
is the only real practice is stating a tradition, not a finding, and should say which
they mean.

For app-based practice, prefer a structured multi-week course over a library of
one-off sessions. The course format supplies the didactic element the research
associates with larger effects; the library format mostly supplies choice, which is
what you already have too much of.

And if you have a history of trauma or dissociation, say so before you start,
regardless of dose. That conversation is not about how long you sit.
