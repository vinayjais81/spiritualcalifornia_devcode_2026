Coaching is the least regulated profession on this website. Anyone can print a business card
tomorrow. There is no licence, no protected title, and no legal barrier to charging $500 an
hour to advise a stranger on their life.

That is a real problem, and it makes the research question sharper than usual: does coaching
work, and what exactly is the "it" that works?

## What it actually is

Coaching is a structured, goal-directed conversation aimed at helping a client clarify what
they want and move toward it. It is future-oriented and non-diagnostic: a coach works with
functioning adults on goals, decisions, performance and transitions, not on psychological
disorders.

Two distinctions matter.

**Executive or workplace coaching** is delivered in an organizational context, usually paid
for by an employer, focused on performance, leadership and work-related development.

**Life coaching** is bought by the individual and covers whatever they bring -- career changes,
relationships, purpose, habits, the general sense that something is not right.

The line between coaching and therapy is the one that matters most in practice. Therapy treats
disorder and works with the past; coaching works with goals and the future. A competent coach
knows the boundary and refers across it.

## What the research shows

**The headline finding is genuinely good.** Theeboom, Beersma and van Vianen's meta-analysis in
*The Journal of Positive Psychology* found significant positive effects across every outcome
category examined, with
[effect sizes from g = 0.43 for coping to g = 0.74 for goal-directed self-regulation](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17439760.2013.837499),
and an overall aggregated effect of g = 0.66. Performance and skills came in at 0.60,
wellbeing at 0.46, work attitudes at 0.54.

Those are respectable numbers. An effect of 0.66 is comparable to many established
psychological interventions.

**Now the part almost nobody quotes.** The title of that paper specifies *individual level
outcomes in an organizational context*. When a later meta-analysis by researchers at Aston
reviewed the workplace coaching literature, it noted directly that of the eighteen studies in
Theeboom's analysis,
[six reported results of general life coaching and one reported health coaching](https://research.aston.ac.uk/files/17213930/Meta_analysis_of_learning_and_performance_outcomes_from_coaching.pdf)
-- and criticized the mixing of coaching types.

So the most-cited evidence that "coaching works" is predominantly evidence that *workplace
coaching* works, generalized well beyond its base. When a life coach cites Theeboom in their
marketing, they are borrowing from a study largely about executives being coached on the
company's account.

**A second finding is more useful than the headline and rarely mentioned.** Theeboom's team
tested whether the number of sessions moderated outcomes and found that
[a greater number of coaching sessions did not significantly improve results](https://www.academia.edu/55862181/Does_coaching_work_A_meta_analysis_on_the_effects_of_coaching_on_individual_level_outcomes_in_an_organizational_context)
-- and that studies with fewer than five sessions actually reported higher effect sizes for
some outcomes.

That is the third time this pattern has appeared in this journal, after mindfulness and slow
breathing. More is not reliably better. It is also, commercially, the least convenient finding
imaginable for anyone selling a twelve-month package.

**Where the evidence stands.** Real, moderate, replicated effects on goal attainment, self-regulation and
wellbeing -- from a literature concentrated in organizational settings, with small samples,
frequent self-report outcomes, and rare active comparators.

## What it does not do

**It is not therapy and does not treat mental health conditions.** If you are depressed,
anxious to the point of impairment, in crisis, or dealing with trauma, coaching is the wrong
service. A good coach says this in the first session. A bad one -- and there are many -- takes
the money.

**The evidence is not specifically about life coaching.** See above. Anyone presenting the
meta-analytic literature as validation of personal life coaching is overstating what was
studied.

**Longer engagements are not evidenced as better.** The dose finding cuts directly against
long retainers.

**And the mechanism may be mundane.** The strongest effect in the meta-analysis was on
goal-directed self-regulation -- which is broadly what structured goal setting, accountability
and regular reflection with an attentive person would be expected to produce. That is a
legitimate service. It is not a specialized technique.

**One risk worth naming.** Because the field is unregulated, coaches sometimes work well
outside their competence -- offering trauma work, nutritional advice, or claims about
manifestation and mindset that shade into telling people their circumstances are a product of
their thinking. That last framing does real harm to people dealing with illness, redundancy or
bereavement.

## What a session is like

Sessions run 45 to 60 minutes, usually by video, typically every one or two weeks.

The structure is more consistent than people expect. You agree a focus for the session. The
coach asks questions rather than giving answers -- a good coach talks considerably less than
you do. You articulate something you had not quite articulated before. You leave with one or
two specific actions and a date.

What clients most often report valuing is not insight but *accountability*: the fact that
someone will ask, on a known date, whether the thing got done.

Expect $150-$400 per hour in the Bay Area, with executive coaching considerably higher. Many
coaches sell packages of three to six months. Given the dose-response finding, a short initial
engagement with an option to continue is the more defensible structure, and it is reasonable
to ask for it.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often come at a transition -- a role change, a business decision, the end of something,
or a long period of doing well at work while feeling that the wrong things are being optimized.
Coaching is well suited to that, and better suited to it than therapy.

It suits people who are functioning, who have a goal they can name even vaguely, and who
respond to accountability. It suits poorly anyone whose actual issue is a mental health
condition, and anyone hoping the coach will supply the answer -- the format is built on the
opposite premise.

## How to choose a coach

Because there is no licence, credentialing does more work here than usual.

**Look for ICF credentials** -- the International Coaching Federation, which issues ACC, PCC and
MCC based on training hours, logged coaching hours, mentor coaching and an examination. These
are the closest thing the field has to a standard. The Board Certified Coach (BCC) designation
is a reasonable alternative.

**Ask how many hours they have coached**, not how long they have been coaching. The two are
very different numbers.

**Ask what they will refer out.** The right answer names mental health conditions specifically
and mentions having referral relationships. A coach who says they can help with anything is
telling you they do not know the boundary.

**Ask for a trial session and a short initial engagement.** Any confident coach offers the
first; the second is supported by the dose evidence.

**And listen for the red flags.** Guaranteed outcomes. Manifestation or law-of-attraction
framing. Certification from a programme that also sells you the right to certify others.
Pressure to buy twelve months up front. Any suggestion that your health, income or
circumstances are simply a reflection of your mindset.
