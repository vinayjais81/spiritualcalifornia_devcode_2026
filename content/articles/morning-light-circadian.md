Almost everything else on this website is a practice. This one is closer to a fact about how
your body works, and the practice follows from it.

Light is the dominant signal by which the human circadian system sets itself. Not diet, not
exercise, not routine -- light, striking the retina, and morning light in particular. That
mechanism is not contested, and it is the reason this is one of the few practices in this journal
with evidence this strong that you can begin tomorrow at no cost.

What is contested is how far the therapeutic claims extend beyond that, and the honest version
of this article spends most of its time there.

## What it actually is

Light reaches specialized retinal cells -- intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells
containing the pigment **melanopsin** -- which project not to the visual system but to the
brain's master clock in the hypothalamus. These cells are
[particularly sensitive to blue wavelengths around 450 to 480 nanometres](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9511000/),
and melanopsin plays a central role in suppressing melatonin production and in alertness and
neurobehavioural performance.

The timing of light exposure determines the direction of the effect. Light in the morning
advances the clock -- pulling sleep and wake earlier. Light in the evening delays it. This is
why the same lamp can help or harm depending entirely on when you sit under it.

**Clinical light therapy** has a standard protocol: white light at 10,000 lux for about 30
minutes in the early morning, delivered at home by a light box, for up to six weeks. Lux is a
measure of illumination that varies sharply with distance from the source, which is why
positioning matters more than people expect.

For scale: a bright indoor office is perhaps 300-500 lux. Outdoors on an overcast morning is
around 10,000. Direct sun is far more. Stepping outside is, in most cases, the highest-dose
option available.

## What the research shows

**Seasonal affective disorder is the established case.** Bright light therapy is
[recommended by the American Psychiatric Association for seasonal affective disorder on the basis of established efficacy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9566126/).
That is a professional body's treatment recommendation, not an association from a wellness
study, and it is the strongest claim in this article.

**Non-seasonal depression is genuinely promising and genuinely mixed.** A systematic review and
meta-analysis of 11 randomized clinical trials covering more than 850 people compared bright
light therapy -- alone or alongside an antidepressant -- against antidepressant alone, dim red
light, or a placebo device. It found
[a 41% remission rate with light therapy versus 23% for comparison treatment](https://www.psychiatry.org/news-room/apa-blogs/bright-light-therapy-beyond-seasonal-depression).

Other syntheses report a moderate effect size against inactive controls, while flagging
[substantial heterogeneity and small sample sizes as limitations](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9511000/).

**And then the nulls, which deserve equal billing.** A randomized clinical trial in 224
adolescents in psychiatric inpatient care found that four weeks of treatment as usual augmented
with morning bright light therapy
[was not superior to treatment as usual plus placebo red light](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10938243/).
Both groups improved significantly; light added nothing detectable. That is a well-powered
trial with a proper placebo, and it did not find an effect.

A smaller double-blind trial in Taiwan -- 43 outpatients on stable antidepressants, four weeks
of 30-minute morning light against dim red light placebo -- also found no significant reduction
in depressive symptoms, which the authors attributed partly to sample size and partly to
conducting the study in a subtropical climate with abundant daylight.

That last detail matters for California. If ambient light exposure is already high, adding a
light box may have less to offer than it does in a Finnish winter.

**Timing may matter and the evidence disagrees with itself.** Some work has found early morning
administration more effective than late morning; other work has found an antidepressant effect
regardless of circadian phase and sleep timing.

**Where the evidence stands.** Strong for the circadian mechanism and for seasonal affective
disorder. Considerably weaker, honestly, for non-seasonal depression -- moderate pooled effects,
real heterogeneity, and at least one well-powered negative trial.

## What it does not do

**It does not treat non-seasonal depression reliably.** See above. It may help some people; the
adolescent inpatient trial is a caution against confident claims.

**A light box is not obviously better than going outside**, particularly in Northern
California. Fifteen to thirty minutes outdoors in the morning delivers comparable or greater
illumination for nothing.

**Blue-blocking glasses in the evening are a related but separate claim** with a thinner
evidence base than the morning-light literature.

**And it does not replace treatment.** Every positive trial above was light therapy added to,
or compared against, standard care.

**Safety.** Light therapy is low-risk but not risk-free. In people with bipolar disorder it can
trigger hypomania or mania, and it should only be used with psychiatric supervision. People
with retinal disease, macular degeneration or diabetes affecting the eye should check with an
ophthalmologist. Some medications -- certain antibiotics, antipsychotics and St John's wort --
cause photosensitivity. Occasional headache and eye strain are common and usually settle.

## How to do it

**The free version, which is what we would suggest first:**

- **Get outside within an hour of waking**, for 15 to 30 minutes. On an overcast Bay Area
  morning that is still roughly ten times brighter than indoor lighting.
- **Do not wear sunglasses for that portion.** The light needs to reach the retina.
- **Combine it with something you already do** -- coffee outside, the first walk, the commute
  on foot rather than by car.
- **Be consistent about the timing**, more than the duration. The clock responds to regularity.

**The light box version**, if outdoors is impractical or you have diagnosed seasonal
depression:

- 10,000 lux, 30 minutes, within an hour of waking.
- Positioned per the manufacturer's distance specification -- lux falls off sharply, and a box
  placed too far away delivers a fraction of its rated output.
- Not directly in your line of sight; angled, at eye level, while you do something else.
- Not in the evening unless a clinician has specifically advised it.

Light boxes run $50-$200. Sunlight is free and, in this state, abundant.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often come to this for winter low mood, for sleep that has drifted late, for jet lag, and
for the specific pattern of the person who does knowledge work indoors, commutes in a car, and
may go days without meaningful daylight exposure. That last group is large in the Bay Area and
is arguably the intended audience for this entire article.

It suits everyone, in the free version. There is no plausible downside to morning daylight for
most people.

It suits poorly, in the light box version, anyone with bipolar disorder without supervision, and
anyone expecting it to resolve a depression that is not seasonally patterned.

## How to choose a device or a clinician

**For a light box:** look for 10,000 lux rated at a specified distance, UV-filtered, and a
diffusing screen. Ignore claims about specific wavelengths or "full spectrum" branding -- the
clinical protocol is white light at intensity.

**For clinical use:** if you have diagnosed depression, seasonal or otherwise, this belongs in a
conversation with a psychiatrist or GP rather than a purchase decision. Light therapy interacts
with bipolar disorder and with medication photosensitivity, and the timing needs to fit your
actual sleep phase.

**And be sceptical of adjacent products.** Red light therapy panels, infrared devices and
"circadian lighting" systems are marketed alongside light boxes and rest on separate and
generally weaker evidence. The thing with an APA recommendation behind it is bright white light,
in the morning, at 10,000 lux.
