Almost everyone recognizes the experience IFS is built on. A part of you wants to leave the
job. Another part is terrified of what that would mean. A third is contemptuous of both for
taking so long. These are not metaphors people struggle with -- they are how internal conflict
actually feels.

Richard Schwartz's contribution was to take that experience literally as a clinical model: the
mind is naturally multiple, made up of parts with their own perspectives and protective
strategies, organized around a core Self that is not itself a part.

It is an elegant, humane and enormously popular framework. It is also, as a therapy, far less
studied than its popularity implies, and this article exists mostly to say so clearly.

## What it actually is

IFS proposes three categories of parts. **Exiles** carry pain, shame or fear, usually from
early experience. **Managers** work to keep exiles from surfacing, through control,
perfectionism, criticism or achievement. **Firefighters** react when an exile breaks through,
using whatever works fast -- bingeing, dissociation, rage, substances.

Beneath these is the **Self**: described in the model as calm, curious, compassionate and
present, and understood as always intact regardless of what has happened to a person.

Therapy consists of turning toward parts rather than away from them -- getting to know a
critical inner voice, for instance, asking what it is afraid would happen if it stopped -- and
gradually unburdening exiles from what they carry, with the Self rather than the therapist
doing the work.

The non-pathologizing stance is a large part of the appeal. In IFS there are no bad parts,
including the ones doing genuine damage.

## What the research shows

**The strongest study is a proof-of-concept trial in rheumatoid arthritis.** Shadick and
colleagues at Brigham and Women's Hospital randomized 79 patients with RA to
[nine months of IFS or an education control group that received mailed materials](https://www.jrheum.org/content/40/11/1831),
with joint pain, physical function, depression and anxiety measured, and disease activity
assessed by rheumatologists blinded to group assignment. Groups were evaluated every three
months and again a year later.

Two things about it. The blinded rheumatologist assessment is a genuine methodological
strength, rare in this field. And the comparator was nine months of therapy versus *mailed
leaflets* -- which tells you very little about IFS specifically, because almost any sustained
group intervention would be expected to beat that.

**The PTSD evidence is pilot-scale.** Hodgdon and colleagues conducted a pilot study of IFS
for PTSD in 17 adults with histories of childhood trauma, reporting reductions in PTSD
symptoms, affect dysregulation, dissociation, somatization and disrupted self-perception. A
2024 study examined the feasibility and acceptability of an online group-based IFS programme
for PTSD, reporting
[statistically and clinically significant reductions in PTSD severity at treatment end and follow-up](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13284207.2025.2533127).

Both are uncontrolled or feasibility designs. Seventeen participants with no control group is
a signal to run a trial, not a result.

**A 2025 scoping review** consolidated the peer-reviewed literature and found encouraging
results in chronic pain, depression and PTSD, alongside the observation that there are
relatively few rigorous studies for such a widely used model. It identified two randomized
controlled trials in the whole literature -- Haddock and colleagues on depression, and
Shadick on rheumatoid arthritis.

**And the critique from within the profession is direct.** A 2024 analysis published by the
Society for the Advancement of Psychotherapy observed a
[strikingly small evidence base for IFS, particularly given that it was developed thirty years ago](https://societyforpsychotherapy.org/internal-family-systems-exploring-its-problematic-popularity/),
noting that three studies show some support for acceptability or effectiveness in specific
presenting concerns, and contrasting that with the sweeping claims made on the IFS
organization's own website. The same piece raised concern about clinicians applying IFS to
borderline personality presentations, where no effectiveness studies exist at all.

**Where the evidence stands, and it is weaker than the model's reputation suggests.** Reading the
actual literature: two randomized trials in three decades, one with a mailed-leaflet comparator, plus
pilots in the teens. IFS may well work. It has not been adequately tested.

## What it does not do

**It is not established as a first-line trauma treatment.** IFS is widely used for trauma, and
that use runs well ahead of the evidence. Trauma-focused CBT, cognitive processing therapy,
prolonged exposure and EMDR have evidence bases orders of magnitude larger and appear in
clinical guidelines.

**"Evidence-based" is doing heavy lifting in IFS marketing.** Two RCTs and a handful of pilots
is an emerging evidence base, not an established one, and the gap between how the model is
promoted and what has been demonstrated is the single most important caution here.

**Parts are a model, not entities.** The framework's clinical usefulness does not depend on
parts being real structures, and no neuroscience has identified them. Where this becomes a
problem is when the language is taken literally enough to become dissociative rather than
integrative.

**It has not been shown to work for everything it is used for** -- including personality
disorders, addiction and eating disorders, where it is applied frequently.

**Safety.** IFS involves approaching painful material, and in people with significant
dissociative symptoms the parts framing can potentially reinforce fragmentation if handled
badly. This is an argument for a properly trained clinician, not against the model.

## What a session is like

Slower and stranger than conventional therapy, and most people adjust within a session or two.

You bring a difficulty and the therapist asks where you notice it, then invites you to turn
toward whatever is there. The questions are directed at the part rather than at you: *how old
does it seem? what is it afraid would happen if it stopped? what does it want you to know?*

You will be asked how you feel *toward* the part -- and if the answer is irritation or
impatience, that is treated as another part, which gets its own attention first. This
recursion is characteristic and initially disorienting.

There is often direct address. You may find yourself speaking to a part of yourself, or
answering on its behalf. People frequently report that unexpected things arrive -- an age, an
image, a specific memory.

Sessions run 50 to 60 minutes, typically weekly, over months. Expect $150-$250 in California,
covered when the therapist is licensed and billing under that licence.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often come to IFS with harsh inner criticism, with long-standing patterns they
understand intellectually but cannot shift, and with the specific experience of feeling at war
with themselves. The non-pathologizing framing is a genuine relief to people who have spent
years being told which of their behaviours are the problem.

It suits people comfortable with an imaginative, internally-focused process. It suits poorly
anyone who wants a structured symptom-focused protocol, and anyone who would find the parts
language alienating enough to spend the hour resisting it.

Our honest recommendation: for trauma or PTSD specifically, ask about a first-line
trauma-focused therapy before choosing IFS, and ask your therapist directly how they compare.
For self-criticism, internal conflict and long-standing patterns, IFS is a reasonable choice
made with clear eyes about the evidence.

## How to choose a practitioner

**The first question is licensure, not IFS training.** Look for an LMFT, LCSW, psychologist or
psychiatrist who also practises IFS. IFS training is open to non-clinicians, and trauma work
with an unlicensed practitioner is a genuine risk.

**Ask about their IFS training level.** The IFS Institute runs Level 1, 2 and 3 trainings and
a certification process. A therapist who has completed Level 1 is at the start of it.

**Ask what else they are trained in**, particularly for trauma. A therapist who also knows
EMDR or trauma-focused CBT can tell you honestly when IFS is not the right tool -- and that
honesty is the best signal available.

**Ask how they handle dissociation.** A specific answer about pacing and grounding is what you
want.

**And listen to the claim.** "This is a promising model with an emerging evidence base that many
people find helpful" is accurate. "IFS is an evidence-based treatment for trauma" overstates
what two randomized trials in thirty years can support.
