Peter Levine's founding observation was about animals. Prey animals routinely survive
life-threatening encounters, and afterwards -- if you watch closely -- they tremble, shake, and
run through movements that look like the escape they did not complete. Then they return to
grazing. They do not, as far as anyone can tell, develop chronic post-traumatic conditions.

Levine's inference was that humans interrupt this discharge, and that the interrupted defensive
response stays active in the nervous system. Somatic Experiencing is the therapy built on that
inference.

It is a compelling story. The evidence is more mixed than its popularity suggests, and the
gap between the two is the reason for this article.

## What it actually is

Somatic Experiencing is a body-focused psychotherapy for trauma. The therapy's own description
frames post-traumatic symptoms as an expression of stress activation and an incomplete
defensive reaction to a traumatic event, and directs attention to inner physical sensations,
which are treated as the carriers of the traumatic memory.

In practice this means the client is guided to notice bodily sensation -- tightness, heat,
trembling, the impulse to push or turn away -- in small, carefully titrated doses. The
practitioner works to keep arousal inside a tolerable range, moving between activation and
settling, rather than having the client narrate the traumatic event in full.

This is the feature that draws people. Unlike prolonged exposure or trauma-focused CBT, you
are not required to tell the story in detail. For many people that is the difference between
starting treatment and not.

Practitioners complete a three-year training and are credentialed as SEPs -- Somatic
Experiencing Practitioners.

## What the research shows

**The foundational trial is real and its numbers are strong.** Brom and colleagues at the
Herzog Israel Center for the Treatment of Psychotrauma conducted the first randomized
controlled study of SE for PTSD: 63 participants meeting full DSM-IV-TR criteria, randomized to
15 weekly SE sessions or waitlist. The published results showed
[significant intervention effects for post-traumatic symptom severity, with Cohen's d between 0.94 and 1.26, and for depression at around d = 0.7](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jts.22189).

Those are large effects. The design also had a genuine strength: the waitlist group received SE
after the waiting period and was evaluated a third time, which is better practice than most
waitlist studies manage.

**But it was a waitlist comparison**, and for a therapy delivered weekly for fifteen weeks by a
warm, attentive practitioner, waitlist is a weak bar. The trial cannot separate SE from the
general effects of receiving psychotherapy.

**The trials with active comparators are more sobering.** Andersen and colleagues in Denmark
ran the first randomized controlled trial of SE against an active control -- adding SE to
treatment as usual for patients with chronic low back pain and comorbid PTSD, compared with
treatment as usual alone. SE significantly reduced the number of PTSD symptoms, corresponding
to a large effect size, and reduced fear of movement moderately.

Then the authors wrote this, and it should be quoted in every honest account of SE: the
[overall effect of SE was less than expected and the clinical importance of the effects can be questioned](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28680540/).
A follow-up trial testing a full twelve-session programme in the same population was published
in 2020.

**And the mechanism has not been demonstrated.** A 2025 qualitative study comparing veterans'
experiences of SE and prolonged exposure notes plainly that despite claims of releasing stuck
energy and enhancing interoception to promote physiological and emotional regulation,
[there is no rigorous research evidencing these claims](https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/papt.12570),
and that mechanisms of change and non-waitlist comparisons have been lacking.

**Where the evidence stands.** One good waitlist-controlled trial with large effects, one active-controlled trial
whose authors questioned the clinical significance of what they found, and a theoretical
mechanism that remains unvalidated. Promising. Not established.

## What it does not do

**It does not release trauma stored in the body.** This is the practice's central metaphor and
it is not a demonstrated mechanism. Bodily sensation accompanies emotional states -- that much
is well established. The claim that traumatic memory is *held in tissue* and can be discharged
through it is theory, and the field's own literature acknowledges the absence of evidence.

**It is not currently a first-line PTSD treatment.** Trauma-focused CBT, prolonged exposure,
cognitive processing therapy and EMDR have far larger evidence bases and appear in clinical
guidelines. SE does not. Anyone presenting it as equivalent is misrepresenting the state of the
field.

**The animal story is an analogy, not evidence.** Levine's observation about prey animals is
evocative and it is not data about human trauma.

**And it has not been shown to work better than other therapies** -- because with one partial
exception, it has barely been compared against them.

**Safety.** Trauma work of any kind can destabilize, and SE's titrated approach is explicitly
designed to reduce that risk. But SE practitioners are not necessarily licensed mental health
professionals -- the SEP credential can be held by people from many backgrounds -- which matters
a great deal when the material is trauma. See the practitioner section.

## What a session is like

Sessions run 50 to 60 minutes and are quieter and slower than most therapy.

There is conversation, but it is repeatedly interrupted by the practitioner asking what you
notice in your body right now. Where is that in you? What is the quality of it? What happens if
you stay with it for a moment? The pace is deliberately slow, and long silences are normal.

You may be asked to track a sensation as it moves or changes, to notice an impulse -- to push,
to turn, to curl -- and to follow it in small movements. Trembling, shaking, temperature changes
and spontaneous deep breaths are common and are treated as the process working.

What is notably absent is pressure to describe what happened. Many clients say more about the
present moment than about the past for several sessions.

A course typically runs 10 to 20 sessions. Expect $150-$250 per session in California, covered
by insurance only when the practitioner is independently licensed and bills under that licence.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often come to SE after other trauma treatments have felt intolerable or have not
helped, and specifically because it does not require re-telling. It is also sought by people
whose difficulty presents physically -- chronic tension, startle, freeze responses, digestive
symptoms with no medical explanation -- and by people who describe feeling disconnected from
their bodies.

It suits people who can tolerate slowness and ambiguity, and who are curious about internal
sensation.

Our honest recommendation: if you have PTSD and have not tried a first-line trauma treatment,
try one first. The evidence gap between those and SE is large. If first-line treatment has
failed you, or you cannot face it, SE is a reasonable next option with a genuine if
preliminary trial record.

## How to choose a practitioner

**The most important question: are you independently licensed?** Look for an SEP who is also an
LMFT, LCSW, psychologist or psychiatrist. The SEP credential alone does not make someone a
mental health professional, and trauma work without that underlying training and accountability
is a real risk. In California, ask for the licence number and check it with the relevant board.

**Ask about their trauma training beyond SE.** A practitioner who also knows EMDR or
trauma-focused CBT can tell you honestly when SE is not the right tool.

**Ask how they handle destabilization**, and expect a specific answer involving pacing,
grounding, and a plan for between sessions.

**Ask what they claim.** "This may help you work with trauma without needing to recount it, and
the research is early" is accurate. "This releases trauma stored in your tissues" is a
metaphor being sold as a mechanism.

**One firm line.** Anyone advising you to stop psychiatric medication, or presenting SE as a
replacement for treatment of a diagnosed condition, should be declined.
