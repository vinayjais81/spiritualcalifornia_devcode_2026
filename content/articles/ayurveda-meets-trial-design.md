Ayurveda is not one thing, and this article will be much more useful if we separate its parts
before assessing them.

There is Ayurveda as a **lifestyle system**: daily routine, sleep timing, diet adjusted to
constitution, oil massage, tongue scraping, yoga and breathing practice. There is Ayurveda as
**bodywork**: abhyanga, shirodhara, the panchakarma programme. And there is Ayurveda as
**pharmacology**: herbal formulas and mineral preparations taken internally.

The first two carry ordinary risks. The third has a documented and serious problem, and it is
the reason this article is long.

## What it actually is

Ayurveda originated in the Indian subcontinent more than two thousand years ago and remains
widely practised -- by an estimated majority of India's population. It organizes health around
three functional principles, the *doshas* (vata, pitta, kapha), whose balance is held to
determine constitution and illness. Treatment aims to restore balance through diet, routine,
bodywork, purification procedures and internal preparations.

The internal preparations divide into two categories, and the distinction is the single most
important practical fact in this article.

**Herbal-only** products contain plant material.

**Rasa shastra** -- literally mercury science -- is the ancient practice of deliberately
combining herbs with metals, minerals and gems, including mercury, lead, iron and zinc, after
elaborate preparation processes. Practitioners hold that these processes render the metals
safe and therapeutically active.

## What the research shows

**On efficacy, the evidence is thin.** Individual Ayurvedic interventions have been trialled --
turmeric and curcumin, ashwagandha, guggul, various formulations for arthritis and metabolic
conditions -- with results that are generally small-scale, heterogeneous, and concentrated in
journals with limited independent replication. There is nothing here comparable to the
individual patient data on acupuncture or the Cochrane review on music therapy. Ayurveda's
lifestyle components overlap heavily with yoga, diet and sleep regularity, each of which has its
own better-developed literature.

**On safety, the evidence is strong, specific and unwelcome.**

Saper and colleagues at Boston University purchased 230 Ayurvedic medicines online from 37 US
and Indian manufacturers and analysed 193 of them. The
[prevalence of metal-containing products was 20.7% (95% CI 15.2-27.1%)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18728265/?dopt=Abstract),
with no significant difference between US-manufactured (21.7%) and Indian-manufactured (19.5%)
products.

Rasa shastra medicines were
[more than twice as likely to contain metals -- 40.6% versus 17.1% -- with higher median lead concentrations](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18728265/?dopt=Abstract).
But note that the herbal-only figure was 17%. One in six purely herbal products contained lead,
mercury or arsenic.

Three details from that study deserve emphasis. Among products containing metals, 95% were sold
by US websites. Seventy-five percent
[claimed Good Manufacturing Practices or heavy metal testing](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2008/08/080826190850.htm).
And all metal-containing products exceeded one or more standards for acceptable daily intake of
toxic metals.

**The earlier study found higher concentrations still.** Saper's 2004 analysis of Ayurvedic
products in Boston-area stores found
[14 of 70 (20%) contained heavy metals -- lead at a median 40 µg/g with a range up to 37,000, and mercury at a median 20,225 µg/g with a range up to 104,000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15598918/).
A median mercury concentration of 20,225 micrograms per gram is roughly 2% mercury by weight.

**And people have been poisoned.** Case series document lead poisoning associated with these
supplements over several decades, including severe lead poisoning in pregnant women in New York
City in 2011 and 2012 and in two small children in 2012, and published case reports of severe
arsenic poisoning from Ayurvedic supplements.

**The counter-argument, stated fairly.** Ayurvedic practitioners and some researchers argue that
metals in properly prepared rasa shastra preparations are converted into mixed oxides whose
toxicity is destroyed and medicinal properties induced, and that studies of these preparations
in model organisms suggest safety. Saper and his colleague Thuppil Venkatesh, a professor
emeritus in Bangalore, respond that those studies are methodologically flawed and that the
preparations do not in fact convert heavy metals into non-toxic compounds.

This journal's position: the burden of proof sits with the party proposing that people ingest
lead and mercury. It has not been met.

**Where the evidence stands.** For the system overall the efficacy literature is thin -- while
the safety literature on internal preparations is strong, and points firmly the wrong way.

## What it does not do

**It does not treat serious disease, and the claim is dangerous.** Ayurvedic treatment
substituted for oncology, cardiology or psychiatric care is the highest-risk scenario on this
entire website.

**"Detoxification" through panchakarma is not detoxification in any measurable sense**, and
some purification procedures -- purgatives, emetics, extended fasting -- carry real risks of
electrolyte disturbance and dehydration, particularly in older or unwell people.

**Constitutional typing is not diagnostic.** Dosha assessment has not demonstrated reliability
or predictive validity, and it should not substitute for medical investigation of symptoms.

**And US manufacture is not a safety signal.** This is the most counter-intuitive finding in the
data and worth repeating: US-made products were just as likely to contain toxic metals as
Indian-made ones, and most metal-containing products were sold by US websites, three-quarters of
which claimed good manufacturing practice or metals testing.

## The parts that are lower-risk

We want to be fair here, because a reader could take the above as a reason to dismiss the whole
tradition, and that would be a mistake.

**Abhyanga** -- warm oil self-massage -- is pleasant, low-risk bodywork. **Shirodhara**, the
warm oil poured on the forehead, is reported as deeply relaxing and carries little risk beyond
the oil. **Dinacharya**, the daily routine emphasising regular sleep and wake times, consistent
meal timing and morning practices, is broadly consistent with what circadian research
supports -- see our morning light article. **Tongue scraping** is harmless and reduces oral
bacteria. And Ayurveda's dietary emphasis on cooked, warm, unprocessed food is unremarkable in
the best sense.

None of these involve ingesting a product of unknown metal content. If Ayurveda appeals to you,
this is where to start, and possibly where to stop.

## What a consultation is like

The first appointment runs 60 to 90 minutes: extensive questions about digestion, sleep,
temperature, energy, appetite, elimination and emotional patterns, plus pulse assessment and
examination of the tongue, eyes and skin. You will be assigned a constitutional type and a
current imbalance.

You will leave with recommendations across four areas: diet, daily routine, bodywork, and
usually herbal preparations.

Take the first three. For the fourth, read the section below before taking anything.

Expect $100-$250 for an initial consultation in California, plus $30-$100 per week for herbs
and $100-$200 for bodywork sessions. Panchakarma programmes run from several hundred to several
thousand dollars.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often come to Ayurveda for digestive complaints, for sleep and energy, for stress, and
for the appeal of a coherent system that addresses the whole of daily life rather than a symptom.
That coherence is a genuine strength; few things in modern healthcare tell you how to structure
a day.

It suits people who want structure and are willing to change routine. It suits poorly anyone
with a serious medical condition seeking an alternative to treatment, anyone pregnant, anyone
with liver or kidney disease, and anyone unwilling to interrogate what is in a supplement.

## How to choose a practitioner -- and the rules on preparations

Ayurveda is not licensed in California. Practitioners may hold credentials from the National
Ayurvedic Medical Association (NAMA) as Ayurvedic Health Counselor, Ayurvedic Practitioner or
Ayurvedic Doctor. Some have BAMS degrees from Indian medical universities -- a substantial
qualification, though it does not confer US licensure.

**Ask what they hold, and ask what they will not treat.**

**Then, for anything you are asked to swallow, apply these rules without exception:**

- **Refuse rasa shastra preparations.** Bhasmas, sindoors and any product listing metals,
  minerals or gems. The 40% figure is not a risk worth taking for a benefit that has not been
  demonstrated.
- **Demand a certificate of analysis** showing third-party heavy metal testing for the specific
  batch. Not a manufacturer's claim on a website -- three-quarters of the metal-containing
  products in the JAMA study made exactly that claim.
- **Demand the full ingredient list in writing.**
- **Tell your physician**, and if you take herbal preparations for any sustained period, ask
  about a blood lead level. This is a simple, cheap test.
- **Do not give Ayurvedic preparations to children or take them while pregnant.** The documented
  poisonings are concentrated in exactly these groups.

If a practitioner is offended by any of that, you have your answer. A good one will already be
doing most of it.
