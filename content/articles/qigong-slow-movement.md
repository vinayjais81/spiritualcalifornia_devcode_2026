Qigong looks like tai chi to anyone watching from a distance, and the two are frequently
taught by the same person. The difference matters for a practical reason: qigong is far
easier to learn, and its evidence base is a step weaker.

Both facts are worth knowing before you choose between them. If your reason for showing up
is balance and falls, the tai chi literature is stronger. If your reason is chronic pain,
fatigue, or the fact that you will realistically only commit to something you can learn in
one session, qigong is the better bet.

## What it actually is

Qigong -- literally energy work -- is an umbrella term covering thousands of Chinese
practices combining slow movement, posture, breath regulation and attention. Some sets are
standing, some seated, some involve almost no movement at all.

The crucial structural difference from tai chi is that qigong is generally built from
*repeated single movements* rather than a long choreographed sequence. Baduanjin -- the
Eight Pieces of Brocade, the most-studied set in the research literature -- consists of
eight discrete movements, each repeated six to eight times. Total practice time: ten to
fifteen minutes. You can be doing it credibly within a week.

That accessibility is why it appears so often in clinical trials. You can teach a hospital
ward or a group of cancer patients Baduanjin in a session or two, which you cannot do with
the Yang 24-form.

## What the research shows

**Chronic low back pain** is where the numbers are strongest. A 2025 systematic review and
meta-analysis of eight randomized controlled trials covering 729 adults found that qigong
and tai chi
[significantly reduced pain intensity (SMD -1.07) and disability (SMD -0.77)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12173434/),
with GRADE certainty rated moderate to high. Read the subgroup finding too: effects were
larger against passive controls (SMD -1.17) than against active ones (SMD -0.98), and
heterogeneity was very high at I² = 93%. The direction is consistent; the precision is not
good.

**Fibromyalgia.** A systematic review and meta-analysis by Lauche and colleagues located
seven trials with 395 patients and found
[low-quality evidence for short-term improvement in pain, quality of life and sleep quality](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3833122/),
and very low-quality evidence for fatigue, compared with usual care. The sentence that
follows in that review is the one that matters: no evidence was found for superiority of
qigong compared with *active* treatments. No serious adverse events were reported.

**Cancer-related symptoms.** Wayne and colleagues meta-analyzed tai chi and qigong for
cancer-related symptoms and quality of life in the *Journal of Cancer Survivorship*,
finding benefits in a population where fatigue is often the dominant complaint and
conventional exercise is frequently not tolerable.

**Cardiovascular and metabolic risk factors.** The stroke-prevention review discussed in
our tai chi article pooled 21 trials and 1,604 patients with hypertension, hyperlipidaemia,
diabetes or metabolic syndrome. Risk factors improved;
[no trial examined stroke incidence](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5690748/).
A separate review examined
[tai chi and qigong in chronic heart failure](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7755480/),
using peak oxygen consumption and six-minute walking distance as primary outcomes -- hard
measures rather than questionnaires.

**Where it thins out fast.** A review of tai chi and qigong for depression and anxiety in
people with substance use disorders found
[one randomized trial and six non-randomized studies, 772 participants total](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7260819/).
One RCT is not an evidence base, and the authors framed the review as filling a gap rather
than answering a question.

**Where the evidence stands.** Replicated, sometimes sizeable effects on pain, fatigue and function, from
trials with high heterogeneity, frequent risk-of-bias concerns, and -- critically -- no
demonstrated advantage over other active interventions. Qigong appears to work. It has not
been shown to work *better* than the alternatives.

## What it does not do

**It has not outperformed active treatment.** This is the single most important sentence in
the article. Against usual care or a waiting list, qigong performs respectably. Against
gentle exercise, stretching or education, the advantage disappears in the fibromyalgia
literature. If you would enjoy qigong more than a stretching class, that is a good reason
to choose it. It is not evidence that it is more effective.

**It does not move qi in any measurable sense.** No instrument has detected the energy the
practice describes, and no study has isolated an energetic mechanism. What is plausibly
happening is low-intensity weight-bearing movement, breath regulation at slow rates with
documented autonomic effects, sustained attention, and group participation -- four things
with independent evidence behind them.

**It is not a treatment for cancer, heart failure, hypertension or diabetes.** It appears
in supportive-care research for all of these, alongside medical treatment. That is a
different claim.

**And external qigong is a separate matter entirely.** Practices in which a master directs
qi toward a patient without contact have a far thinner and poorer-quality evidence base
than the self-practice movement sets described here. Trials of the two are frequently
pooled in popular summaries. They should not be.

Qigong is notably safe -- the fibromyalgia review reported no serious adverse events, which
is unusual to be able to say plainly.

## What a class is like

You stand, feet parallel, knees slightly soft. The teacher shows one movement -- often
something like raising both arms overhead while inhaling and lowering while exhaling -- and
you repeat it six or eight times. Then the next. Then the next.

It is far less intimidating than tai chi because there is no sequence to forget. Nobody is
watching you fall behind. Most sets take ten to twenty minutes and can be done in a
bedroom in ordinary clothes.

The sensation people report first is warmth in the hands and a slight heaviness in the
arms -- consistent with peripheral circulation and slow breathing, and traditionally
described as qi gathering. Both descriptions point at the same experience.

Classes run $10-$25 in California, frequently free at senior and community centres, and
qigong is genuinely well-suited to learning from good video once a teacher has corrected
your posture a few times. Seated versions exist for anyone who cannot stand for twenty
minutes, which is one of its real advantages over almost everything else on this site.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often seek qigong for persistent low back pain, for the fatigue that follows illness
or treatment, and for the specific situation of needing to move without impact, sweat or
floor work. It is one of the few practices here that is realistic during cancer treatment,
in later life, or with limited mobility.

It suits people who want the shortest possible distance between deciding to start and
actually practising. It suits poorly anyone looking for a demanding workout or a deep
technical study -- for the latter, tai chi rewards the investment more.

## How to choose a teacher

Ask which set they teach and where they learned it. Baduanjin, Yijinjing, Wuqinxi and
Liuzijue are the standardized traditional sets and the ones the research overwhelmingly
uses; a teacher who names a set and a lineage is on firmer ground than one teaching a
personal synthesis.

Ask whether they teach self-practice or external qigong. If the answer involves them
sending energy into you, understand that you have crossed from a practice with real if modest evidence into
something with almost no supporting evidence, and that the price should reflect it.

And ask what they claim. A teacher describing improvements in pain, stiffness, energy and
sleep is consistent with the literature. One offering to treat a diagnosed disease, or
advising you to change medication, is not, and should be left.

For anyone whose main concern is falling, read our tai chi article before choosing. That
evidence is considerably stronger, and honesty about which practice fits which problem is more
useful to you than promoting both equally.
