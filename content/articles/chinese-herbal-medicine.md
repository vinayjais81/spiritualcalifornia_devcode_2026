In 2015, Tu Youyou received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. She had spent the
early 1970s reading fourth-century Chinese medical texts, found a reference to *qinghao* --
sweet wormwood -- prescribed for intermittent fevers, and isolated from it the compound
artemisinin. Artemisinin-based therapy has since become the global first-line treatment for
malaria and has saved millions of lives.

That is the strongest possible argument for taking the Chinese herbal *materia medica*
seriously. It is also, precisely, an argument for pharmacology. Artemisinin works because
it is a molecule with a dose, a mechanism, a purity standard and a manufacturing chain --
not because it appeared in an old book.

Which is the frame for everything that follows. Chinese herbs contain real drugs. Real
drugs have real risks, and the risks in this field are unusually well documented.

## What it actually is

Chinese herbal medicine prescribes formulas -- typically six to fifteen ingredients --
selected according to a traditional diagnostic pattern rather than a Western disease label.
Ingredients are predominantly plant material, with some mineral and animal components,
prepared as raw decoctions to be boiled at home, as granules dissolved in water, or as
manufactured "patent" pills.

A trained practitioner is not treating your headache. They are treating a pattern -- liver
qi stagnation, spleen deficiency, blood stasis -- and the formula is adjusted at each visit.
This is a coherent internal system with two millennia of accumulated clinical observation
behind it.

The distinction that matters practically is between **raw herbs prescribed and prepared
individually** and **patent medicines manufactured at scale**. Nearly all of the documented
harm in the literature clusters in the second category.

## What the research shows

Let us be straightforward: the efficacy evidence for whole Chinese herbal formulas is
substantially weaker than the evidence for acupuncture, and it is not where the strongest
research in this field sits. The strongest research in this field is on safety, and it is
extensive.

**Liver injury is the best-characterized harm.** A systematic review by Zhang and colleagues
in *Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine* identified
[four single herbs, twenty-one patent drugs and four decoctions reported to cause hepatotoxicity](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2016/3560812),
with *He-Shou-Wu* (*Polygonum multiflorum*) by far the most frequently implicated -- 65 of
114 cases. He-Shou-Wu is widely sold in the West as a hair and longevity tonic.

The NIH's LiverTox database summarizes that review and notes that of the 114 cases, 54 were
judged
[at least probably attributable, with an average latency of 30 days and mean ALT of 1,246 U/L](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548589/).
An ALT of that magnitude is not a mild abnormality; it is significant hepatocellular injury.

**Adulteration with pharmaceutical drugs is documented and not rare.** Edzard Ernst's
systematic review in the *Journal of Internal Medicine* found Chinese herbal products
adulterated with conventional medications -- NSAIDs, corticosteroids, benzodiazepines,
diuretics and antidiabetic drugs -- in up to 24% of products examined. That is the finding
to sit with. A patient taking a "natural" formula may be taking an undisclosed steroid at
an unknown dose.

**Heavy-metal contamination is measurable and widespread.** An analysis of 1,773 herbal
samples from around the world using ICP-MS found that
[30.51% exceeded the Chinese Pharmacopoeia limit for at least one metal](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7883644/)
-- lead in 5.75%, cadmium 4.96%, arsenic 4.17%, mercury 3.78%, copper 1.75%. For twenty-five
herbs, estimated daily intake of lead, cadmium, mercury or arsenic exceeded the provisional
tolerable daily intake. A separate clinical review examined
[adverse events specifically from metal contamination of traditional Chinese medicines in Korea](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4108800).

**And a 2026 analysis** of 386 alternative medicinal products implicated in liver injury at a
tertiary hepatology centre found
[hepatotoxic botanicals, pharmaceutical adulteration, heavy metals and undisclosed animal content](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/gastroenterology/articles/10.3389/fgstr.2026.1784785/full),
noting that systematic reviews of herbal contamination have documented multi-organ failure,
hepatorenal syndrome and death, with traditional Indian and Chinese remedies most commonly
implicated.

**Where the evidence stands.** This assessment is about efficacy, not about the safety evidence --
which is strong, and points the wrong way. Individual constituents have proven pharmacological activity.
Whole-formula clinical trials are largely small, heterogeneous, and concentrated in
journals with known publication-bias problems. Meanwhile the harm literature is specific,
replicated, and quantified.

## What it does not do

**It is not safe by virtue of being natural.** This is the central misconception and the one
this article exists to correct. Aconite, podophyllin and anticholinergic-containing herbs
have caused severe and fatal poisonings. Reviews identify the specific risk factors:
misidentified herbs, improper processing, overdose, excessive duration, inappropriate
combination, and microbial or heavy-metal contamination.

**It does not interact neutrally with your medication.** Herb-drug interactions are a
documented category, not a theoretical one. If you take anticoagulants, immunosuppressants,
antiretrovirals, chemotherapy, thyroid medication or antiseizure drugs, herbal formulas
belong in a conversation with your prescribing physician first. Not instead. First.

**It is not regulated as medicine in the United States.** Herbal products are sold as
dietary supplements, which means no pre-market approval for safety or efficacy, and no
routine verification that the contents match the label. The contamination and adulteration
findings above are what that regulatory gap looks like in practice.

**And the tradition cannot vouch for the supply chain.** Two thousand years of clinical
observation says something about which plants to use. It says nothing about whether the
batch on the shelf was grown in contaminated soil or cut with prednisone.

## What a consultation is like

The first visit runs 45 to 90 minutes and resembles the acupuncture intake: detailed
questions about sleep, digestion, temperature, energy, appetite and cycle, plus pulse at
three positions on each wrist and examination of the tongue.

You leave with a formula. Raw herbs come as a bag of dried material to be simmered for
thirty to forty minutes -- the resulting decoction is famously unpleasant, bitter and
earthy, and the smell stays in the kitchen. Granules dissolved in hot water are more
common now and taste marginally better. Pills are easiest and are also the category with
the worst documented quality control.

Formulas are revised every two to four weeks. A practitioner who gives you the same formula
for six months is not practising the system as designed.

Expect $60-$150 for the consultation plus $20-$60 per week for herbs in California, rarely
covered by insurance.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often seek herbal medicine for digestive complaints, menstrual and menopausal
symptoms, sleep, and chronic conditions where they have found conventional treatment
insufficient. It is often prescribed alongside acupuncture by the same practitioner.

Given the above, it suits people who are willing to treat it as *taking medication* -- which
means disclosing it to their doctor, knowing what is in it, and being alert to symptoms.
It suits poorly anyone taking narrow-therapeutic-index drugs, anyone pregnant, anyone with
existing liver or kidney disease, and anyone who wants something they can take casually
without thinking about it.

## How to choose a practitioner -- and this is the important section

In California, look for **L.Ac.** licensure. California's scope of practice includes
herbal prescribing and the training covers it substantively. Nationally, the relevant
credential is **Dipl. O.M.** or **Dipl. C.H. (NCCAOM)** -- the NCCAOM Chinese herbology
certification specifically, which is distinct from acupuncture-only certification. Ask
which they hold.

Then ask about sourcing, and treat the answer as the deciding factor:

- **Which supplier do you use, and do they provide third-party testing for heavy metals and pesticides?** A serious herbalist knows their supplier by name and can answer this without hesitation. Reputable suppliers publish certificates of analysis.
- **Are these raw herbs or patent pills?** Prefer practitioner-prescribed raw herbs or granules from a tested supplier over off-the-shelf patent medicines of unclear origin.
- **What is in this formula, in writing?** You are entitled to the full ingredient list. If you cannot get it, do not take it.

Three things that should end the appointment. Anyone who tells you to stop or reduce a
prescribed medication. Anyone who says herbs cannot harm you. And any product containing
He-Shou-Wu offered without a word about liver monitoring.

Finally: tell your physician. Herbal medicine's documented risks are almost entirely
manageable when someone with access to your bloodwork knows you are taking it. Almost all
the documented catastrophes involve nobody knowing.
