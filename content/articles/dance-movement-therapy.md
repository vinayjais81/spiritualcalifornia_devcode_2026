Marian Chace was a dancer, not a clinician. In the 1940s she began working at St Elizabeths
Hospital in Washington with patients whom psychiatry had largely given up on -- people who had
stopped speaking, or whose speech had stopped carrying meaning.

Her method was disarmingly simple. She watched how a patient moved, and moved with them.
Mirroring, she called it -- not imitation exactly, but reflecting the quality of a person's
movement back to them as a way of saying *I see what you are doing, and I am willing to do it
too*.

Eighty years later that remains the technique at the centre of the profession, and the
research has an interesting relationship with it.

## What it actually is

Dance/movement therapy (DMT) is the psychotherapeutic use of movement to support emotional,
cognitive, physical and social integration. Practitioners in the United States are credentialed
by the American Dance Therapy Association.

It is not dance instruction, and there is no choreography. Sessions typically involve a warm-up
in which the group moves together, a middle phase of improvisation and thematic exploration
often initiated by whatever the therapist notices in the room, and a closing that brings the
group back to something shared and settled.

The central techniques are **mirroring** -- the therapist reflecting a client's movement
qualities to communicate empathic understanding -- and **kinaesthetic empathy**, the therapist's
use of their own bodily responses as clinical information. Rhythmic group synchrony is used
deliberately to build cohesion.

The clinical premise is that emotional states have postural and movement signatures, that
those signatures are available before and beneath language, and that working with them
directly reaches material that conversation circles.

## What the research shows

**Cochrane looked, and declined to conclude.** Meekums, Karkou and Nelson reviewed dance
movement therapy for depression and found only
[three studies meeting inclusion criteria, covering 147 participants in total](https://www.cochrane.org/CD009895/DEPRESSN_dance-movement-therapy-effective-treatment-depression-review-evidence).
A subgroup analysis suggested a positive effect for adults, with two of the three studies
showing DMT reducing depressive symptoms more than standard care at follow-up. But the
methodological quality was low and the samples small, and the reviewers stated that no firm
conclusions could be drawn.

**A later meta-analysis reached a warmer conclusion by a defensible route.** Karkou and
colleagues restricted their analysis to controlled designs with pre- and post-treatment
testing, and reported that
[studies of moderate to high methodological quality demonstrate strong support for DMT in depression](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00936/full).

Those two findings are not in contradiction. Cochrane applies a strict inclusion filter and
then reports what it finds, including when what it finds is not enough. The later analysis
included more studies and weighted by quality. Both are legitimate; the difference is method,
not honesty.

**A multicentre randomized trial in Finland** subsequently tested DMT for depression across
several sites, adding a better-powered data point to a literature that badly needed one.

**Cochrane has reviewed DMT across other populations too** -- schizophrenia, dementia, and
cancer care, where Bradt and colleagues assessed psychological and physical outcomes. A 2024
systematic review and meta-analysis extended this to dance movement interventions in cancer
patients more broadly. The recurring pattern is a small number of studies per condition.

**And Cochrane made an observation that this journal admires.** In discussing mechanism, the
reviewers noted that as a form of psychological therapy, DMT is very likely to work primarily
through *non-specific factors* -- notably the therapeutic relationship, which is one of the
best-established determinants of outcome across all therapies. They then argued that DMT's
specific contribution may lie in non-verbal communication and embodied empathy.

That is exactly how a field should talk about itself: name the common factor first, then make
the case for what is distinctive.

**Where the evidence stands.** A real and replicated signal in depression, a credentialed profession with coherent
theory, and an evidence base that remains small enough that the most rigorous reviewer of it
would not commit.

## What it does not do

**It is not established as a standalone treatment for depression.** The trials compare it
against standard care or add it to standard care. Nothing here supports replacing treatment.

**The specific movement techniques have not been isolated.** Per Cochrane's own reasoning, much
of the effect may be the therapeutic relationship and the group. Whether mirroring outperforms
a warm therapist doing something else has not been tested.

**It is not exercise, and it should not be sold as fitness.** Some benefit may come from the
physical activity, which is itself well evidenced for mood -- but that would make it a pleasant
delivery mechanism for exercise rather than a distinct psychotherapy.

**And "the body keeps the score" is a book title, not a finding.** The idea that trauma is
stored in tissue in a literal, retrievable way is not established. What is reasonable and
better supported is that emotional states have bodily correlates, and that attending to them
can be clinically useful.

DMT is safe. The considerations are physical -- injury, mobility, pain conditions -- and
psychological, since moving expressively in a group can surface strong feeling. A credentialed
therapist screens for both.

## What a session is like

Everyone stands in a loose circle. There is music, often unobtrusive. The therapist begins
with something simple and repetitive that the whole group does together -- swaying, reaching,
walking the room -- and the synchrony does most of the early work of making people less
self-conscious.

Then it opens up. The therapist may notice something in one person's movement and invite the
group to take it up. There may be work in pairs, mirroring each other. There is usually no
requirement to speak, and for many clients that is the appeal.

Individual sessions are quieter and more like conversation interrupted by movement -- a
therapist might ask what a feeling would look like if your shoulders showed it.

What people report is a specific and unfamiliar tiredness, occasional strong emotion arriving
mid-movement without warning, and a sense afterwards of occupying more of themselves. The
self-consciousness of the first twenty minutes is universal and does pass.

Sessions run 60 to 90 minutes. Expect $80-$150 individually in California, $25-$60 in groups,
and free or covered inside hospital, psychiatric and eldercare programmes.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often come to DMT when verbal therapy has plateaued, when they feel disconnected from
their bodies, or when the difficulty is not something they can put into a sentence. It is
widely used in psychiatric settings, in dementia care, with children, and in eating disorder
treatment, where the relationship with the body is the clinical territory.

It suits people who are willing to move in front of others without performing. It suits poorly
anyone expecting a dance class, and anyone for whom being observed while moving would itself be
the source of distress -- though a good therapist can work with that, and sometimes it is the
work.

## How to choose a practitioner

**Look for R-DMT or BC-DMT** -- Registered or Board Certified Dance/Movement Therapist, through
the American Dance Therapy Association. Both require a master's degree; BC-DMT requires
substantial additional supervised clinical experience.

**Ask what else they are licensed as.** Many are also LMFTs or LPCCs, which matters for scope
and for insurance.

**Ask about their setting and population.** DMT in dementia care and DMT in eating disorder
treatment share a method and almost nothing else.

**Ask what happens if you do not want to move.** The right answer involves meeting you where
you are, not encouragement to push through.

**One line worth drawing.** A "dance healing" or "movement medicine" facilitator without ADTA
credentials is offering something else. It may be joyful and worth doing -- our ecstatic dance
article covers that honestly -- but it is not the practice the trials studied, and it should not
borrow the evidence.
