For most of the twentieth century, fascia was what anatomists cut away to get to the
interesting parts. It arrived in the dissection room as packing material: whitish,
uncooperative, in the way. Textbooks named its regions and moved on.

Then in 2002, Helene Langevin and Jason Yandow published a paper in *The Anatomical Record*
-- a mainstream, unglamorous anatomy journal -- reporting that when they mapped acupuncture
points against connective tissue planes in the human arm, they found something they had not
expected.

## What it actually is

Fascia is the continuous sheet of connective tissue that wraps and separates every muscle,
organ, nerve and vessel in the body. Not a collection of structures with individual names,
but one interconnected network -- which is why the field's own reviews stress the common
origin of the fascial system's layers despite the diverse names given to its parts.

Three properties matter for what follows. It is **richly innervated** -- densely supplied
with sensory nerve endings, making it a sensory organ, not merely a wrapping. It
**transmits mechanical force** across distances, so tension applied in one place is felt
elsewhere. And it is **populated by fibroblasts** that respond to mechanical strain by
altering their shape and their protein synthesis -- a process called mechanotransduction.

That last property is the one that turns an anatomical curiosity into a possible mechanism.

## What the research shows

**The correspondence finding.** Langevin and Yandow reported in
[*The Anatomical Record*](https://doi.org/10.1002/ar.10185)
the anatomical relationship between acupuncture points and meridians and connective tissue
planes. Subsequent summaries of that work describe correlations between intermuscular or
intramuscular connective tissue planes and more than 80% of acupuncture points, along with
roughly 50% of meridian intersections.

Independent work has approached it from the other direction. Analyses comparing acupuncture
Principal Meridians with the "myofascial meridians" identified from Western anatomical
dissection found a strong correspondence between the two distributions -- an anatomically
independent line of evidence arrived at by people who were not looking for it.

**The mechanism work.** Langevin's group went further and asked what a needle actually does
to tissue. Their paper on
[connective tissue involvement in acupuncture](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2005290110600234)
in *The FASEB Journal* documented that needle rotation winds and grips the connective
tissue, creating measurable mechanical tension. A
[2025 review of fibroblasts as cellular targets in acupuncture](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2025.1662525/full)
sets out the current picture: tension between needle and connective tissue transmits
mechanical signals to connective tissue cells, which regulate matrix protein synthesis
through autocrine and paracrine pathways and influence cell proliferation and migration.
The review notes this mechanotransduction may partly explain acupuncture's local and distant
effects, and that the *de qi* sensation -- the deep, spreading heaviness patients report --
appears related to connective tissue rather than to nerve stimulation alone. Langevin's team
also found that roughly 30% of fibroblasts connect directly to one another, suggesting a
role in cellular communication across the network.

**The pain work, which is arguably the most clinically important.** A convergence of
evidence suggests chronic low back pain may originate in connective tissue rather than in
bone, cartilage or muscle. The magnitude of low back pain
[does not correlate with the magnitude of disc displacement](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3092510/)
-- a finding that has frustrated spine clinicians for decades -- and subsequent work has
implicated perispinal ligamentous tissue and lumbar fascia, with histological evidence of
inflammation in the lumbar fasciae. Langevin and Sherman built a model of chronic low back
pain on exactly this basis.

**And the hypothesis, clearly labelled as such.** A review in *Evidence-Based Complementary
and Alternative Medicine* proposed the fascia network hypothesis of meridians: that the
fascia network is the anatomical basis for acupoints and meridians, and that the
histological composition of meridians is nonspecific connective tissue. China's 973 Program
funded a project on precisely this, in which a team led by Lin Yuan applied digital
reconstruction to map fascial connective tissue in the human forearm and compared its
spatial distribution against traditional meridian pathways.

**Where the evidence stands.** The anatomy is real and published in mainstream venues. The mechanotransduction
is real and measurable. The claim that fascia *is* the meridian system remains a hypothesis
with supportive but not conclusive evidence, and its own advocates say so -- one review of
the connective tissue hypothesis concluded there is little good-quality conclusive evidence
available, and that what exists is compelling and incomplete.

## What it does not do

**Fascia does not hold memory.** You will see this claim constantly -- that fascia stores
emotional trauma, that releasing it releases the memory. The phrase appears even in some
fascia-research reviews as an informal descriptor, and it has no established meaning. There
is no mechanism by which connective tissue encodes autobiographical content, and no evidence
that manipulating it retrieves any. That people cry during deep bodywork is well attested.
That the tissue was storing the reason is not.

**Confirming that fascia is where needles act would not confirm the traditional theory.**
This is the intellectual honesty the topic demands. If acupuncture works through
connective-tissue mechanotransduction, then the traditional map turns out to have been a
remarkably good empirical guide to where the mechanically responsive tissue planes are --
which is genuinely impressive. It does not mean qi circulates, and it does not validate the
diagnostic categories built on that premise. A correct map does not vindicate a wrong
explanation of the terrain.

**"Fascial release" is not the same as fascia research.** Whether manual pressure durably
changes fascial structure is contested -- the tissue's tensile strength is high, and the
forces a therapist's hands can apply are limited. Sensory and neurological effects are a
more plausible route than mechanical deformation. Anyone describing themselves as
"breaking up adhesions" is asserting more than the evidence supports.

**And it does not cure anything.** This is a mechanism story, not a treatment.

## What this means in practice

If you are choosing between modalities, the fascia literature does two useful things.

It gives you a way to hear traditional language without either swallowing it or dismissing
it. When a practitioner says a meridian is blocked, you can reasonably translate: there is
a plausible physical substrate here, described in a system that lacked microscopes, and
modern anatomy has found something real in roughly the same place.

And it points toward the practices that load connective tissue systematically rather than
passively. Slow weight-bearing movement, sustained positional loading, and the deliberate
tension-and-release patterns of qigong and tai chi are all mechanical inputs to the fascial
network. That is not a claim that they work *because* of fascia. It is a note that the
mechanism, if it holds, is not exotic.

## Who tends to find this useful

People who want to explore traditional practice but cannot get past the metaphysics -- which
describes a large share of the professionals this journal is written for. The fascia
literature is the most credible available bridge, because it was built by anatomists
publishing in anatomy journals rather than by advocates.

It is also useful for anyone with persistent low back pain that imaging has failed to
explain. The disc-displacement finding above is worth raising with a clinician.

## How to evaluate what you are told

When a practitioner invokes fascia, three questions separate the informed from the
fashionable.

**Can they name a researcher?** Langevin is the obvious answer, and someone who has read the
work will say so.

**Do they distinguish mechanism from treatment?** "Needling creates mechanical tension in
connective tissue, and there is research on what that does to fibroblasts" is accurate.
"I release the fascia holding your trauma" is not.

**Do they say what is unknown?** Fascia is currently the most fashionable word in bodywork,
and fashion outruns evidence. The honest version of this topic includes the sentence *we do
not yet know*.
