In April 2015, the Cochrane Collaboration -- the most conservative and most respected
producer of evidence reviews in medicine -- published its assessment of Reiki for
anxiety and depression. The conclusion ran to a single deflating sentence: there is
insufficient evidence to say whether or not Reiki is useful for people over sixteen
with anxiety, depression or both.

Seven years later, a systematic review in *Frontiers in Psychology* looked at fourteen
randomized placebo-controlled trials and reached a strikingly different conclusion --
that Reiki consistently outperformed placebo for some mental health symptoms, with the
evidence graded high for clinically relevant stress and depression.

Both reviews are competently done. Neither is lying. Understanding why they disagree
tells you more about what Reiki is than any confident answer would.

## What it actually is

Reiki is a Japanese practice developed by Mikao Usui in the 1920s, in which a
practitioner places their hands lightly on -- or a few inches above -- a clothed
recipient's body in a series of positions, for roughly an hour. The stated framework is
the channeling of universal life energy through the practitioner to the recipient.

Note the date. Reiki is often described as an ancient tradition, and you will see
"2,500 years old" repeated in the literature, including in the Cochrane review's own
plain-language summary of how proponents describe it. The practice as taught today is
about a century old. That is not a criticism of the practice, but a fact-first
publication should get it right.

Practically speaking, what a recipient experiences is an hour of lying still, in quiet,
receiving sustained non-invasive attention from another person. Hold that description
in mind -- it becomes important below.

## What the research shows

Start with the pessimistic reading. The [Cochrane review by Joyce and Herbison](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25835541/)
found only three trials that met its inclusion criteria. The risk of bias across them
was rated unclear or high in most domains, and the results could only be reported
narratively rather than pooled. On that basis the reviewers declined to draw any
conclusion at all -- and Cochrane's refusal to conclude is itself a finding worth
respecting.

Now the optimistic reading. The [2022 review in *Frontiers in Psychology*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.897312/full)
cast a wider net -- fourteen randomized placebo-controlled trials, adults with or
without a formal diagnosis, any Reiki dose -- and applied GRADE to the results. It
reported high-grade evidence for clinically relevant stress and depression, moderate to
high for clinically relevant anxiety, and low to moderate for burnout and for ordinary,
non-clinical levels of stress. An earlier and narrower analysis by
[McManus in 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/2156587217728644) had argued along similar
lines from placebo-controlled studies only.

A [2024 meta-analysis in *BMC Palliative Care*](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12904-024-01439-x)
pooled 824 participants across anxiety trials and reported a significant effect on
anxiety, with a standardized mean difference of -0.82 -- a large figure by
conventional benchmarks, and one that should be read alongside the small total sample
and the mixed quality of the constituent studies.

So why the disagreement? Three reasons, all mundane. The reviews included different
trials. Cochrane restricted itself to diagnosed anxiety and depression while the later
reviews included healthy and subclinical participants, where effects are easier to
demonstrate. And Cochrane applies a deliberately unforgiving standard to small,
single-site, unblinded studies -- which is most of this field.

The honest position: there is a real and repeatedly observed signal,
generated almost entirely by small studies with methodological limitations, and the
field's most rigorous reviewer is not yet convinced. That is a genuinely interesting
place for a practice to be. It is not the same as proof, and it is not nothing.

## What it does not do

**There is no measured energy field.** No physical mechanism for Reiki has been
demonstrated, and no instrument has detected the transfer it describes. Studies that
claim to have measured "biophoton emission" during energy work are exploratory and
have not been replicated in a way that would support the claim.

**It does not treat disease.** Nothing in this literature supports Reiki as a treatment
for cancer, infection, or any organic condition. Where it appears in hospital settings
it is offered as supportive care alongside treatment, for distress and comfort, and
that is the only defensible framing.

**The blinding problem may be unsolvable.** Sham Reiki involves an untrained person
mimicking the hand positions. Whether a recipient can reliably be kept unaware, and
whether a mimicked hour of attentive presence is actually an inert control, are both
open questions -- and they cut in both directions.

**And the most likely explanation is the least mystical one.** An hour of stillness,
quiet, warmth and undivided human attention is a meaningful intervention for a person
under chronic strain. If that turns out to be the entire mechanism, the effect on the
person receiving it is not thereby diminished. It just means we should describe it
accurately.

## What a session is like

You stay fully clothed and lie on a padded table, usually with a blanket. The room is
warm and dim. Music is common, sometimes annoyingly so -- say if you would rather have
silence.

The practitioner moves through a sequence of hand positions, beginning at the head and
working down, holding each for two to five minutes. Contact is either light or absent
entirely. There is almost no conversation. Sessions run 50 to 90 minutes.

People report a range of sensations: warmth spreading from the practitioner's hands,
heaviness in the limbs, occasional tearfulness, and very frequently sleep. Some people
feel nothing at all, which practitioners will often interpret as significant. It is
also possible that nothing happened.

Expect $80-$150 per session in California. Reiki is unlicensed in the state, which
matters for the section below.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often seek Reiki when they are depleted rather than ill -- during burnout, after
a bereavement, through the exhausting middle stretch of cancer treatment, or in periods
of sustained anxiety where they want support that asks nothing of them. Its particular
appeal is that it requires no effort, no talking, and no belief.

It suits people for whom being touched gently and left alone is restorative. It suits
people badly if they need something to *do*, or if paying for an hour of unexplained
stillness will itself become a source of irritation.

## How to choose a practitioner

Reiki is not licensed or regulated in California, so credentials carry less
information than in acupuncture or therapy. What to look for instead:

- **Level and lineage.** Practitioners are trained through three levels, the third
  being Master. Ask who trained them and when. A named teacher and a traceable lineage
  is the minimum.
- **Insurance and a clear scope statement.** Professional practitioners carry liability
  insurance and can tell you in one sentence what they do not do.
- **How they describe outcomes.** "People often find this reduces their sense of stress"
  is the language of someone paying attention. "This will clear the blockage causing
  your illness" is not.

Two hard red flags. Anyone who suggests delaying or declining medical treatment should
be left immediately. And anyone offering distance Reiki as equivalent to in-person work
is making a claim well beyond anything in the literature -- the trials reviewed above
tested hands-on or near-body treatment, not remote intention.
