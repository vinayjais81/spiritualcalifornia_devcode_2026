There is a category error running through most conversations about music and wellbeing, and
clearing it up is most of what this article does.

Listening to a calming playlist is not music therapy. Neither is a sound bath, nor a
meditation track, nor a nurse putting the radio on in a ward. Music therapy is a
credentialed clinical profession requiring a bachelor's or master's degree, a supervised
clinical internship, and a national board certification -- and it involves a therapist
working with you, usually with you *making* the music rather than receiving it.

That distinction matters because the clinical version has one of the strongest evidence bases
of anything on this website.

## What it actually is

A board-certified music therapist assesses a client and designs a course of sessions using
music toward specific, non-musical goals: reduced depressive symptoms, improved
communication, motor rehabilitation, emotional expression, orientation in dementia.

The work divides into two broad approaches. **Active** music therapy has the client playing,
improvising, drumming or singing -- often with no musical training and no expectation of skill.
**Receptive** music therapy involves guided listening, discussion of what the music evoked,
and structured use of recorded pieces. Most therapists use both.

The essential feature, and the one that separates it from everything adjacent, is the
therapeutic relationship. Research reviews distinguish this from "music medicine" -- listening
to pre-recorded music without a therapist -- and, as we will see, the two produce different
results.

You do not need to be musical. This is the most common reason people rule themselves out, and
it is a misunderstanding of the work.

## What the research shows

**The Cochrane review is the anchor.** Aalbers and colleagues assessed music therapy for
depression and found moderate-quality evidence of large effects favouring music therapy added
to treatment as usual over treatment as usual alone --
[SMD −0.98 for clinician-rated depressive symptoms and SMD −0.85 for patient-reported symptoms](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD004517_music-therapy-depression).

An effect size approaching 1.0 is large by any conventional standard, and it comes attached to
Cochrane's moderate-quality rating rather than the "low or very low" that accompanies most
complementary interventions in this journal. Music therapy was also superior to usual care for
anxiety and for functioning, not only for mood.

**The wider literature agrees on direction and adds nuance.** A meta-analysis of 55
randomized controlled trials found music therapy significantly reduced depressive symptoms
([SMD −0.66](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7673528/)), and -- interestingly --
found music *medicine*, meaning listening without a therapist, produced a larger pooled effect
of SMD −1.33. That result cuts against the simple story and the authors flag it; it likely
reflects differences in the trials pooled rather than a genuine superiority of listening
alone, but it is in the literature and should be reported.

**Cochrane has reviewed music therapy across multiple conditions**, with the strongest signals
in depression, autism spectrum conditions, dementia, schizophrenia and end-of-life care --
a striking breadth for a single modality. And an evaluation summarizing the depression review
for family physicians concluded that music therapy is a
[low-cost, low-risk intervention that may be worth adding to standard care](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2020/0301/p273),
which is roughly the clinical bottom line.

**Where the evidence stands, with one honest caveat:** only one included study evaluated effects over a
period as long as six months. The evidence for immediate and short-term benefit is strong. The
evidence for durability is thin, and that gap is real.

## What it does not do

**It does not replace treatment for depression.** Every finding above is for music therapy
*added to* usual care. The comparison was never music therapy instead of medication or
psychotherapy, and no one should read it that way.

**Durability is not established.** See above. If you complete a course and feel better, that
is what the evidence predicts. What happens at twelve months has barely been studied.

**It is not the same as listening to music, therapeutically framed.** Music medicine and music
therapy are distinct interventions with distinct literatures. When a spa or wellness centre
describes a listening session as music therapy, that is a misuse of a protected professional
term.

**And specific frequency claims are not part of this field.** Nothing in the clinical
literature supports 432 Hz tuning, healing frequencies or tonal correspondences to organs.
Those belong to sound healing, a separate practice with considerably weaker evidence -- our
sound bath article covers it.

Music therapy is very safe. The realistic considerations are volume for anyone with tinnitus
or hyperacusis, and the fact that music reaches emotional material quickly, which can be
intense. A trained therapist expects this and is equipped for it; that is much of what the
credential is for.

## What a session is like

The first session is an assessment and mostly conversation: what you are dealing with, what
music has meant in your life, what you listen to, whether you have ever played anything.

After that it varies more than most modalities. You might be handed a drum or a xylophone and
asked to play whatever comes while the therapist plays alongside -- a musical conversation,
which sounds excruciating for about ninety seconds and then usually stops being. You might
choose recorded pieces and talk about what they surfaced. In grief work, people often bring
music connected to the person they lost. In dementia care, familiar songs from a person's
teens and twenties are used, because that material remains accessible long after much else has
gone.

Sessions run 30 to 60 minutes, individually or in groups, typically weekly across eight to
twenty sessions.

In California, expect $80-$150 per individual session privately. Music therapy is
increasingly available inside hospitals, hospices, schools and rehabilitation settings, often
covered, and in those settings it may be free -- worth asking about before paying privately.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often come to music therapy for depression and anxiety, and the evidence supports that
directly. It is also widely used in dementia care, in autism-related communication work, in
stroke and brain injury rehabilitation, in end-of-life care, and with children and adolescents
for whom talking is not the natural route.

It suits people who find verbal therapy effortful or exhausting -- who have said the same
things in the same words for years and are looking for a different door into the same room. It
also suits people at the other end: those for whom music is already the most emotionally
direct thing in their lives.

It suits less well anyone expecting music lessons, or anyone who wants a purely receptive,
low-engagement experience -- for which a sound bath is the more honest match, on weaker
evidence.

## How to choose a practitioner

This is the clearest credentialing picture in the whole journal, and you should use it.

**Look for MT-BC** -- Music Therapist, Board Certified, awarded by the Certification Board for
Music Therapists. It requires an approved degree programme, roughly 1,200 hours of supervised
clinical training, and a national examination, with ongoing recertification. You can verify a
certification directly with the board.

**In California, also check for a state licence where applicable.** California established a
licensure pathway for music therapists, so ask what they hold.

**Ask about their clinical population.** Music therapy in dementia care and music therapy in
adolescent mental health are genuinely different practices. Someone experienced with your
situation matters more than someone experienced generally.

**Ask about goals and duration.** A therapist should be able to describe what they are working
toward in non-musical terms, and when they would reassess.

**One clear line.** If someone offers "music therapy" without the MT-BC credential, they are
offering something else -- which may be pleasant and valuable, but it is not the thing the
Cochrane review studied, and it should not be priced or promoted as though it were.
