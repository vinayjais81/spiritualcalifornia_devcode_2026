Here is a puzzle worth walking through, because it teaches you how to read this entire field.

A 2020 meta-analysis of foot reflexology pooled 26 randomized controlled trials covering
2,366 participants and reported effect sizes that would be extraordinary for any
intervention: Hedges' *g* of −0.92 for depression, −1.24 for anxiety, −1.67 for sleep
quality. For comparison, antidepressants against placebo typically land somewhere around
0.3.

A 2024 evidence map of the same field assessed reflexology across a range of health
outcomes and rated the confidence in those findings as: four high, four moderate, three
low, and twenty-three critically low.

Both of those are accurate summaries of the reflexology literature. Understanding how they
coexist is more useful than either one alone.

## What it actually is

Reflexology is the application of firm, systematic thumb and finger pressure to specific
zones of the feet -- sometimes the hands or ears -- according to a map on which each zone
corresponds to an organ, gland or body region. Pressing the arch is held to affect the
digestive organs; the base of the toes, the head and sinuses.

The map, in its modern form, dates from the early twentieth century: the zone therapy of
William Fitzgerald and the reflexology charts developed by Eunice Ingham in the 1930s. It
does not correspond to any known anatomical or neurological projection. There is no nerve
pathway, vascular route or fascial connection linking the medial arch specifically to the
pancreas.

That absence matters for interpreting the research, and it is why this article is organized
the way it is. Reflexology consists of two separable claims: that firm structured foot
pressure does something, and that *where* you press determines *what* it does.

## What the research shows

**The reported effects are large and consistent.** The
[2020 meta-analysis and meta-regression of 26 RCTs](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7512096/)
found significant improvements in depression, anxiety and sleep quality, and its
meta-regression found that greater total reflexology time and longer duration significantly
improved sleep quality. A separate
[meta-analysis of 13 randomized trials in 1,189 pregnant women](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10781947/)
reported reduced anxiety and pain, shortened labour stages, increased birth satisfaction, and
reduced pulse and respiratory rate -- though not blood pressure. A systematic review in
multiple sclerosis found reductions in pain, anxiety, stress and depression in single-blind
placebo-controlled trials.

**The confidence in those effects is mostly very low.** The
[reflexology evidence map](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2212958824000909)
is the single most useful document in this field: 23 of 34 assessed outcomes rated
critically low confidence. And the long-running independent assessment reaches the same
place -- Ernst and colleagues' systematic review of randomized clinical trials, and its
update, concluded that
[scientific evidence of high methodological quality was insufficient to support positive effects of foot reflexology](https://www.scielo.br/j/brjp/a/njx6pgYGnvNthbwjqTDGhyH/?lang=en).

A review of reflexology in breast cancer symptom management found the pattern that recurs
throughout: improvements in quality of life but not depression or anxiety, benefit for
fatigue, nausea and vomiting but not peripheral neuropathy, and insufficient high-level
evidence to confirm effectiveness overall. Positive findings scattered across outcomes,
without a coherent pattern.

**And then there is the finding that speaks directly to the map.** A randomized clinical
trial compared foot reflexology against *hand* reflexology in patients awaiting colonoscopy,
and found that
[both reduced anxiety, with closely comparable improvements in systolic blood pressure and heart rate](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2451865425000237)
-- 123.5 and 122.7 mmHg respectively, against 132.1 in controls.

Two entirely different maps, applied to two entirely different body parts, producing
effectively the same result. That is what you would expect if the active ingredient were
twenty minutes of firm attentive pressure, and not what you would expect if the specific
correspondence mattered.

**Where the evidence stands.** Something real happens -- probably relaxation, autonomic shift and the effects of
sustained skilled touch. The specific claim on which reflexology is built is not supported,
and the trials reporting the largest benefits are the ones the evidence map rates lowest.

Why are the effect sizes so implausibly large? Because in small unblinded trials of a
pleasant hands-on intervention against a waiting list, with self-reported outcomes, effect
sizes inflate. This is a systematic feature of the literature, not a criticism of any
individual study.

## What it does not do

**It does not act on your organs through your feet.** No mechanism has been demonstrated and
none is anatomically plausible. A reflexologist who tells you your liver zone is congested is
describing a chart, not your liver.

**It is not diagnostic.** Reflexology cannot detect disease, and tenderness in a zone
indicates tenderness in that part of your foot. Where this becomes actively harmful is when a
zone finding substitutes for a medical investigation.

**It has not been shown to treat any medical condition.** Reflexology research is
overwhelmingly about symptoms -- anxiety, sleep, pain, fatigue -- in people who are already
under medical care. That is the honest scope.

**And the specific map appears not to matter**, per the foot-versus-hand comparison. If you
enjoy the work, that finding should not stop you. It should stop you paying a premium for
somebody's proprietary chart.

Reflexology is very safe. Reported adverse events are minimal. Caution is warranted with
active foot infections, recent foot or ankle injury, deep vein thrombosis, and in advanced
diabetes with peripheral neuropathy, where sensation is impaired and pressure tolerance
cannot be judged reliably.

## What a session is like

You stay clothed apart from shoes and socks, in a reclining chair or on a table. Your feet
are cleaned, and then worked systematically -- usually the whole of one foot before the other
-- with firm thumb pressure walking across each region.

The pressure is considerably stronger than most people expect. Certain spots can be
genuinely sharp, and practitioners will typically pause and mention what the chart says that
spot corresponds to. Take the sensation seriously and the interpretation lightly.

Sessions run 45 to 60 minutes. The characteristic experience is deep drowsiness -- many
people sleep -- and a warmth spreading up the legs. Afterwards people frequently report the
kind of specific, uncomplicated relaxation that is easy to underrate.

Expect $60-$120 in California. Reflexology is often available in nail salons and spa
settings at lower prices; the training there varies from substantial to none.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often seek reflexology for anxiety, poor sleep, and general depletion, and
particularly in situations where more invasive bodywork is unwelcome -- during pregnancy,
during cancer treatment, in advanced illness, or for anyone who does not want to undress and
be touched across the whole body. Both the pregnancy and the supportive-care literature are
aimed at exactly those uses, and it is a real and decent niche.

It suits people who want an hour of pleasant, low-risk, attentive touch and are relaxed about
the theory. It suits poorly anyone who wants organ-specific treatment, and anyone tempted to
use a zone reading in place of a diagnosis.

Our honest framing: reflexology is a good foot treatment with an implausible explanation
attached. If you would value the first part, the second need not deter you -- so long as you
are not being sold the second.

## How to choose a practitioner

Reflexology is not licensed in California. The available signals:

- **Certification through a recognized body** -- the American Reflexology Certification
  Board (ARCB) is the main national credential, and it verifies training hours and testing.
  Ask whether they hold it.
- **Whether they hold another qualification.** A CMT or nurse who also practises reflexology
  brings scope awareness that a spa technician may not.
- **How they talk about the map.** The question to ask is: *what do you think is actually
  happening when you press that point?* A thoughtful practitioner will say something about
  relaxation, nerve endings and the density of sensory receptors in the foot, and be
  comfortable saying the mechanism is not settled. That answer is a good sign.

Three things that should end it. Anyone who claims to detect a disease or an organ problem
from your feet. Anyone who advises you against or instead of medical investigation. And
anyone selling a package on the basis that reflexology treats a diagnosed condition -- the
evidence does not support that, and the evidence map explains why the studies that seem to are
the least reliable ones.
