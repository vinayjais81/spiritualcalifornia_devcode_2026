People who have done a long silent retreat tend to describe the same arc, and they
describe it unprompted.

Day one is logistics and relief. Day two the mind is loud and the body hurts and the
schedule feels absurd. Day three is when something gives -- and what gives is not always
pleasant. Many people report day three as the point at which grief they had filed away
arrives unannounced, or boredom becomes intolerable, or they seriously plan their
departure. Then, for most, it passes, and the second half of the retreat is quieter in
every sense.

The research has caught up with parts of this. It has also produced one number that
retreat marketing consistently omits.

## What it actually is

A silent retreat means several days to several weeks of residential practice with speech
suspended, eye contact often discouraged, and phones surrendered. The dominant form in the
West is Vipassana in the Goenka tradition -- ten days, taught by donation, with a fixed
schedule of roughly ten hours of seated meditation daily. Zen *sesshin*, Tibetan retreats
and secular insight retreats follow comparable shapes.

The daily load is the part people underestimate. In studies of month-long Vipassana
retreats, the mean duration of daily practice was eight to nine hours, with a vegetarian
diet and compulsory silence. This is not a holiday with an early bedtime. It is closer to
a full-time job at which you are not permitted to speak.

What makes it different from daily practice is not technique -- often the technique is
simpler. It is the absence of the exits. No conversation, no reading, no screen, no
errand. Whatever the mind produces, you stay in the room with it.

## What the research shows

A systematic review and meta-analysis of traditional meditation retreats in the *Journal
of Psychosomatic Research* found that retreats were
[moderately to largely effective for symptoms of stress, anxiety and depression](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022399916305049)
in healthy populations -- and the authors were notably candid about needing to address
their own expectancy in conducting the review.

The durability question has been tested. A study of 195 participants at a leading retreat
centre measured mindfulness, anxiety, depression, dysfunctional attitudes, emotion
regulation and attention before a pre-retreat control period, immediately after a one-week
retreat, and four weeks later. Significant improvements in mindfulness, anxiety,
depression and dysfunctional attitudes were maintained at follow-up -- a design that is
stronger than most, because the pre-retreat control period helps rule out simple
anticipation effects.

Longer retreats have been studied in experienced practitioners. A controlled
non-randomized trial of a
[one-month Vipassana retreat](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5149565/)
compared 19 retreatants against 19 matched meditators who stayed home, and examined
whether psychological change was mediated by non-attachment.

There is also research on the intense experiences themselves -- the day-three phenomena.
A preregistered prospective study of 96 adults attending six-day insight retreats, with 47
matched controls, characterized seventeen distinct peak meditative experiences and found
them
[primarily pleasant, with a large beneficial impact afterwards](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38573713/).
That finding runs against the more alarmist framing and deserves equal billing.

**Now the number.** Farias and colleagues systematically reviewed adverse events across
meditation practices and meditation-based therapies in *Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica*,
and found an
[overall prevalence of 8.3% -- comparable to what is reported for psychotherapy in general](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/acps.13225).
They also concluded there is no standard method for assessing adverse events in this
literature and that randomized trials are likely under-reporting them.

Read that carefully, because it cuts both ways. Roughly one in twelve is not a scandal --
it is an ordinary rate for an active psychological intervention, and it is the honest
price of something that does anything at all. The problem is that psychotherapy discloses
its risks and retreat centres largely do not.

Related work found unpleasant meditation-related experiences were more common in
non-religious participants, in those with higher levels of repetitive negative thinking,
and in those practising deconstructive forms such as insight meditation -- which is
precisely what a ten-day Vipassana retreat consists of.

**Where the evidence stands.** Consistent, moderate-to-large effects, but from small samples, non-randomized
designs and self-selected populations. People who choose ten days of silence are not
comparable to people who do not.

## What it does not do

**It is not therapy, and it is not supervised care.** There is no clinician on site at
most retreats, teachers are not trained to manage psychiatric crisis, and the structure
that produces the benefit -- silence, no exits, long hours -- is the same structure that
makes distress harder to detect.

**It does not reliably fix anything you brought with you.** The maintained gains in the
studies above are in symptom and attitude measures at four weeks, not resolutions of life
circumstances.

**And intensive retreat conditions carry documented risk of destabilization.** The case
literature on meditation-induced psychosis names retreat conditions specifically --
fasting, sleep restriction, isolation -- alongside pre-existing vulnerability and absence
of individualized screening. If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder,
dissociation or recent severe trauma, a long silent retreat is a decision to make with a
clinician, not a booking to make online.

The version worth avoiding entirely is the retreat that treats difficulty as evidence of
progress and discourages leaving.

## What a retreat is like

Wake around four. Sit. Eat in silence, slowly, looking at your food. Sit. Walk slowly
around a marked path. Sit. A talk in the evening from the teacher, which after two days of
silence lands with disproportionate force. Sleep early.

The physical reality dominates the first days: knees, hips, lower back. Most people
underestimate this and most centres provide more cushions and chairs than newcomers
expect. Ask.

The mental reality arrives later. The frequently reported day-three feature is that
without conversation, screens or tasks, the mind stops producing content it can distract
itself with, and starts producing whatever is actually there. That is the entire mechanism
and it is also the entire risk.

Goenka Vipassana courses are taught by donation, which removes cost as a barrier and is
genuinely admirable. Other residential retreats in California run $600-$2,500 for a week
including accommodation. Ten days is the standard entry point for Vipassana; three to five
days is a more reasonable first attempt elsewhere.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often go when the problem is noise rather than a symptom -- when the practical
capacity to hear themselves think has gone. It also draws experienced daily practitioners
who have reached the limits of twenty minutes a day.

It suits people who are stable, prepared for physical discomfort, and not going in order
to fix a crisis. It suits poorly anyone in acute distress, anyone recently bereaved, and
anyone whose main motivation is to escape a situation that will be waiting unchanged.

## How to choose a retreat

Four questions, and the answers are diagnostic.

**Do you screen applicants, and for what?** A good centre asks about psychiatric history
and current medication before accepting you.

**What is your protocol if a participant becomes psychologically distressed?** Look for a
named person, a break from the schedule, permission to speak, and an external referral
route.

**Can I leave?** The answer should be an immediate yes. Any hesitation, or any framing of
leaving as failure, is disqualifying.

**Who is the teacher and how long have they taught?** Retreat teaching is not credentialed
in any consistent way, so lineage and years are the available signals.

Start shorter than you think you need to. A weekend tells you a great deal about whether
ten days is a good idea, and almost nobody regrets that order.
