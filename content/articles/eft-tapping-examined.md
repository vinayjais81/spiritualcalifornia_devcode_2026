EFT is unusual among the practices in this journal, because it is not one thing. It is a
bundle, and the bundle contains something well evidenced and something contested, delivered
together in a single fifteen-minute procedure.

The well-evidenced part is borrowed openly from cognitive behavioural therapy and prolonged
exposure: awareness building, imaginal exposure, cognitive reframing, systematic
desensitization. The contested part is the tapping.

The field has spent a decade trying to determine whether the tapping does anything, and the
story of that attempt is more instructive than any single result.

## What it actually is

In Emotional Freedom Techniques -- popularly "tapping" -- you identify a specific distressing
memory, feeling or symptom, rate its intensity, and state a setup phrase that names the problem
while affirming self-acceptance. Then you tap with your fingertips through a fixed sequence of
points on the face, collarbone and hand, repeating a reminder phrase. You re-rate the
intensity, and repeat until it drops.

The points are drawn from acupressure, and the traditional explanation involves disrupting a
disturbance in the body's energy system. Clinical EFT has a standardized manual and a
credentialing structure.

Structurally, note what is happening in that procedure independent of the tapping: you are
holding a distressing memory in mind, repeatedly, without avoidance, while doing something
regulating with your hands and breath, and tracking a numeric intensity rating that gives
immediate feedback. That is imaginal exposure with self-monitoring -- a recognizable and
effective clinical technique.

## What the research shows

**On the whole protocol, the evidence is reasonably good.** Meta-analyses report large effect
sizes for PTSD, depression and anxiety. The honest framing comes from the EFT researchers
themselves, who acknowledged in print that
[treatment effects may be due to components EFT shares with other therapies](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/jonmd/abstract/10.1097/nmd.0000000000000878~is-tapping-on-acupuncture-points-an-active-ingredient-in?redirectionsource=fulltextview).

That is the right question, and the way to answer it is a **dismantling study**: run the full
protocol against an identical protocol with the tapping replaced by something inert -- sham
points, diaphragmatic breathing, mindful attunement.

**Here is where it gets complicated, and this sequence is worth following carefully.**

In 2018, Church and colleagues published a systematic review and meta-analysis of six such
comparative studies in the *Journal of Nervous and Mental Disease*, concluding that the
acupressure component was an active ingredient.

Subsequently, as the authors themselves recorded in a published corrigendum,
[errors in the statistical analysis were identified -- primarily incorrect standard deviation values -- and the methodological approach was questioned by others](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/jonmd/abstract/10.1097/nmd.0000000000001222~corrigendum-to-is-tapping-on-acupuncture-points-an-active?redirectionsource=fulltextview).
They had an independent senior statistician rerun the analysis comparing pre-treatment to
follow-up, obtaining Hedges' g of 0.73 to 0.74, and concluded that the original finding stood.

The critics were not satisfied, and their response is the most important document in this
whole literature. In a reply titled
[*Corrigendum Compounds Errors and Again Fails to Support the Specificity of Acupoint Tapping*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35080521/),
they reported that their own analysis using corrected effect sizes found **no significant
benefit for acupoint tapping at study endpoint** -- and objected that the corrigendum had
shifted to a post hoc outcome, using follow-up rather than endpoint measures while pooling
highly disparate follow-up points, and had set aside the non-significant result of the
pre-specified analysis.

**Where that leaves things.** Proponents continue to argue, in reviews published largely by
the same small group of researchers, that
[dismantling studies show tapping to be an active ingredient rather than a placebo](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1660375/full).
Critics maintain that the pre-specified analyses do not support it. Both sides publish; the
field has not converged.

**Where the evidence stands.** The full protocol produces real improvements, most plausibly through its
exposure and cognitive components. Whether tapping on acupoints contributes anything beyond
that is genuinely unresolved, and the meta-analysis most often cited to settle it is disputed
on its face.

## What it does not do

**It does not clear energy blockages or disruptions in a body energy system.** No such system
has been detected. Our Reiki and healing touch articles cover the blinded tests of that broader
claim.

**It has not been shown that the specific points matter.** Two sham-point studies favoured
real points; the pooled analysis of that question is contested. If the points were doing what
the theory says, this would be the easiest thing in the world to demonstrate cleanly, and after
a decade it has not been.

**It is not a substitute for trauma treatment.** Trauma-focused CBT, prolonged exposure,
cognitive processing therapy and EMDR carry far larger evidence bases and guideline
recommendations.

**And a note on the literature itself.** A striking proportion of EFT research -- including the
meta-analyses, the mechanism reviews and the rebuttals to critics -- is produced by a small
group of authors closely identified with the method. That does not make the findings wrong. It
does mean independent replication carries more weight than volume of publication, and the
independent work is thin.

**Safety.** EFT is physically harmless. The real risk is the same as with any self-administered
exposure technique: bringing up traumatic material alone, without support, can destabilize
people. It is also frequently marketed for serious conditions by people with no clinical
training.

## What a session is like

A practitioner-led session runs 45 to 60 minutes. You describe the issue, and together you make
it specific -- not "my anxiety" but a particular moment, image or bodily sensation. You rate the
distress from 0 to 10.

Then the setup phrase, which most people find awkward for the first minute or two: *even
though I feel this, I deeply and completely accept myself.* Then the tapping sequence, eight or
nine points, with a short reminder phrase at each.

You re-rate. Usually the number has dropped. You go round again, often finding that a different
aspect has surfaced.

What people report is a fairly rapid drop in the felt charge of a specific memory, and
frequently surprise at how mechanical the process feels relative to the effect. Sessions can
be emotional.

Expect $80-$180 per session in California. EFT is also widely self-administered from free
videos, which is worth knowing: the technique is simple, and the cost of trying it yourself on
something mild is essentially zero.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often use it for specific, circumscribed distress -- a phobia, a performance situation,
a particular memory that still stings, cravings -- rather than for diffuse or long-standing
difficulty. Its usability is a genuine advantage: it is portable, brief and self-administrable,
which is more than can be said for most things in this journal.

It suits people who can tolerate feeling slightly ridiculous and who like a concrete
procedure with a number attached. It suits poorly anyone with significant trauma working
alone, and anyone who needs the theoretical explanation to be true in order to participate.

Our honest framing: EFT is most defensible as a self-administered exposure and self-soothing
technique with an unproven somatic add-on. Judged that way, it is cheap, low-risk, and worth
a try on something small. Judged as an energy medicine, the claims outrun the evidence
considerably.

## How to choose a practitioner

**Strongly prefer a licensed mental health professional who also uses EFT** -- an LMFT, LCSW,
psychologist or counsellor. This matters more here than almost anywhere else in the journal,
because EFT is frequently used on trauma by people with no clinical training whatsoever.

**Look for EFT International or Clinical EFT certification** as a secondary signal, and ask
what their primary qualification is.

**Ask what they treat and what they refer out.** Naming mental health conditions specifically
is the answer you want.

**And listen to how they explain it.** "This combines exposure and cognitive techniques with
acupressure, and the research on whether the tapping itself adds anything is contested" is an
accurate and genuinely reassuring answer. "This clears the energy disruption causing your
problem" is a claim the evidence does not support.

**Two hard stops.** Anyone offering EFT as treatment for a physical disease. And anyone
proposing to work through serious trauma with tapping alone and no clinical qualification.
