Before the sound bath, before the breathwork, before the practitioner directory: have you had
any water today?

This is not a rhetorical device. Controlled experiments have induced mild dehydration -- around
1 to 2% of body mass, which is less than most people lose across a busy morning -- and measured
what happens to mood, vigilance and concentration. The answer is that both get measurably
worse, and that drinking water reverses it.

It is the least interesting practice in this journal. It is also the one most likely to be
relevant to a reader on the specific afternoon they go looking for something to fix how they
feel.

## What it actually is

Dehydration in this research means a modest loss of total body water, typically induced by
exercise, by fluid restriction, or by a diuretic. Not the dramatic dehydration of heat illness
-- a level most office workers reach routinely without noticing.

The relevant physiology is unremarkable. Body water loss affects blood volume, cerebral
perfusion and neurotransmitter function, and the brain is sensitive to relatively small
changes. Thirst is a reasonably good regulator, but it lags, and it is easily overridden by
work.

## What the research shows

**The two anchor studies were run in the same laboratory with the same protocol, one in men and
one in women.**

Ganio and colleagues studied 26 young men across
[three randomized, single-blind, repeated-measures trials](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-nutrition/article/mild-dehydration-impairs-cognitive-performance-and-mood-of-men/3388AB36B8DF73E844C9AD19271A75BF):
exercise-induced dehydration plus a diuretic, exercise-induced dehydration plus placebo, and
exercise while maintaining hydration. Deliberately, body water loss was induced by mild exercise
in a mild environment without hyperthermia, so that dehydration was isolated as the variable.
Mood and vigilance were impaired.

Armstrong and colleagues ran the equivalent study in healthy young women. The combined finding,
as summarized by the investigators, is that in both sexes mild dehydration
[adversely affected mood and the ability to concentrate, with increased fatigue and diminished vigilance](https://www.hydrationforhealth.com/en/hydration-science/emerging-science/list-emerging-science-article/laurence-e-armstrong-2012-matthew-s-ganio-2011/).

**The sex difference is the most interesting result and is rarely reported.** At an almost
identical level of dehydration -- around 1.39% of body weight -- produced by the same exercise
regimen in the same laboratory,
[cognitive performance in women was not substantially altered](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-nutrition/article/mild-dehydration-impairs-cognitive-performance-and-mood-of-men/3388AB36B8DF73E844C9AD19271A75BF),
while mood was. Women were more likely to report headache, fatigue, confusion and lack of
energy, and to perceive moderate exercise as harder. Men showed more short-term memory
degradation.

**The threshold appears to be low.** A mediator analysis in the *American Journal of Clinical
Nutrition* found that drinking water after a loss of just
[0.72% of body mass prevented declines in memory, attention and mood](https://ajcn.nutrition.org/article/S0002-9165(22)04582-8/fulltext),
and noted separate work in which a 1% loss increased driving errors -- a finding later extended
by research on driver errors during prolonged monotonous driving.

**And the literature is not unanimous.** A randomized controlled trial in China notes that
[other studies have found no negative cognitive effects from dehydration](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8539979/),
and that results in this area have been inconsistent, confounded by the exercise used to induce
dehydration and by heat stress. One study of 24-hour fluid deprivation in young men found no
mood effects at all.

**One disclosure this journal will not skip.** Several of the key studies above include authors
affiliated with a bottled water company's research division, and the most convenient summaries
of this literature are published on a platform run by that industry. The trials themselves are
well designed -- randomized, single-blind, with a genuine control condition -- and the finding
is independently plausible. But you should know who has an interest in it, and the same standard
we applied to grounding applies here.

**Where the evidence stands.** Consistent, replicated effects on mood and vigilance at low levels of water loss,
from small well-controlled studies, with real inconsistency across the wider literature and a
partial industry funding footprint.

## What it does not do

**It does not treat depression or anxiety.** Dehydration worsens mood in healthy people. That is
a different claim from hydration improving a clinical condition.

**Eight glasses a day is not an evidence-based number.** It has no clear origin in the research
literature. Requirements vary substantially with body size, activity, climate and diet -- and a
meaningful share of intake comes from food.

**More is not better.** Overhydration is a real if uncommon problem, and drinking far beyond
thirst can dilute blood sodium dangerously. This matters mainly for endurance athletes and for
people following aggressive intake rules.

**And expensive water is not better water.** Alkaline, structured, hydrogen-rich and
mineral-enhanced waters are marketed extensively in this category and none of them have
demonstrated advantages over tap water for any of the outcomes above.

## What to actually do

- **Drink when thirsty, and drink before you are.** Thirst lags behind need.
- **Front-load the morning.** Most people wake mildly dehydrated after seven or eight hours
  without fluid, which is precisely when they start their most cognitively demanding work.
- **Attach it to something.** A glass with each coffee is the simplest rule that survives a busy
  day.
- **Watch the specific situations that catch people out:** back-to-back meetings, air travel,
  hot Bay Area inland afternoons, and any day organized so tightly that leaving the desk feels
  impossible.
- **Use urine colour as a rough check** -- pale straw rather than dark. It is crude, free, and
  more reliable than how you feel.

Cost: nothing. Tap water in California is regulated and safe in the great majority of the state.

## Who this is for

Everyone, which is why it is here. But specifically: the reader who has arrived at this website
in the late afternoon, feeling foggy, irritable, headachy and unable to concentrate, and is
considering booking something.

Those symptoms -- fatigue, difficulty concentrating, headache, low mood, reduced vigilance --
are precisely the symptom cluster that mild dehydration produces in controlled trials. They are
also the symptom cluster that most of the modalities on this site are sought for.

We are not suggesting that a glass of water substitutes for the rest of this journal. We are
suggesting it is the cheapest thing to rule out first, it takes ninety seconds, and if it works
you have saved yourself a considerable amount of money and time.

## Where this fits

This article exists partly as a corrective to the shape of a wellness marketplace. When
someone feels persistently unwell, the sequence that serves them best runs from the cheapest
and most reversible explanations to the most expensive and most elaborate -- water, sleep,
daylight, movement, food, then everything else.

A site that sells sessions has an obvious incentive to skip the first four. This one would
rather not.

And if you have ruled those out and still feel wrong, that is genuinely useful information --
it is a reason to see a doctor before booking a practitioner, because persistent fatigue,
headache and cognitive fog have medical explanations that no modality on this site addresses.
