In 1996, a nine-year-old in Loveland, Colorado watched a video of nurses describing how they
could feel a human energy field with their hands. One said it felt as warm as Jell-O. Another
said it was as tactile as taffy. What struck Emily Rosa was how certain they all sounded.

For a fourth-grade science fair project, she built a cardboard screen and ran a test.
Practitioners put their hands through two holes. She flipped a coin, held her own hand a few
inches above one of theirs, and asked which one they could feel.

Two years later the results were published in *JAMA*, making her the youngest person ever to
author a paper in a peer-reviewed medical journal. Whatever you conclude about the practice,
that is a remarkable piece of history.

## What it actually is

**Therapeutic Touch** (TT) was developed in the 1970s by Dolores Krieger, a nursing professor
at New York University, and Dora Kunz, a theosophist. **Healing Touch** (HT) emerged from
nursing in the late 1980s as a related but distinct system with its own certification. Both
are biofield therapies: the practitioner's hands move over -- usually not on -- a clothed
patient, assessing and then modifying a field held to surround the body.

The distinguishing feature of both, and the reason they matter more than their evidence base
alone would suggest, is institutional. These are nursing practices. They are taught in
nursing programs, credentialed through nursing bodies, and delivered inside hospitals -- pain
clinics, oncology units, surgical settings, hospice. When people say energy healing has
entered mainstream medicine, this is usually what they mean.

## What the research shows

**The foundational claim was tested and failed.** Rosa and colleagues tested 21 practitioners
with between one and 27 years of experience, under blinded conditions, to see whether they
could correctly identify which of their hands was nearest the investigator's. As reported in
[*JAMA*](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/187390),
they could not. The authors' conclusion was blunt: the failure to substantiate the practice's
most fundamental claim was, in their words, unrefuted evidence that its claims are groundless.

In fairness, the study drew substantial published criticism. *JAMA* ran multiple letters
under the heading "An even closer look at therapeutic touch", raising objections about
protocol, sample and interpretation. Those critiques exist and readers should know they do.
What has not happened in the nearly thirty years since is the obvious remedy: practitioners
demonstrating the ability under equally controlled conditions. The test is cheap and simple.
It has not been passed.

**The clinical literature is more sympathetic and much weaker.** Anderson and Taylor's
systematic review of randomized clinical trials of Healing Touch, published in the *Journal
of Holistic Nursing*, noted that the practice is used in pain centres, surgical settings and
private practice with
[reported benefits including decreased anxiety, pain and depressive behaviours](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21228402/),
and set out to evaluate that trial data systematically -- the first such assessment.

The broadest synthesis comes from Hammerschlag and colleagues, who reviewed randomized
controlled trials of biofield therapies delivered with *no physical contact* -- an important
restriction, because light touch itself reduces stress and pain, which confounds every trial
that permits it. Of 74 RCTs identified, 23 met inclusion criteria across 18 conditions, and
[19 of the 23 (83%) reported at least one outcome favouring the real treatment](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3373869/).

Read that carefully, because it is easy to over-read. "At least one outcome favoured" across
studies measuring many outcomes each is a low bar, and the authors themselves framed the
review as developing evaluative criteria appropriate to the early-phase, pilot-study nature of
most biofield trials. They also work at institutions dedicated to complementary medicine,
which is disclosed and worth knowing.

**Where the evidence stands, and only just.** A consistent pattern of positive findings from small early-phase
trials; a failed test of the central mechanism that has never been answered.

## What it does not do

**No human energy field has been detected.** Not by practitioners under blinded conditions,
not by instruments. This is the whole load-bearing claim, and it is unsupported.

**Practitioners cannot assess or diagnose you by scanning.** Whatever a hand-scan produces, it
is not information about your body.

**It does not treat disease.** Where these practices appear in hospitals, they are supportive
care -- for anxiety, distress and comfort during treatment -- and every responsible programme
describes them that way.

**And the honest alternative explanation is not dismissive.** A trained nurse spends thirty to
sixty minutes with a frightened patient, in a quiet room, giving them unhurried, focused,
non-clinical attention with no needle and no bad news attached. In a hospital, that is a
scarce and genuinely valuable thing. If that is the entire mechanism, it does not make the
half hour worthless. It means we should describe it accurately and, arguably, fund more of it.

Physical risk is essentially nil. The risk is substitution, and in hospital settings that risk
is well managed by the surrounding care.

## What a session is like

You sit in a chair or lie on a bed, fully clothed. The practitioner centres themselves
briefly, then moves their hands slowly over your body a few inches above the surface, from
head to feet. They may pause in places. They may make sweeping motions away from the body,
described as clearing.

Contact is minimal or absent. Sessions run 20 to 60 minutes, shorter in hospital settings.

What people report is warmth from the practitioner's hands, heaviness, drowsiness, and a
distinctive sense of being attended to. Many people cry, particularly patients in the middle
of difficult treatment. In hospitals, sessions frequently take place with the patient in bed,
mid-treatment, and the reported value is often about the interruption as much as anything.

In hospital or hospice programmes it is generally free as part of integrative or palliative
services. Privately, expect $60-$120 in California.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often encounter these practices while already unwell -- during chemotherapy, before
surgery, in palliative care -- rather than seeking them out in ordinary life. That context is
the most defensible use, and the one the research population reflects.

It suits people who want quiet, non-invasive, undemanding contact during a hard period. It
suits poorly anyone looking for treatment of an underlying condition, or anyone who would be
irritated by an explanatory framework they do not accept.

Our honest framing: if you are offered Healing Touch in a hospital, take it. It is free,
harmless, and half an hour of calm attention in an environment short on both. Just understand
what you are receiving.

## How to choose a practitioner

Unlike most modalities on this site, credentialing here is relatively orderly.

**Healing Touch** practitioners are certified through Healing Beyond Borders as **HTCP**
(Healing Touch Certified Practitioner), a multi-level programme. **Therapeutic Touch**
practitioners are qualified through Therapeutic Touch International Association. Ask which
they hold.

**Prefer a practitioner who is also a licensed clinician** -- most commonly an RN. That
underlying licence brings scope awareness, an obligation to refer, and accountability that a
certificate alone does not.

**Ask what they claim.** A nurse who says patients often find it calming and that it sits
alongside their medical care is describing the practice accurately. Someone offering to
diagnose your condition by scanning your field, or to treat it, is not.

**One firm line.** Anyone suggesting that this can substitute for medical treatment, or that
your illness stems from an energetic imbalance they can correct, should be declined and
reported to the programme that placed them.
