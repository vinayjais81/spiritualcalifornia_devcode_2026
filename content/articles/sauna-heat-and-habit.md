In 1984, researchers in Eastern Finland began enrolling middle-aged men into the Kuopio
Ischemic Heart Disease Risk Factor Study. Among many other questions, they asked how often the
men used a sauna.

Then they waited two decades.

What came out of that patience is the largest and longest body of evidence for any heat-based
practice anywhere, and it is genuinely striking. It is also observational, culturally specific,
and carries a confounding problem that nobody has yet solved.

## What it actually is

Finnish sauna means short whole-body exposure to hot dry air, typically 80°C to 100°C at 10-20%
humidity, in sessions of five to twenty minutes, often repeated with cooling breaks between.

Physiologically, heat exposure raises core temperature, increases heart rate to levels
comparable with moderate exercise, dilates peripheral blood vessels, and produces sweating and
plasma volume changes. The proposed mechanisms for long-term benefit include improved vascular
function, reduced blood pressure, and repeated mild heat stress producing adaptations similar
in some respects to those from exercise.

Infrared saunas are a different intervention -- lower ambient temperature, different heating
mechanism -- and almost none of the evidence below applies to them.

## What the research shows

**The anchor study.** Laukkanen and colleagues followed 2,315 men aged 42 to 60 for a median of
20.7 years, grouped by sauna frequency: once weekly (601 men), two to three times (1,513), and
four to seven times (201). Over that period there were
[190 sudden cardiac deaths, 281 fatal coronary events, 407 fatal cardiovascular events and 929 deaths from all causes](https://www.gasteiner-heilstollen.com/fileadmin/user_upload/PDFs/Wissenschaftliche-Unterlagen/2015_Association-Between-Sauna-Bathing-and-Fatal-Cardiovascular-and-All-Cause-Mortality-Events.pdf).

Compared with once-weekly bathers, men using the sauna two to three times a week had a 22%
lower risk of sudden cardiac death, and those using it four to seven times a week had a
[63% lower risk, with the pattern holding after adjustment for baseline cardiovascular risk factors](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/journal-scans/2015/03/12/15/58/association-between-sauna-bathing-and-fatal-cardiovascular).
Cardiovascular mortality was roughly halved in the most frequent group.

**A later cohort included women.** A 2018 analysis followed 1,688 men and women, mean age 63,
just over half of them women, for a median of 15 years. The frequency-mortality relationship
was dose-dependent with no apparent threshold: four to seven sessions weekly carried a hazard
ratio of 0.23 (95% CI 0.08-0.65) for cardiovascular death compared with once weekly. The two to
three times group sat at 0.75 (95% CI 0.52-1.08) -- an association that lost statistical
significance after full adjustment.

**The same cohort has produced a run of related findings**: reduced incident hypertension,
reduced stroke risk in men and women, and an inverse association with dementia and Alzheimer's
disease in middle-aged Finnish men. Further analyses found that
[frequent sauna bathing appeared to offset the elevated all-cause mortality risk associated with high C-reactive protein](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9792415/)
across 2,575 men followed for a median of 27.8 years, and a similar interaction with elevated
systolic blood pressure.

**And there is now randomized evidence, though on intermediate outcomes.** A trial of eight
weeks of unsupervised Finnish sauna bathing in adults with coronary artery disease examined
[markers of vascular health](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/japplphysiol.00322.2023).
That is the right direction of travel for this field, and it measures vascular markers rather
than deaths.

## Why this isn't as strong as it looks

The effect sizes are the problem as much as the attraction. A hazard ratio of 0.23 -- a 77%
reduction -- is larger than most pharmaceutical interventions achieve, from a behaviour that
involves sitting in a hot room. When an observational finding is that large, the responsible
first question is what else differs between the groups.

**Three specific concerns.**

**Reverse causation.** People who are already ill sauna less. Someone with unstable angina,
severe COPD or advanced frailty is not taking a 90°C sauna five times a week. Some of the
mortality difference may reflect who is well enough to sauna rather than what sauna does.
Analyses attempt to address this, but it cannot be fully removed observationally.

**Confounding by everything else.** In this cohort, frequent sauna use plausibly tracks with
income, home ownership, social integration, leisure time and general health behaviour. The
models adjust for known risk factors, physical activity and socioeconomic status. They cannot
adjust for what was not measured.

**Group size and setting.** Only 201 men were in the highest-frequency group in the 2015
analysis. And this is Finland, where sauna is a near-universal domestic habit embedded in
family and social life -- not a $60 wellness purchase. Whether the association transfers to a
different cultural context is untested.

None of this makes the finding wrong. Consistency across outcomes -- cardiac death, stroke,
hypertension, dementia -- and a clean dose-response relationship are exactly what you would
want to see if the effect were real. But observational data of this kind establishes a strong
case for a trial, not a conclusion.

## What it does not do

**It does not detoxify you.** Sweat is overwhelmingly water and electrolytes. The liver and
kidneys handle detoxification, and no study has shown meaningful elimination of toxins through
sweating.

**It is not established as a treatment for anything.** These are associations in a healthy
population, plus early randomized work on vascular markers.

**Infrared saunas are not the intervention studied.** They operate at much lower temperatures
and the Finnish evidence does not transfer.

**And it is not a substitute for exercise**, notwithstanding a great deal of marketing to that
effect. The cohort adjusted for physical activity precisely because the two are not
interchangeable.

**Safety.** Sauna is well tolerated by most people, but heat stress is real. Avoid alcohol
entirely -- the combination is implicated in a meaningful share of sauna-related deaths in
Finland. Talk to a doctor first if you have unstable angina, recent myocardial infarction,
severe aortic stenosis, orthostatic hypotension, or are pregnant. Hydrate, get out if you feel
unwell rather than pushing through, and stand up slowly.

## What a session is like

Five to twenty minutes in the heat, then out to cool -- shower, cool air, or in Finland
frequently a lake -- then back in. Two or three rounds is standard. Total time including
cooling: 45 to 90 minutes.

The first few minutes feel pleasant, then genuinely hot, then the sweating starts properly and
the heat becomes easier to sit with. Heart rate climbs to something resembling a brisk walk.
The cooling round is the part people underestimate and often the part they come to prefer.

Afterwards there is a characteristic heavy, emptied-out calm and, for most people, notably
better sleep that night.

In the Bay Area, sessions at bathhouses and gyms run $30-$60; a home sauna is a several-thousand
dollar purchase. Given a dose-response relationship with no clear threshold, frequency is what
the evidence points to -- which is an argument for whatever option you will actually use four
times a week rather than the best one you use monthly.

## Who tends to find it useful

People often come to sauna for relaxation and sleep and stay for the ritual. The cardiovascular
findings are a reason to make it habitual rather than occasional, but they are not why anyone
starts.

It suits people who can build it into a routine -- after the gym, a fixed evening, a weekend
morning. It suits poorly anyone with the cardiac conditions listed above without medical advice,
and anyone who will treat it as a substitute for exercise.

Our honest framing: this is the largest and longest observational evidence base for anything in
this journal, pointing consistently in one direction, and it remains observational. If you enjoy
it, the case for doing it often is good. If you dislike it, nothing here justifies enduring it.

## How to choose a facility

**Look for a traditional Finnish-style sauna** if you want the studied intervention -- dry, 80°C
or above, with the option to add water to the stones. Infrared is a different product.

**Check ventilation and cleanliness**, unglamorously, and whether the temperature is actually
what the sign says.

**Ask about cooling facilities.** The contrast round is part of the traditional practice, and a
facility with only a warm shower is offering half of it.

**And be sceptical of the claims on the wall.** Detoxification, immune boosting, weight loss and
"burning 600 calories a session" are marketing. The real case for sauna is a twenty-year Finnish
cohort, which is a considerably better story than any of them.
