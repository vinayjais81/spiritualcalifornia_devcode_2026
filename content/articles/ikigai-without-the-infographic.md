You have seen the diagram. Four overlapping circles: what you love, what you are good at, what
the world needs, and what you can be paid for. At the centre, in the small petal where all four
meet, the word *ikigai*.

It has been on a million slide decks since about 2014. It is elegant, it is memorable, and
according to the Japanese writers who introduced the word to English-speaking audiences, it is
largely an invention -- a diagram about purpose in general, adapted and then retrofitted onto a
Japanese word that does not mean that.

The underlying research is real and rather more interesting. It is also, in one specific
respect, contradicted by the diagram that made it famous.

## What it actually is

*Ikigai* (生き甲斐) combines *iki*, life, with *kai*, an old word for shell that came in classical
Japanese to carry a sense of worth or value. The usual English gloss is a reason to get up in
the morning, and even that flattens it.

In Japanese usage it is ordinary rather than grand. Ikigai can be a grandchild, a garden, a
weekly meeting with friends, a craft practised badly, the fish you keep. It is domestic and
frequently small. Japanese researchers studying it ask a plain question -- do you have ikigai in
your life? -- and treat the answer as a self-report about whether daily life feels worth
meeting.

Notice what is absent from that: any requirement that the thing be your work, be valued by the
world, or generate income.

## What the research shows

**The Ohsaki Study is the anchor.** Sone and colleagues followed 43,391 Japanese adults aged 40
to 79 in Miyagi Prefecture for seven years, asking that single question at baseline and tracking
mortality. Those who did not report a sense of ikigai had a
[multivariate-adjusted hazard ratio for all-cause mortality of 1.5 (95% CI 1.3-1.7)](https://pure.fujita-hu.ac.jp/en/publications/sense-of-life-worth-living-ikigai-and-mortality-in-japan-ohsaki-s/)
compared with those who did. Adjustments included age, sex, marital status, education, BMI and
medical history.

**The cause-specific breakdown is where it gets genuinely interesting.** The elevated risk
among those without ikigai was significant for cardiovascular disease (HR 1.6, 95% CI 1.3-2.0)
and for external causes -- accidents and suicide -- (HR 1.9, 95% CI 1.1-3.3). For cancer
mortality, the association
[was not significant (HR 1.3, 95% CI 1.0-1.6)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Sense-of-Life-Worth-Living-(Ikigai)-and-Mortality-Sone-Nakaya/bffe858c706dc4298b8e1e947e2721bb580a6367).

That pattern is a small piece of evidence in itself. Having a reason to get up appears to track
with the heart and with the decision to keep going, and not with cellular disease. It is the
shape you would expect if the mechanism ran through stress physiology, health behaviour and
will to live rather than through anything oncological.

Related work in the much larger Japan Collaborative Cohort Study has examined ikigai against
cardiovascular mortality in a similar direction, and a validated instrument -- the Ikigai-9 --
now exists for measuring it more finely than a yes/no question allows.

**Where the evidence stands, and the limitation is design rather than size.** These are large, well-conducted
prospective cohort studies. They are also observational, which means the direction of causation
is genuinely open: it is entirely plausible that people who are already healthier, better
connected and less depressed are more likely to report having ikigai, rather than the reverse.
Reverse causation is partly addressed in these analyses by excluding early deaths, but not
eliminated. And there are no intervention studies -- nobody has randomized anyone to acquire a
sense of purpose.

## What it does not do

**It has not been shown that cultivating ikigai extends life.** This is the crucial gap.
Observing that people with ikigai live longer is not the same as showing that acquiring it
would have helped anyone. The studies cannot support the inference the wellness industry makes
from them.

**The four-circle diagram is not the research construct.** Nothing in the Japanese literature
requires ikigai to be something the world needs, something you excel at, or something anyone
would pay for.

**And the money circle actively contradicts it.** The Ohsaki respondents included retired
people, homemakers, and the very old. Their ikigai was frequently a person, a routine or a
plant. Requiring that a reason for living also be economically productive is a Western
importation into a concept that specifically does not demand it -- and it converts a gentle
idea into another performance target for people who already have too many.

The Okinawan elders who became the public face of this concept do not describe it the way
career-coaching material does.

**One more caution.** Purpose research generally has a dark edge worth naming: framing meaning
as an individual achievement can imply that people without it have failed at something.
Isolation, illness, poverty and grief all make ikigai harder to report, and none of them are
character defects.

## How to think about it, practically

If you want to use this idea rather than merely admire it, the research points somewhere quite
different from the diagram.

**Start from the actual question.** Not *what is my purpose* but *is there something that makes
tomorrow morning worth meeting*. That is a lower bar and a more answerable one.

**Look at the small and the recurring.** The things that show up as ikigai in Japanese survey
research are unremarkable: a person you look after, a craft, a routine, a place, an animal, a
weekly obligation to someone else.

**Do not require it to be your job.** If it happens to be, fine. The evidence does not ask for
it, and organizing your identity around work being your reason for living is not what these
cohorts were describing.

**Notice that most of them involve other people.** The mortality pattern -- cardiovascular and
external causes, not cancer -- is consistent with what the social connection literature would
predict, and connection recurs throughout the qualitative descriptions.

## Who tends to find this useful

People often reach for ikigai at exactly the point where the diagram is least useful: a
successful career, competence established, income secure, and a persistent sense that the
question of what it is all for has gone unanswered for several years.

For that reader, our honest suggestion is to abandon the four circles entirely. They will send
you looking for a vocation, which you already have, and which is not the problem. The research
construct is asking something quieter and more domestic, and for people whose lives are already
organized around achievement, the quieter question is usually the one that has gone unasked.

It suits people willing to accept a small answer. It suits poorly anyone looking for a single
grand purpose -- which the literature does not describe and which the diagram invented.

## Where this connects

If the mechanism here runs through social connection and having a reason that involves other
people, then the practices in this journal most likely to help are the ones that put you
repeatedly in a room with the same humans -- group drumming, a weekly class, a community of
practice -- rather than anything done alone.

That is a hypothesis, not a finding. But it is the direction the cause-specific mortality
pattern points, and it is more actionable than a Venn diagram.
