The work is good. Someone else described it in the meeting. Your name was not on the thing that
went up, and when you raised it, it was fixed cheerfully and immediately, which somehow made it
worse.

## Do this now

**Before any conversation with anyone, twenty minutes on paper:**

1. **Write what specifically happened.** Dates, projects, who said what. Not "I'm not appreciated"
   — the events.
2. **Then answer this honestly: what do I actually want?** The options are genuinely different and
   people conflate them constantly:
   - **Credit** — my name attached to work I did
   - **Money** — a raise, a band change
   - **Advancement** — a title, scope, a path
   - **Being valued** — to be told the work matters
   - **Different work** — recognition is not the issue; the job is
3. **Pick one.** They require different conversations and asking for all of them at once produces
   none of them.
4. **Write the ask as one sentence.** *"I'd like to lead the next phase"* or *"I'd like my name on
   the deliverables I produce"* or *"I'd like to discuss my band at the next review."*
5. **Ask for a meeting, not a moment.** Not in the corridor, not at the end of a standup.

## If that doesn't fit

**If none of the five options fit:** that is worth sitting with. Sometimes the honest answer is that
you want someone to notice without being asked — which is real, and unfortunately not something
anyone can grant on request.

**If the ask feels too large:** ask for the smallest version. Credit on one deliverable is a
conversation. "I want to be valued" is not.

## Why naming it first

Most of these conversations fail for a mundane reason: the person having them has not decided what
they want, so it comes out as complaint. Complaints get sympathy. Requests get answers.

The move — from *how am I regarded* to *what am I asking for* — is the same one that underpins
values-based work in [ACT](/clinic/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy), and it is more tractable
because it has a verifiable answer.

**On what structured help with this actually delivers**, the coaching literature is worth knowing
accurately. Theeboom's meta-analysis found an overall effect of *g* = 0.66, with the strongest
result on **goal-directed self-regulation** at
[*g* = 0.74](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17439760.2013.837499).

Two caveats we cover in full in our [life coaching article](/journal/life-coaching-evidence).
That evidence is predominantly about **workplace coaching**, not general life coaching — a later
analysis noted only a handful of the included studies were life coaching. And **more sessions did
not improve outcomes**; studies with fewer than five reported higher effects on some measures.

If you use a coach for this, a short engagement is the defensible structure.

## The uncomfortable possibilities

Three, and they are worth considering before the meeting rather than after:

**The work may genuinely be less visible than it is good.** Some roles produce outcomes that are
invisible when done well. That is not your fault and it is also not something anyone will correct
without being told.

**The recognition may be going somewhere else deliberately.** If a specific person consistently
presents your work, that is a distinct problem with a distinct answer, and it needs documenting.

**Or the job may be wrong.** If the honest answer to *what do I want* is "different work," then
recognition was never the issue, and our article on
[being competent and bored](/what-to-do/competent-and-bored) is closer to the point.

## When this isn't enough

If you have asked clearly, more than once, and nothing has changed, you have your answer — and it
is information about the organisation rather than about you.

**Worth escalating properly** if the pattern maps onto a protected characteristic, or if credit is
being systematically taken. That is a documentation and HR matter, not a self-management one.

**And worth a GP conversation if** feeling unseen at work has become feeling worthless generally,
or is affecting sleep and mood outside work. Where the persistent theme is *there is something
wrong with me* rather than *this situation is unfair*, our article on
[compassion-focused therapy](/clinic/compassion-focused-therapy) is the relevant one — that
distinction between shame and circumstance matters, and it changes what helps.
