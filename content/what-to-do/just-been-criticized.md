It might have been fair. It might even have been kind. Your face is warm anyway and you have
stopped hearing the rest of the sentence.

## Do this now

**In the room, one thing only:**

1. **Ask a clarifying question.** *"Can you give me a specific example?"* or *"What would it look
   like done well?"*

That is it. Not a defence, not an explanation, not agreement. A question does three things at
once: it buys you thirty seconds, it converts a judgment into something actionable, and it is the
only response that never looks bad in retrospect.

**Then, afterwards:**

2. **Go outside for ten minutes.** Do not go straight to your desk and do not go straight to a
   colleague.
3. **Write down what was actually said** — the words, as close as you can get them. Not what you
   heard. There is often a gap.
4. **Wait 24 hours before deciding what you think of it.**
5. **Then separate it into two lists:** what is true and useful, and what is not. Most feedback is
   a mixture and treating it as one thing is why it lands so hard.

## If that doesn't fit

**If you are about to cry:** that is a physiological response and not a professional failing. The
clarifying question buys enough time to end the conversation gracefully — *"Let me think about
this and come back to you."* Then leave.

**If the criticism was public:** the 24-hour rule matters more, not less. Public correction
produces a stronger urge to respond immediately and a worse response.

## Why the delay

Nothing on this page has a randomized trial behind it, and we would rather say so than dress it
up. What we can point to is the mechanism it is built on.

Acute stress narrows attention and biases interpretation toward threat. In that state you are not
evaluating the feedback — you are defending against it — and the thing you write down an hour
later will be a more accurate record than the version you are replaying now.

**The more useful observation is about what you replay.** Most people, asked a week later, cannot
recall the substance of the criticism but can recall exactly how it felt. If the feeling that
persists is *there is something wrong with me* rather than *I did that badly*, that is shame
rather than guilt — and the distinction is clinically meaningful.

[Compassion-focused therapy](/clinic/compassion-focused-therapy) was developed for precisely that
problem: people who can complete every cognitive exercise correctly and still not believe the
output, because the voice generating it remains hostile. If criticism reliably produces a
disproportionate collapse, that is the article worth reading.

**And if it stays with you for days**, the four-day expressive writing protocol is a reasonable
low-cost experiment — with the caveat that people usually feel worse immediately after writing,
with benefit appearing over weeks. Our
[article on it](/journal/expressive-writing-pennebaker) covers the honest effect size.

## When this isn't enough

**Feedback that is a pattern is a different problem.** If you are being criticized repeatedly by
the same person in ways others are not, or the criticism is personal rather than about work, that
is a workplace issue rather than a self-management one — and it is worth documenting dates and
wording as they happen.

If a single piece of routine feedback derails a week, or you have started avoiding situations
where you might be evaluated, that is worth a conversation with a therapist. It responds well to
treatment, and it is a common reason capable people plateau.
