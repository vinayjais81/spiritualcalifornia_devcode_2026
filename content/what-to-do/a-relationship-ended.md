The practical arrangements are the least of it. What has actually gone is the shape of your days,
the person you told things to, and the version of the future you had stopped noticing you were
counting on.

## Do this now, this week

1. **Fix a wake time and keep it.** Every day. This is load-bearing in a way that will not feel
   like it.
2. **Get outside within an hour of waking**, fifteen minutes. Sleep timing goes first after a
   separation and daylight is the strongest correction available.
3. **Eat at roughly normal times**, even badly. The eating tends to go before you notice it has.
4. **Schedule three specific things a day** — with times attached, deliberately small. At least one
   that is not about the relationship or the logistics.
5. **See one person, in person, this week.** Not to discuss it. Just to be somewhere with someone.
6. **Decide what happens with contact** — messages, social media, shared spaces — and write the
   decision down. Deciding once is much easier than deciding forty times a day.

## If that doesn't fit

**If three things is too many:** one. With a time.

**If you cannot face anyone:** keep the wake time and the daylight anyway. Those two survive weeks
where nothing else does.

## Why structure before understanding

The urge is to work out what happened. That question is real and it does not answer well in week
two, on no sleep, from a standing start.

The alternative is to work from the outside in. [Behavioural
activation](/clinic/behavioural-activation) rests on the observation that low mood narrows
behaviour, the narrowing reduces reward and contact, and the reduction deepens the low mood. You
break the loop at the behaviour.

The evidence for it is unusually good for something this plain. The COBRA trial randomized 440
adults with major depression to behavioural activation delivered by *junior* mental health workers
or CBT delivered by trained therapists. At twelve months both groups finished at
[8.4 points on the PHQ-9 — a difference of 0.1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27461440/).

**The scheduling is the intervention.** Not preparation for feeling better.

## On writing about it — and the warning

The four-day expressive writing protocol is one of the few things with any evidence behind it for
this specific situation: fifteen to twenty minutes, four consecutive days, writing continuously
about the relationship and how you feel about it. Nobody reads it. Destroy it afterwards if that
helps you write honestly.

**Three things you should know before you do it.**

**The effect is small.** It shrank as it was studied properly — from *d* = 0.47 in an early
meta-analysis of 14 studies to *d* = 0.075 across 146 studies, with Pennebaker himself now
reporting around
[*d* = 0.16](https://cssh.northeastern.edu/pandemic-teaching-initiative/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2020/10/Pennebaker-Expressive-Writing-in-Psychological-Science.pdf).

**Most people feel worse straight afterwards** — sadder, more stirred up — with benefit appearing
over subsequent weeks. Do not do it before bed and do not judge it on day one.

**And the mechanism appears to be narrative, not venting.** The moderator research points toward
writing that builds structure, sequence and perspective over the four days rather than writing
that only discharges feeling. Circling the same distress repeatedly may entrench rumination rather
than relieve it — which is the specific risk after a breakup, when the mind is already looping.

If four days of writing turns into four days of re-running the same argument, stop.

## The thing that takes longest

Most people find the practical loss resolves faster than the **loss of the person you talked to**.
The instinct to tell them things persists long after you have stopped wanting them back, and it
catches people off guard because it does not feel like missing them — it feels like an unfinished
sentence.

That is ordinary and it fades. Telling someone else instead, deliberately and repeatedly, is what
replaces it.

## When to talk to someone

Feeling wrecked for weeks after a significant relationship ends is not a disorder. Sleep
disruption, poor appetite, difficulty concentrating and waves of distress are the ordinary shape
of it.

**Worth a GP conversation if**, after several weeks to a couple of months: you have stopped
enjoying anything at all, you are not functioning at work, you are drinking substantially more, you
cannot get out of bed most days, or the low mood is constant rather than fluctuating.

[Interpersonal therapy](/clinic/interpersonal-therapy) is worth knowing about specifically here —
it is guideline-recommended for depression, and "role transition" is one of its four defined
problem areas, which is precisely what this is.

**And two firm things.** If you are having thoughts of harming yourself, contact your doctor, a
crisis line, or emergency services today. Relationship breakdown is a recognised risk period, and
that is a reason to have support rather than to get through it alone.

If the relationship involved violence, coercion or fear, leaving is the highest-risk period and
this page is not the right resource. Contact a domestic violence service — in the US, the National
Domestic Violence Hotline operates 24 hours.
