The room, the pitch, the first day, the panel. And underneath it the specific conviction that
you have been mistaken for someone competent and today is when it gets noticed.

## Do this now

**Not affirmations. Do not tell yourself you are brilliant — you will not believe it, and the
attempt usually makes the feeling louder.**

1. **Write one sentence: what am I here to do?** Not *be impressive*. Something concrete —
   *explain the trade-offs clearly so they can decide*, *understand what they actually need*.
2. **Write one sentence: what would doing this well look like, regardless of outcome?** This
   separates the part you control from the part you do not.
3. **Then list three specific things you have actually done** that are relevant. Not qualities —
   events. *Ran the migration. Handled the client in March. Wrote the thing they cited.* Evidence,
   not encouragement.
4. **Breathe out longer than you breathe in**, five minutes, before you go in — not during.
5. **Go.**

## If that doesn't fit

**If you have two minutes:** step one only. The single sentence about what you are there to do
does most of the work.

**If the feeling is physical more than mental** — shaking, nausea, blank mind — treat it as
arousal rather than truth. Our article on
[speaking in public](/what-to-do/about-to-speak-in-public) covers the timing, and the reminder
that calm is not the goal.

## Why values rather than affirmations

Affirmations fail for a specific and well-described reason: **if you do not believe the statement,
saying it invites the counter-argument.** Telling yourself you are excellent when you feel like a
fraud produces an internal rebuttal, and the rebuttal is more persuasive than the affirmation
because it comes with examples.

The alternative is to shift from *what am I* to *what am I doing here* — which is a question with
an answer you can actually verify.

That move is the core of [acceptance and commitment
therapy](/clinic/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy), whose central construct is
**values-based action**: taking steps in a direction you care about while the uncomfortable
thoughts come along, rather than resolving them first. Notably, across GRADE-rated analyses, the
outcome ACT researchers are *most* confident it moves is psychological flexibility itself
([2025 meta-analysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165178125003488)).

And the same construct turned up somewhere unexpected. In a study of 444 people walking the Camino
de Santiago against 124 on ordinary holidays, **valued living** fully mediated the benefit on
stress, negative affect, life satisfaction and happiness
([Project Ultreya](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/353121429_The_effects_of_the_pilgrimage_to_Santiago_de_Compostela_Way_of_Saint_James_on_mental_health_and_wellbeing)).
Two entirely separate literatures, same variable.

**The evidence list in step three is doing something different again.** Impostor feelings run on
attributing success to luck, timing or other people. Writing down specific events you were
actually responsible for is not self-praise — it is a factual correction, and it is harder to
argue with.

## The part worth knowing

Impostor feelings correlate with competence more often than with incompetence, which is the cruel
joke of the whole thing. People who know enough to see the edges of their knowledge feel
uncertain; people who do not, do not.

That observation is genuinely comforting and it is also **not a reason to dismiss the feeling
permanently**. Sometimes the honest answer is that you are underprepared for this specific thing —
in which case the useful response is preparation, not reassurance. The two are distinguishable,
and the question that separates them is: *if a colleague I respect were doing this with my
preparation, would I be worried for them?*

## When this isn't enough

If the feeling is constant rather than situational — if it is there on ordinary days, if you
cannot absorb praise, if success reliably produces anxiety rather than satisfaction — that is
worth more than a pre-meeting exercise.

Where the persistent theme is *there is something wrong with me* rather than *I did that badly*,
that is shame rather than doubt, and it has a specific treatment approach. Our article on
[compassion-focused therapy](/clinic/compassion-focused-therapy) covers it — including the
finding that self-compassion is actively frightening for some people, which is documented,
expected, and not a failure on your part.

And if it comes with low mood, avoidance of opportunities, or having turned down things you wanted
because you felt unqualified, that is worth a conversation with a therapist. It is one of the more
common reasons capable people stop progressing.
