They are asking. You can feel the yes forming. You know you do not have the time, you know you
will resent it by Thursday, and you are going to say yes anyway.

## Do this now

**Do not try to say no. Say this instead:**

> *"Let me check what I've got on and come back to you tomorrow."*

That is the entire technique. Then:

1. **Do not decide today.** The yes lives in the moment; the honest answer usually arrives an hour
   later.
2. **Tomorrow, write the answer down before you send it.** One or two sentences.
3. **Do not explain.** Explanations invite negotiation. *"I can't take this on right now"* is
   complete. Adding *because* opens a discussion about whether your reason is good enough.
4. **Offer an alternative only if you mean it.** "I can't do the whole thing but I could review a
   draft" is generous. "I could maybe do it next month" when you cannot is a delayed yes.
5. **Send it and do not follow up to soften it.** The apologetic second message is where most
   people undo the first.

## If that doesn't fit

**If they need an answer now:** *"Then the answer has to be no, I'm afraid — I can't commit to it
today."* Urgency imposed by someone else is not your emergency to solve.

**If it is your manager and no is not available:** the useful sentence is not no but a trade —
*"I can take this on if we push X, or if Y goes to someone else. Which would you prefer?"* That
converts a capacity problem into a prioritisation decision, which is their job rather than yours.

## Why delay rather than assertiveness

Most advice here tries to make you a person who declines confidently in real time. For a lot of
people that is a large change attempted in a high-pressure moment, and it fails.

Delay is a much smaller ask. It requires one rehearsed sentence and no change in personality, and
it moves the decision to a context where you can think.

**We should be honest that this specific script has no trial behind it.** What is well described is
that acute social pressure narrows options and biases toward the immediately comfortable choice —
and saying yes is almost always immediately more comfortable than saying no.

**Where the evidence does bear on this:** structured goal-directed conversation improves
goal-directed self-regulation, which is the strongest single effect in the coaching literature
(*g* = 0.74 in [Theeboom's meta-analysis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17439760.2013.837499)).
Our [life coaching article](/journal/life-coaching-evidence) covers what that evidence does and
does not support — including that it is largely workplace coaching, and that more sessions did not
predict better outcomes.

## The pattern underneath

If you always say yes, the reason usually is not politeness. The common ones people report:

- **Being needed is load-bearing** for how you see yourself
- **Declining feels like being found out** as less capable or less generous than you appear
- **Conflict avoidance** — the discomfort of no is immediate, the cost of yes is deferred

That third one is worth naming, because it is a straightforward trade you are making badly: you
are paying with Thursday to feel comfortable for the next ninety seconds.

If the second one resonates — declining producing a sense that *there is something wrong with me*
rather than *I have limited capacity* — that is closer to shame than to politeness, and our
[compassion-focused therapy article](/clinic/compassion-focused-therapy) is the relevant one.

## When this isn't enough

If you cannot decline anything, and your week is largely composed of other people's priorities,
that is worth working on properly rather than script by script. Assertiveness is a standard
component of CBT, it is trainable, and it is one of the more satisfying things to change.

**And if the inability to say no is specific to one person** — a manager, a parent, a partner — the
question is about that relationship rather than about your general assertiveness. If saying no to
that person feels unsafe rather than uncomfortable, that distinction matters and is worth raising
with a therapist.
