It arrived. You noticed the absence of the feeling within about a week, and you have been slightly
embarrassed about it since — because people worked hard for less, and you are not entitled to be
unmoved by something you spent years pursuing.

## Do this now

**The instinct is to find the next goal. Resist that for twenty minutes.**

1. **Write down how you spent last week.** Hours, not calendar entries.
2. **Mark each item: does this connect to something you would say matters to you?** Not whether it
   was necessary. Whether it *matters*.
3. **Look at the proportion.** People in this position usually find the marked column much emptier
   than they expected.
4. **Then a different question: what did you think this would give you?** Security, respect,
   permission to relax, proof of something. Name it specifically.
5. **And: did it?** Usually the honest answer is partly, and less durably than expected.
6. **One thing to add this month** that connects to the marked column. Preferably involving other
   people.

## If that doesn't fit

**If the audit feels bleak:** stop at question four. Naming what you thought it would give you is
the useful part, and it can be done in five minutes.

**If the honest answer is that you wanted it for someone else's approval:** that is extremely
common, it is not shameful, and it explains the anticlimax entirely.

## Why alignment rather than the next goal

The research finding worth knowing here comes from an unexpected place.

Researchers followed **444 people walking the Camino de Santiago** against **124 people on ordinary
holidays** — a comparison that controls for time off, travel and rest. The pilgrims did better on
positive affect, life satisfaction, anxiety and stress. Then they tested what the benefit travelled
through.

The answer was **valued living**: the degree to which daily actions align with what a person
considers important. It
[**fully mediated** the effects on perceived stress, negative affect, life satisfaction and
happiness](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/353121429_The_effects_of_the_pilgrimage_to_Santiago_de_Compostela_Way_of_Saint_James_on_mental_health_and_wellbeing).

Not the exercise. Not the scenery. Alignment between action and values.

**Applied here:** achievement and alignment are different things, and pursuing one does not
reliably deliver the other. You can spend a decade getting something and arrive with your days
composed almost entirely of activities that connect to nothing you actually care about — which is
what most people in this position discover in step three.

**The same construct** is what [acceptance and commitment
therapy](/clinic/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy) is most confident about moving. Two separate
literatures, same variable.

**One honest caveat**: the Camino study is observational, participants self-selected, and outcomes
were self-reported. You cannot randomize people to a pilgrimage.

## The trap in "what's next"

The reflex after an anticlimax is to set a bigger goal, on the theory that the last one was too
small. That sometimes works and frequently produces the same result eighteen months later, at
greater cost.

Our [ikigai article](/journal/ikigai-without-the-infographic) covers a related correction. The
famous four-circle diagram — what you love, what you are good at, what the world needs, what you
can be paid for — is largely a Western invention, and the money circle **contradicts** the research
construct. In the Japanese cohort studies, people's stated ikigai was frequently a person, a
garden, a routine, a weekly obligation. Small and domestic.

For someone who has just achieved a large thing and felt nothing, the four circles will send you
looking for a grander vocation, which is not the problem.

## When this isn't enough

**Anticlimax is normal and usually passes within a few months** as ordinary life reasserts itself.

**Worth a GP conversation if:** the flatness has lasted more than a couple of months, extends to
things unrelated to the achievement, comes with disturbed sleep or appetite, or you have stopped
enjoying things you used to.

Anhedonia — the loss of the capacity to enjoy — is a symptom rather than a mood, and it responds
well to treatment. It is also easy to mistake for a philosophical problem, particularly by people
who are used to thinking their way out of things.

**And if this comes with a sense that nothing has been worth it**, or with thoughts of harming
yourself, please contact your doctor or a crisis line. Achievement without relief is a recognised
and under-discussed risk pattern, and it is not something to sit with privately.
