You have almost no time, no reliable sleep, and no obvious end point. Most self-care advice
assumes at least one of those things is different.

This is a twelve-minute practice, done sitting, that was tested specifically in family
caregivers of people with dementia.

## Do this now

1. **Sit comfortably**, spine upright, eyes closed. A chair is fine.
2. **Chant the syllables *Saa Taa Naa Maa*** — repeating, at a steady pace, roughly one syllable
   per second.
3. **Touch a fingertip to your thumb with each syllable.** Index on *Saa*, middle on *Taa*, ring
   on *Naa*, little finger on *Maa*. Both hands.
4. **Change the volume as you go:** two minutes out loud, two minutes in a whisper, four minutes
   silently, then back out — two whispered, two aloud.
5. **Visualize the sound** entering through the top of your head and leaving between your
   eyebrows.
6. **Twelve minutes total.** Then sit for a moment before you get up.

Doing four things at once is the point. There is very little attention left over for anything
else — which is the whole reason it works for people whose minds will not stop.

## If that doesn't fit

**If twelve uninterrupted minutes is not available:** do it in the car before you go in, or after
you leave. Several caregivers describe this as the only reliable private time they have.

**If the syllables feel too strange:** the finger movements and the changing volume are doing much
of the work. Any repeated neutral sound will occupy the same channel.

**If you cannot sit at all:** put on music you loved before any of this started, and listen
without doing anything else. In the trial below, the music group also improved.

## Why it works

Researchers at UCLA randomized family dementia caregivers with depressive symptoms to either
this practice or listening to relaxing music — twelve minutes a day for eight weeks.

The meditation group showed
[reduced depressive symptoms and improved mental health and cognitive function compared with the
music group](https://www.uclahealth.org/news/article/new-understanding-power-yoga).

Two things make this more useful than most wellness research.

**The comparison was fair.** Not a waiting list. The control group also spent twelve minutes a
day deliberately doing something restful — so the result is not simply "taking a break helps."

**And the population was you.** Not students, not volunteers. Stressed family caregivers, which
is a group with very little time and a well-documented burden.

A follow-up randomized trial in adults with subjective memory decline found
[robust enrolment, excellent retention and compliance, and high
satisfaction](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0965229916300279) — meaning
people under strain actually do this one, which is not true of most practices.

**One honest note.** Several of these studies were funded in part by an organization that
promotes this practice, and a founding figure appears as a co-author. The trials are competently
designed and the disclosures are published, but independent replication is thinner than the
number of papers suggests. We would rather tell you that than not.

## When this isn't enough

Twelve minutes a day does not fix caregiving, and nothing on this page pretends otherwise.

**Two things are worth more than any technique**, and both are frequently delayed until crisis:

**Respite care.** Adult day programmes, in-home respite and short-stay residential care exist
specifically so that carers can stop for a few hours. In California, your county's **Area Agency
on Aging** is the starting point, and the Alzheimer's Association helpline operates 24 hours.
Most people wait far too long to ask.

**Your own doctor.** Caregiver depression is common, treatable, and routinely missed because
carers present as tired rather than unwell. If you have stopped sleeping, stopped eating properly,
stopped seeing anyone, or find yourself frightened by your own anger, that is not a character
failure — it is the predictable result of sustained strain, and it is a reason to be seen.

**And support that involves other people matters more than solitary practice.** Caregiver support
groups — in person or online — are among the better-evidenced interventions in this area, and they
address the isolation that a private twelve-minute practice cannot.

If you are having thoughts of harming yourself, or if you are frightened of what you might do
when you are at the end of your patience, contact your doctor or a crisis line today. Both are
more common among carers than anyone says out loud, and both are reasons to get help rather than
to feel ashamed.
