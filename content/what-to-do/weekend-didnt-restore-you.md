Two days. No obligations to speak of. A reasonable amount of sleep, a lot of screen, some
scrolling, a film you cannot now name. And on Monday morning you feel exactly as you did on
Friday.

## Do this now, for next weekend

1. **Keep your wake time roughly constant.** Within an hour of the weekday one. Sleeping until
   eleven on Saturday shifts your clock later, and Sunday night is then spent trying to fall asleep
   at a biological time your body thinks is early evening.
2. **Get outside on both days**, within an hour or two of waking. This is the single most effective
   thing on the list.
3. **Do one thing with other people**, in person. Not a phone call.
4. **Do one thing that requires your hands or your body** rather than your judgment. Cook something
   involved, garden, swim, build, walk somewhere new.
5. **Take one two-hour block with no screen at all.** Not "less screen" — none.
6. **Do not schedule the whole weekend.** One thing per day plus space.

## If that doesn't fit

**If you have caring responsibilities or a second job:** the wake time and the daylight are the two
that survive constrained circumstances. Do those.

**If you genuinely need to sleep in:** cap it at an hour later than usual, and get outside as soon
as you are up.

## Why doing nothing doesn't work

The mistake is treating rest as one thing. What has usually been depleted by a working week is
**directed attention** — the effortful, top-down kind you spend on decisions, screens, meetings and
other people's priorities.

Two days of scrolling does not restore directed attention. It spends it, in smaller denominations.

What appears to restore it is different in kind: **outward attention, novelty, physical engagement
and other people** — the things that ask something of you without asking you to decide anything.

**Where the evidence sits.** Studies comparing meditation retreats against ordinary vacations found
retreat participants doing better on several measures — though both improved, and the retreat
literature is small and self-selected. Our
[article on silent retreats](/journal/silence-retreat-day-three) covers it honestly, including the
8.3% adverse-event rate that retreat centres do not publish.

More directly applicable: the Camino study, where 444 pilgrims were compared against 124 people on
ordinary holidays. The pilgrims did better — and the mediator was **valued living**, the alignment
between daily action and what you consider important
([Project Ultreya](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/353121429_The_effects_of_the_pilgrimage_to_Santiago_de_Compostela_Way_of_Saint_James_on_mental_health_and_wellbeing)).

Not the rest. Not the scenery. What they were doing with the time.

**And the timing part is not soft.** Bright light is the strongest signal your body clock receives,
and a weekend spent indoors with a shifted wake time produces something close to jet lag by Monday.
Our [morning light article](/journal/morning-light-circadian) has the mechanism.

## What to be honest about

**Some weekends are genuinely for lying down**, and a week that has flattened you may need exactly
that. This page is about the weekend that does not work despite being restful — not an argument
that you should be productive.

**And there is a version of this that no weekend fixes.** If Monday arrives no better regardless of
what you do, and has for months, the problem is upstream of the weekend.

## When this isn't enough

If rest reliably fails to restore you, that is the definition of the thing our
[burnout article](/what-to-do/burnt-out-holiday-didnt-fix-it) covers — and the answer there is
about what you are spending your days on rather than how you recover from them.

**Worth ruling out medically**, because all of these present as unrefreshing rest and all are
easily tested: sleep apnoea, thyroid dysfunction, anaemia. Our article on
[sleeping eight hours and still feeling terrible](/what-to-do/slept-eight-hours-still-tired)
covers what to ask for.

**And if the flatness extends to things you used to enjoy** — if the weekend is not restorative
because nothing is any longer enjoyable — that is worth a GP conversation. Anhedonia is a symptom
rather than a lifestyle problem, and it responds well to treatment.
