The information exists. Someone will read it on Thursday. Until then you are carrying a question
you cannot answer, and every hour offers you the chance to research it instead.

We are not going to tell you to stay positive.

## Do this now

**Two rules, set today:**

1. **Decide when you will look things up, and for how long.** Twenty minutes, once a day, at a set
   time. Not before bed.
2. **Outside that window, do not search.** When the urge comes — and it will, repeatedly — note it
   and continue what you were doing. It passes in under a minute.

**Then, structure, because the days lose shape:**

3. **Keep your wake time fixed** and get outside within an hour of waking.
4. **Schedule three specific things a day**, with times. At least one that has nothing to do with
   this.
5. **Tell one person you are waiting**, and what for. Carrying it alone makes the days longer.
6. **Ask when you will hear, and by what route.** "About a week" is unbearable; "the clinic calls
   on Thursday afternoons" is manageable. If nobody has told you, call and ask — this is a
   legitimate question and staff answer it routinely.

## If that doesn't fit

**If you cannot stop searching:** shorten the window rather than abandoning it. Ten minutes, once,
is still a boundary.

**If the nights are the worst part** — and for most people they are — see our article on
[waking at 3am](/what-to-do/awake-at-3am). The body-rotation practice there is well suited to a
mind that will not stop.

## Why limiting the searching

The loop is the same one that runs behind compulsive checking. Uncertainty produces anxiety.
Searching resolves it briefly. The relief teaches you to search again. The next round of
uncertainty arrives faster, and the searching gets worse rather than better.

The way out is the principle behind [exposure and response
prevention](/clinic/erp-for-ocd), the first-line treatment for OCD: allow the uncertainty to be
present and do not perform the act that resolves it. The anxiety rises, then falls on its own.

**We are not saying this is OCD.** Searching for information about your own possible diagnosis is
an entirely rational response to a frightening situation. But the mechanism is the same, and so is
the way out.

**There is also a specific problem with medical searching.** What you find is written for
populations rather than for you, weighted toward severe outcomes because those are what get
written about, and stripped of the context — your history, your specific findings — that determines
what any of it means. The clinician reading your result on Thursday has that context. The internet
does not.

## What actually helps in the meantime

**Doing things that matter to you rather than waiting well.** The construct with the best evidence
here is **values-based action** — taking steps in a direction you care about while the difficult
feelings come along, rather than resolving them first. That is the core of
[acceptance and commitment therapy](/clinic/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy), whose approach is
built for exactly this: situations that cannot be fixed, only lived through.

The claim ACT researchers are most confident about is that it moves psychological flexibility
itself, modestly — which our full article covers, including where the evidence is thinner than the
enthusiasm.

**And a practical note that is not psychological:** write down your questions as they occur to you.
People routinely leave the appointment where the result arrives having forgotten what they meant
to ask, because that conversation is not one anyone processes well in real time. Take someone with
you if you can.

## When this isn't enough

**If the waiting has become unmanageable** — if you cannot work, cannot sleep at all, or the
anxiety is constant — tell your GP. Short-term support during a wait is a legitimate reason to be
seen and does not require the result to be bad.

**If the wait is long and the question is serious**, ask whether anyone can tell you the expected
timescale, and whether there is a nurse specialist you can contact. In cancer pathways
particularly, clinical nurse specialists exist substantially for this, and most people do not know
they can call them.

**And if you are having thoughts of harming yourself**, contact your doctor, a crisis line or
emergency services today. Waiting for a serious result is a recognised period of acute distress,
and that is a reason to be supported rather than to get through it alone.

One last thing, said plainly: whatever the result is, you will handle it better rested and less
alone than exhausted and isolated. That is most of what the list above is for.
