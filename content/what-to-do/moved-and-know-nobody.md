The flat is sorted, the job is fine, and you have realised you have not had a conversation that
was not transactional in eleven days.

## Do this now

The instinct is to meet people. The thing that works is duller and more reliable.

1. **Pick one recurring activity and commit to eight weeks of it.** Not a one-off event — the same
   thing, same time, same people. A class, a choir, a run club, a volunteering slot, a league.
2. **Choose it for the activity, not for the people.** If you would do it alone, you will keep
   going on the weeks when you do not feel sociable. If you are only there to meet people, you
   will stop by week three.
3. **Go the second time.** The first time is easy and tells you almost nothing. The change happens
   somewhere around the fourth or fifth.
4. **Do not try to make friends.** Learn two names. Say hello next week. That is the whole
   assignment.
5. **Add one repeated low-stakes place** — the same café, the same shop, the same time. Being
   recognised somewhere is worth more than it sounds when you are new.
6. **Say yes to everything for the first three months**, including things you would normally
   decline. Filter later.

## If that doesn't fit

**If nothing appeals:** pick the least objectionable one anyway. The mechanism is repetition and
proximity, not enthusiasm.

**If your schedule is genuinely impossible:** one thing every fortnight, protected, beats one
thing weekly that you abandon.

## Why repetition rather than events

The research does not study "making friends," but it studies the mechanism repeatedly, and it
keeps pointing the same way: **the group is doing the work.**

In a ten-week group drumming programme with mental health service users, participants improved on
anxiety, depression and social resilience — and showed a shift away from a pro-inflammatory and
toward an anti-inflammatory immune profile
([Fancourt et al., *PLOS ONE*, 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0151136)).
The same research group found comparable effects for group *singing*, and a separate study found
repetitive drumming produced no greater cortisol reduction than simply listening to instrumental
music.

Read that together: the drum was not the active ingredient. **Doing something rhythmic and
absorbing, with the same people, repeatedly, was.**

The same conclusion arrives from a different direction in group therapy research, where
**cohesion** — the bond between members — predicts outcome at 0.56, almost identical to the
alliance–outcome relationship in individual therapy
([Burlingame et al.](https://findings.org.uk/PHP/dl.php?f=Burlingame_GM_2.cab&s=eb&sf=rel)). Our
[group therapy article](/clinic/group-therapy) covers it.

**And the practical finding worth acting on**, from social prescribing research: connection works
considerably better when someone helps you get there the first time than when you are handed a
name and a phone number. Our
[social prescribing article](/clinic/social-prescribing) is honest that the controlled evidence
for referral schemes is weak — but accompaniment is the component that consistently distinguishes
the ones that work.

If you know anyone at all in your new city, ask them to bring you to something once.

## What tends not to work

**Apps designed for making friends** produce a lot of first meetings and very few second ones. The
mechanism above is repetition; a series of coffees with different strangers is the opposite of it.

**Waiting until you feel more settled.** The settling largely comes from the going.

**And a specific trap:** treating every interaction as an audition. People become friends through
sustained low-stakes proximity — the accumulation of forty ordinary exchanges — far more often
than through one good conversation.

## When this isn't enough

Loneliness after a move is expected and usually improves over six to twelve months. If it has not
begun to shift after a year of genuine effort, that is worth attention rather than endurance.

**Worth a GP conversation if:** you have stopped trying, you are avoiding social situations you
used to manage, you are drinking more, or the isolation has become low mood that persists
regardless of circumstances.

If anxiety about social situations is what is stopping you — not lack of opportunity but dread of
taking it — that is social anxiety, and it is among the most treatable conditions we cover. CBT
with graded exposure has strong evidence. Our
[article on finding a therapist](/clinic/finding-a-therapist) covers where to start, and
[group therapy](/clinic/group-therapy) is worth reading, since it treats the very thing that makes
it hard.
