You are good at your job. That is the problem. It has stopped costing you anything, and the days
now pass through you rather than being spent.

## Do this now

**Not the four-circle diagram. See below.**

1. **Ask a smaller question than you have been asking.** Not *what is my purpose* — **is there
   something that makes tomorrow morning worth meeting?**
2. **Write down five things you used to find absorbing**, at any point in your life, including
   childhood. Do not filter for viability.
3. **Pick one and do a small version of it this month.** Not a career change. An evening class, a
   tool, a project with a deadline you invented.
4. **Choose something you will be bad at.** Competence is the thing that has stopped working; the
   remedy is not more of it.
5. **Make it involve other people** if you can. See below for why.
6. **Do not resign this month.** The clarity you are looking for arrives from doing something, not
   from clearing space to think.

## If that doesn't fit

**If nothing on the list of five appeals now:** that is common and does not mean the list is
wrong. Pick the one you are most curious about rather than the one you most want.

**If the honest answer is that the job needs to change:** that decision is real, and it is better
made from inside a life with something in it than from a standing start.

## Why smaller rather than grander

You have seen the ikigai diagram — four overlapping circles, with *what you can be paid for* as one
of them.

**That diagram is largely a Western invention** and one of its circles contradicts the research it
borrows authority from. In the Ohsaki Study, 43,391 Japanese adults were followed for seven years;
those without a sense of ikigai had a
[**hazard ratio of 1.5 for all-cause mortality**](https://pure.fujita-hu.ac.jp/en/publications/sense-of-life-worth-living-ikigai-and-mortality-in-japan-ohsaki-s/).

The respondents included retired people, homemakers and the very old. Their stated ikigai was
frequently **a grandchild, a garden, a weekly obligation, a craft practised badly**. Nothing
required it to be economically productive.

**The cause-specific breakdown is the interesting part.** The elevated risk was significant for
cardiovascular disease and for external causes — accidents and suicide — and
[**not significant for cancer**](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Sense-of-Life-Worth-Living-(Ikigai)-and-Mortality-Sone-Nakaya/bffe858c706dc4298b8e1e947e2721bb580a6367).
Having a reason to get up tracks with the heart and with the will to keep going, not with cellular
disease — which is what you would expect if the mechanism runs through stress physiology, health
behaviour and social connection.

**That is why the instruction above says to involve other people.** It is the direction the data
points, and our [full ikigai article](/journal/ikigai-without-the-infographic) covers it.

**One honest limitation:** these are observational cohort studies. Nobody has randomized anyone to
acquire a sense of purpose, and it is entirely plausible that healthier, better-connected people are
simply more likely to report having ikigai.

## Why the diagram is wrong for you specifically

For someone competent and bored, the four circles will send you hunting for a **vocation** — work
that is loved, valued, needed and paid. You already have three of those. What you are missing is
not a job.

The Japanese research construct is asking something quieter: whether there is anything in your week
that you would meet the morning for. For people whose lives are organised entirely around
achievement, that question has usually gone unasked for years, which is exactly why it is the
useful one.

## On getting help with this

If you want structured help thinking it through, coaching is a reasonable fit — this is closer to
its actual evidence base than most things it is sold for. Theeboom's meta-analysis found an overall
effect of
[*g* = 0.66, with the strongest result on goal-directed
self-regulation](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17439760.2013.837499).

**Two caveats**, covered in our [life coaching article](/journal/life-coaching-evidence). That
evidence is predominantly about workplace coaching rather than life coaching. And more sessions
did **not** predict better outcomes — studies with fewer than five reported higher effects on some
measures. A short engagement is the defensible structure, whatever a package says.

## When this isn't enough

**Boredom and depression are distinguishable and worth distinguishing.** Boredom is
under-stimulation: you can imagine enjoying things, you just are not doing any of them. Depression
is the loss of the capacity — nothing sounds good, including the things that used to.

If it is the second — if the flatness extends everywhere, if sleep or appetite has changed, if you
have stopped enjoying things unrelated to work — that is worth a GP conversation rather than a
career decision. Large decisions made from inside a depressive episode are frequently regretted,
and treating the depression first tends to change what the right decision looks like.

And if this has become a sense that none of it was worth it, or thoughts of harming yourself,
contact your doctor or a crisis line. Midlife achievement without satisfaction is a recognised
risk pattern and a reason to have support around you.
