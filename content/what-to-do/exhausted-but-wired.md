You were exhausted all afternoon. Now it is eleven at night, you are in bed, and you are
completely alert. This is not a wind-down problem, and no amount of chamomile is going to touch
it.

There are two things to do. One is tonight. The one that matters is tomorrow morning.

## Do this now

**Tonight, so you can sleep:**

1. **Get out of bright light.** Overhead lights off, lamps on, screens dimmed as far as they go.
2. **Breathe out longer than you breathe in.** Four seconds in, six out. Five minutes, lying
   down. Do not breathe deeply — slow the rate, not the volume.
3. **If you are still awake after twenty minutes, get up.** Low light, no screen, something dull.
   Return when sleepy.

**Tomorrow morning, so this stops happening:**

4. **Get outside within an hour of waking.** Fifteen to thirty minutes. No sunglasses for that
   part.
5. **Do it again the next morning, and the one after.** Consistency of timing matters more than
   duration.

## If that doesn't fit

**If you cannot get outside in the morning:** stand at the brightest window you have, or use a
10,000 lux light box positioned per the manufacturer's distance, angled at eye level, for thirty
minutes while you do something else.

**If you work nights or shift patterns:** the principles hold but the timing does not. This
needs individual advice from a clinician who knows your schedule.

## Why it works

The pattern you are describing — tired in the day, alert at night — is usually a phase problem,
not a relaxation problem. Your internal clock is running later than your life is.

Light is the dominant signal by which that clock sets itself. Specialized cells in the retina
containing a pigment called melanopsin project not to the visual system but to the brain's master
clock, and they are particularly sensitive to blue wavelengths around 450 to 480 nanometres
([*Blue-light therapy: systematic review and
meta-analysis*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9511000/)).

**Timing determines direction.** Morning light advances the clock — pulls sleep and wake earlier.
Evening light delays it. The same lamp helps or harms depending entirely on when you sit under
it.

For scale: a bright office is perhaps 300 to 500 lux. Outdoors on an overcast morning is around
10,000. Stepping outside is almost always the highest-dose option available, and it is free.

**On the breathing:** slowing to around six cycles a minute increases parasympathetic control of
the heart, with a longer exhale increasing the effect
([Laborde et al., 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0149763422002007);
[Bae et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.13905)). Five minutes is as good as twenty. This
is a real but modest effect and it is not going to overcome a clock that is three hours out.

**One honest caveat.** Bright light therapy is well established for seasonal affective disorder
and recommended by the American Psychiatric Association. For non-seasonal depression the evidence
is more mixed — a randomized trial in 224 adolescents found morning bright light added nothing
detectable over placebo red light
([2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10938243/)). We are recommending it here for
sleep timing, where the mechanism is not in dispute, rather than as a mood treatment.

## When this isn't enough

If the pattern persists after two or three weeks of consistent morning light, something else is
going on. Worth investigating:

- **Sleep apnoea.** Snoring, waking unrefreshed, daytime sleepiness. Extremely common and very
  treatable.
- **Alcohol.** It shortens sleep onset and reliably fragments the second half of the night.
- **Caffeine timing.** Its half-life is around five to six hours; a 3pm coffee is still
  meaningfully present at bedtime.
- **Depression and anxiety**, both of which disrupt sleep architecture directly.
- **Delayed sleep phase disorder**, which is a diagnosable circadian condition rather than a
  habit, and which responds to properly timed light and sometimes melatonin under supervision.

If you are managing bipolar disorder, do not start light therapy without psychiatric supervision
— it can trigger hypomania.

And if insomnia has lasted more than a month, ask your doctor about **CBT-I** by name. It is the
guideline-recommended first-line treatment and it is not what most people are offered by default.
