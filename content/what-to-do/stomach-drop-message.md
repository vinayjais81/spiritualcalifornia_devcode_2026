The email, the message, the comment on the document. You felt it in your stomach before you had
finished the second line, and you are now composing a reply in your head that is considerably
better argued than anything you would actually send.

## Do this now

1. **Do not reply.** Not a holding reply. Not a single line. Nothing.
2. **Stand up and move away from the screen.** Physically. Another room, the corridor, outside.
3. **Breathe out longer than you breathe in.** Four seconds in, six out. Five cycles, about a
   minute.
4. **Name the specific thing that stung**, in one sentence, to yourself. *He implied I hadn't
   read it.* Specificity shrinks it; vagueness lets it expand.
5. **Come back and re-read the message once**, slowly. A meaningful proportion of the time it says
   something less severe than it did the first time.
6. **Then wait.** Ideally until tomorrow. Minimum, an hour.

## If that doesn't fit

**If a reply is genuinely urgent:** send four words — *"Got it, will respond."* — and nothing
else. That buys hours without appearing to ignore anyone.

**If you must reply today:** write the reply you want to send, in a document rather than the
message box, and do not paste it anywhere. Then write the one you will actually send. The first
draft was never for them.

## Why it works

Two separate things are being managed here.

**The physiology.** Slowing your breathing to roughly six cycles a minute increases parasympathetic
control of the heart — a meta-analysis of voluntary slow breathing found consistent increases in
vagally-mediated heart rate variability both during and after practice
([Laborde et al., 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0149763422002007)).
Lengthening the exhale relative to the inhale increases the effect
([Bae et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.13905)), which is why the counts are uneven. No
device is needed, and five minutes performs as well as twenty.

The effect is modest and temporary. What it buys is a gap.

**The delay.** This part has no elegant trial behind it, and we would rather say so. What is well
established is that acute stress narrows attention and biases interpretation toward threat — which
is precisely the state in which people write emails they later have to apologize for. Re-reading
after arousal has come down is not a technique so much as an admission that your reading a minute
ago was not neutral.

## When this isn't enough

If your stomach drops at most messages from a particular person, that is a pattern rather than an
incident, and the useful question is about the relationship rather than the email.

If it happens with most messages from anyone, and you are scanning your inbox with dread, that is
worth taking seriously — chronic anticipatory anxiety responds well to treatment, and our article
on [finding a therapist](/clinic/finding-a-therapist) covers where to start.

And if the message concerned something serious — a formal complaint, a legal matter, a
redundancy — the right next step is advice rather than a reply. Do not respond to that category of
message without talking to someone first.
