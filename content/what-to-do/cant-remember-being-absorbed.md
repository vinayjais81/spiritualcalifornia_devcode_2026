You watch things while doing other things. You read with a phone nearby. Somewhere in the last few
years the experience of being entirely inside one activity stopped happening, and you did not
notice it leaving.

## Do this now

**Pick something that meets three conditions**, and do it once this month:

- **With other people**, in person
- **Rhythmic or physical** — hands, body, voice
- **Slightly beyond your competence**, so it demands attention rather than allowing it

Concretely: a drumming circle, a choir, a dance class, a pottery wheel, a climbing wall, a
team sport, a cooking class where you cannot check your phone because your hands are covered.

1. **Book one thing.** This week, for a date within the month.
2. **Choose it for the activity, not the outcome.** You are not trying to become good at it.
3. **Leave your phone in a bag.** Absorption cannot survive an interruptible pocket.
4. **Go twice.** The first time you will be managing self-consciousness. The second is when
   something can happen.

## If that doesn't fit

**If groups are impossible right now:** the same three conditions apply solo, minus the strongest
ingredient. Something with your hands, slightly too hard, no phone. It works less well and it
works.

**If nothing appeals:** pick the least objectionable. Interest frequently follows participation
rather than preceding it.

## Why groups and rhythm

The evidence keeps pointing at the same component, from several directions.

A ten-week **group drumming** programme with mental health service users produced improvements in
anxiety, depression and social resilience — alongside a shift away from a pro-inflammatory and
toward an anti-inflammatory immune profile
([Fancourt et al., *PLOS ONE*, 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0151136)).
The authors were careful to call the immune work preliminary, and so are we.

**But the drum was probably not the active ingredient.** The same research group found comparable
effects for group *singing*. And a separate study found repetitive drumming produced no greater
cortisol reduction than listening to instrumental meditation music.

Read those together and the mechanism is **the group**, doing something rhythmic, repeatedly.

The same conclusion arrives from group therapy research, where **cohesion** predicts outcome at
0.56 — almost identical to the therapeutic alliance's relationship with outcome in individual
therapy
([Burlingame et al.](https://findings.org.uk/PHP/dl.php?f=Burlingame_GM_2.cab&s=eb&sf=rel)).

**And why "slightly too hard" matters:** absorption requires that the task use most of your
attention. Something you can do while thinking about work will be done while thinking about work.

**One thing to be honest about.** Nobody has run a trial on "restoring the capacity for
absorption." We are assembling this from adjacent evidence and labelling it as such.

## What tends not to work

**Trying to concentrate harder.** Absorption is not effortful attention — it is what happens when
an activity takes your attention rather than requiring you to give it.

**Removing distractions and waiting.** A phone-free evening does not produce absorption; it
produces a quiet evening in which you notice you are not absorbed.

**And most solitary screen-based hobbies**, which are designed to be interruptible.

## When this isn't enough

If nothing has been absorbing for a long time, and nothing is enjoyable either, the useful
distinction is between **fragmented attention** and **anhedonia** — the loss of the capacity for
pleasure.

The rough test: if you can imagine enjoying something but cannot settle into it, that is attention.
If you cannot imagine enjoying anything, that is worth a GP conversation. Anhedonia is one of the
core features of depression, it is easy to mistake for a lifestyle problem, and it responds well to
treatment.

**Also worth considering:** if attention has changed noticeably in adulthood — if you cannot follow
a film you want to watch, cannot finish books you are enjoying, or have started losing track
mid-conversation — that is worth raising with a doctor rather than absorbing as a personal failing.
Several things cause it, including sleep disorders, thyroid problems, depression, and adult ADHD
that was never identified.
