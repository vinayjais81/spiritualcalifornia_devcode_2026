You have had this for years. You have read more about it than most clinicians you have met. You do
not need an explanation of chronic pain — you need to get through the next few days.

We are not going to pretend a breathing exercise will do much here.

## Do this now

1. **Reduce, do not stop.** Complete rest during a flare tends to lengthen it and costs conditioning
   you will have to rebuild. Aim for roughly half of what you would do on an ordinary day.
2. **Use your baseline, not your ambition.** If you know you can walk twenty minutes without
   consequence, do ten today — not thirty because it is a good hour.
3. **Break activity into shorter blocks with rests between**, rather than one long effort followed
   by collapse.
4. **Take your usual medication as prescribed**, on schedule rather than reactively, if that is
   your regimen.
5. **Heat or cold, whichever has helped you before.** There is no universal answer and your own
   history is better evidence than any general advice.
6. **Protect sleep**, which flares reliably damage and which reliably worsens pain when damaged.
   Keep the wake time fixed even after a bad night.
7. **Tell one person.** Flares are isolating and the isolation makes them worse.

## If that doesn't fit

**If this flare is different from your usual pattern** — new location, new character, new
associated symptoms — that is a reason to contact your clinician rather than to apply your usual
flare plan.

**If you do not have a flare plan**, this is worth making with your clinician when you are not in
one. A written plan made in advance is worth considerably more than decisions made at 2am in pain.

## What the evidence actually supports

**Pacing** — matching activity to a sustainable baseline rather than to how you feel on a given
day — is the core of most chronic pain self-management programmes. The boom-and-bust cycle it
prevents is one of the better-described patterns in the field.

**Psychological approaches change function more reliably than they change pain.** A meta-analysis
of 21 randomized trials of [acceptance and commitment
therapy](/clinic/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy) for chronic pain found medium effects on pain
interference and functional impairment, and **small** effects on pain intensity
([*PLOS ONE*, 2024](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0301226)).
At three months, functional impairment showed a large effect.

That distinction is the honest one. These approaches are more likely to change what you can do
while it hurts than to make it hurt less — which is not the same as nothing, and is also not what
most people are hoping for.

**One caveat on that trial evidence**: the analysis included only studies where the control was
treatment as usual or a waiting list. Comparisons against CBT, relaxation or exercise were
excluded.

**On the physical treatments**, our journal covers them honestly:
[acupuncture](/journal/acupuncture-what-the-data-shows) has the strongest evidence of anything in
that library for chronic pain — real, replicated, and modest, with the sham-controlled difference
smaller than advocates suggest. [Tai chi](/journal/tai-chi-evidence) has good evidence in
fibromyalgia including a *New England Journal of Medicine* trial.
[Massage](/journal/therapeutic-massage-evidence) helps pain short-term and does not measurably
change function.

## The thing worth saying

Most articles like this one end with something about acceptance, and it is usually written by
someone who does not have chronic pain.

What the evidence supports is narrower and less inspiring: **not fighting the sensation is
associated with better function than fighting it.** That is not the same as being at peace with
it, and nobody is required to be grateful for their condition. Our
[ACT article](/clinic/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy) makes the point that acceptance is not
resignation, and that where circumstances can be changed, changing them is the
values-consistent action.

## When this isn't enough

**Contact your clinician** if the flare is severe, prolonged beyond your usual pattern, or
different in character. New neurological symptoms — weakness, numbness, loss of bladder or bowel
control — need urgent assessment regardless of your pain history.

**Ask about a pain management programme** if you have not been through one. Multidisciplinary
programmes have better evidence than any single component, and many people with long-standing pain
have never been offered one.

**And chronic pain and depression travel together**, in both directions. If the flare has brought
low mood, hopelessness, or thoughts that you cannot keep doing this, that is common, treatable,
and worth raising directly rather than absorbing as part of the condition.

If you are having thoughts of harming yourself, please contact your doctor, a crisis line, or
emergency services. Chronic pain carries a documented elevated risk, and that is a reason to be
supported rather than a reason to be alarmed at yourself.
