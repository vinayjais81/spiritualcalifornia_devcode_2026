Flying west, you stay up late and it mostly sorts itself out. Flying east, you arrive at breakfast
time in a body that is convinced it is the middle of the night, and it takes days.

The asymmetry is real: **eastward travel requires your clock to advance — to run earlier — and the
human circadian system resists advancing more than it resists delaying.**

## Do this now

**The three days before you fly:**

1. **Shift your bedtime and wake time thirty to sixty minutes earlier each day.** Not the whole
   shift — just a head start.
2. **Get bright light immediately on waking** at that new earlier time. This is what tells the clock
   to advance.
3. **Dim the evening.** Overhead lights off, screens down, from a couple of hours before your new
   bedtime.

**On arrival:**

4. **Get outside in the morning at your destination**, as early as you reasonably can. Morning
   light at the new local time is the main lever.
5. **Avoid bright light in the destination's late evening** — that pushes your clock the wrong way.
6. **Eat at local meal times** from the first day, even without much appetite.
7. **Stay up until a normal local bedtime.** A short nap before mid-afternoon is survivable; a long
   one is not.
8. **Expect three to five days**, roughly one per time zone crossed. Nothing makes this instant.

## If that doesn't fit

**If the shift is very large — eight time zones or more east:** the timing becomes genuinely
counter-intuitive, because badly timed morning light can push your clock the wrong way. If you do
this route regularly, it is worth getting individual advice from a sleep clinician rather than
following general rules.

**If it is a short trip — two or three days:** consider not adapting at all. Keeping home hours and
scheduling around them is often less costly than shifting twice.

## Why light rather than melatonin first

Light is the dominant signal by which the human circadian system sets itself. Specialised retinal
cells containing melanopsin project to the brain's master clock and are particularly sensitive to
blue wavelengths around 450 to 480 nanometres
([review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9511000/)).

**Timing determines direction, and this is the whole thing.** Light in your biological morning
advances the clock, pulling everything earlier — which is what eastward travel needs. Light in your
biological evening delays it. The same light helps or harms depending entirely on when it lands.

For scale: indoor lighting is roughly 300-500 lux; an overcast morning outdoors is around 10,000.
Going outside is the highest-dose intervention available and it is free.

**On melatonin:** it is widely used for jet lag and timing matters as much as with light — taken at
the wrong time it can shift you in the wrong direction. It is worth discussing with a pharmacist or
doctor rather than guessing, and it is not appropriate for everyone.

**On alcohol:** it shortens sleep onset and reliably fragments the second half of the night, which
is the half you need most on arrival.

## When this isn't enough

Jet lag resolves. If it does not — if your sleep remains disrupted weeks after travel — that is
worth a conversation with a doctor, since travel sometimes unmasks an underlying sleep or circadian
problem rather than causing one.

If you travel across time zones frequently and your sleep never fully consolidates, that is an
occupational health issue as much as a personal one, and shift-work and travel sleep problems have
specific management.

And if you find yourself using alcohol or sedatives routinely to manage travel sleep, that is worth
raising with a doctor before it becomes the pattern rather than the exception.
