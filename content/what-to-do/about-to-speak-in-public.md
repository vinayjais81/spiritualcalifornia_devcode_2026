You are on in ten minutes. Your heart rate is up, your mouth is dry, and you have started
rehearsing the first line so many times it has stopped meaning anything.

## Do this now

1. **Find somewhere to sit for five minutes.** A corridor, a stairwell, a car. It does not need to
   be private.
2. **Breathe in for four seconds, out for six.** Continue for five minutes. Do not breathe
   *deeply* — you are slowing the rate, not filling your lungs.
3. **Do this before you go on, not while you are on.** This is the finding that matters and it is
   below.
4. **Then stop, stand, and let your heart rate be up.** It will be. That is appropriate for what
   you are about to do.
5. **Say your first sentence out loud once**, at normal volume, then stop rehearsing.
6. **Drink water.** Dry mouth is the most fixable of these symptoms and the most distracting.

## If that doesn't fit

**If you have thirty seconds, not five minutes:** three cycles still matter. Do them with your
eyes open.

**If breath focus makes you more anxious** — true for some people, particularly with panic
history — skip it. Instead: press your feet into the floor, name four objects in the room, and
roll your shoulders back once.

## Why the timing matters

A meta-analysis of binaural auditory beats across cognition, anxiety and pain examined *when*
during a task the intervention was applied, and found that exposure **before** a task — or before
and during — outperformed exposure during the task alone
([Garcia-Argibay et al., *Psychological Research*, 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00426-018-1066-8)).

The same logic holds for slow breathing. Trying to run a breathing protocol while you are talking
divides your attention at exactly the moment you need it. Front-load it.

**On the breathing itself:** slowing to around six cycles a minute reliably increases
parasympathetic control of the heart, with a longer exhale increasing the effect
([Laborde et al., 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0149763422002007);
[Bae et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.13905)). A dose-response study comparing 5, 10, 15
and 20 minutes found no differences between them
([You et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph182312478)) — five minutes is the whole
intervention.

**And one thing not to aim for.** The goal is not to be calm. Elevated arousal before speaking is
functional — it sharpens attention and projects energy. Audiences read flat delivery as
disengagement far more often than they read nerves as incompetence. What you are trying to reduce
is the *spiral*, not the arousal.

## When this isn't enough

If fear of speaking has led you to turn down opportunities, avoid meetings, or change roles, that
is social anxiety rather than nerves — and it is one of the most treatable conditions in this
whole library. CBT with graded exposure has strong evidence, and beta blockers are sometimes
prescribed for performance situations specifically. A GP conversation is worth having.
