You stood up and something locked. Now you are moving like a much older person and every position
is wrong.

## First: the things that mean see a doctor now

Most acute back pain is non-specific and settles. **A small proportion is not**, and these are the
signs that need urgent assessment rather than a stretch:

- **Loss of bladder or bowel control**, or difficulty passing urine
- **Numbness in the saddle area** — inner thighs, groin, buttocks
- **Progressive weakness in a leg or foot** — dragging a foot, unable to lift the front of it
- **Fever**, or feeling generally unwell with the pain
- **Significant unexplained weight loss**, or a history of cancer
- **Back pain after a fall, accident, or significant trauma**
- **Severe pain that is worse at night or when lying down**, rather than better

The first three together can indicate **cauda equina syndrome**, which is a surgical emergency
where hours matter. If any of those apply, go to an emergency department today.

Everything below assumes none of them do.

## Do this now

The old advice was bed rest. The current advice is close to the opposite.

1. **Keep moving.** Gently, frequently. Prolonged rest makes non-specific back pain worse and
   slower to resolve.
2. **Walk for five to ten minutes**, several times today, even if slowly.
3. **Change position often.** Do not find one tolerable position and stay in it for two hours.
4. **Heat, not ice**, for muscular spasm — a heat pack for fifteen to twenty minutes.
5. **Over-the-counter pain relief** as directed, if you can take it, so that you can keep moving.
   The point of the analgesia is the movement.
6. **Stand up every twenty minutes tomorrow.** Set an alarm; you will not remember.

## If that doesn't fit

**If you cannot walk at all**, or the pain is severe and unmanageable, that is a call to your
doctor today — not an emergency, but not something to sit out either.

**If you have had this before and know what helps you**, do that. Recurrent non-specific back pain
often has a personal pattern worth trusting.

## Why movement rather than rest

Movement is the intervention with the best evidence for non-specific low back pain, and the format
matters much less than the fact of it.

Cochrane's review of yoga for chronic non-specific low back pain found low-certainty evidence of
clinical improvement against non-exercise controls — and, against *other back-focused exercise*,
[moderate-certainty evidence of little or no difference in back-specific function at three
months](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD010671.pub3/full).

Read that as good news. It means the exercise you will actually do is the right one. Cochrane's
own conclusion was that the choice may depend on availability, cost and preference.

**One caution from the same review**, because it applies directly to someone whose back has just
gone: yoga was associated with **increased adverse events compared with no exercise** — risk ratio
4.76, mostly increased back pain. Deep forward folds and twists on an irritable lumbar spine are a
predictable way to make this worse. Gentle movement now; classes later.

**On massage**, Cochrane found benefits mostly in short-term follow-up for pain, with
[no difference in function](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD001929.pub3/full)
and evidence graded low or very low quality. Reasonable for symptom relief; not a structural fix.

## What not to do

**Do not go to bed.** This is the single most common mistake and it prolongs recovery.

**Do not rush to imaging.** Guidelines advise against routine imaging for non-specific back pain
without red flags, because incidental findings are extremely common in people with no pain at all
and frequently lead to unnecessary intervention. Our
[fascia article](/journal/fascia-and-chinese-medicine) covers the striking finding that the
magnitude of low back pain does not correlate with the magnitude of disc displacement.

**Do not let anyone diagnose a structural problem by touch alone**, or tell you your spine is out
of alignment and needs repeated correction.

## When this isn't enough

If it has not started improving within a few weeks, see your doctor. Most acute episodes settle
substantially inside six weeks.

If it becomes chronic, the treatments with the best evidence are exercise-based, and
[yoga](/journal/yoga-evidence-base) is one reasonable delivery route among several — as is
[tai chi](/journal/tai-chi-evidence), which has the strongest evidence base of anything in our
wellness journal.

And if pain radiates below the knee with numbness or tingling, that suggests nerve involvement and
is worth a proper assessment rather than self-management.
