You have been aware of the date for a couple of weeks — not thinking about it exactly, but aware
of it, the way you are aware of weather coming.

There is no technique on this page that will make the day smaller. What follows is about not being
ambushed by it.

## Do this now, about a week ahead

1. **Decide what the day will be.** Not *get through it* — actually decide. Work, or not. Alone,
   or with someone. At home, or somewhere else.
2. **Tell one person the date.** This is the single most useful thing on this page. Anniversaries
   are largely invisible to everyone else, and being the only person who knows what today is makes
   it considerably heavier.
3. **Decide whether you want to mark it**, and how. A visit, a meal they liked, a walk you took
   together, a candle, nothing at all. All of these are legitimate, including nothing.
4. **Do not schedule anything demanding.** Not because you will fall apart, but because you may
   simply not have much available that day.
5. **Plan the evening**, which is often harder than the day.
6. **Decide in advance what you will do if it is worse than expected** — who you will call, where
   you will go.

## If that doesn't fit

**If you would rather not mark it at all:** that is a legitimate choice and not avoidance. Some
people find the day easier when it is treated as an ordinary day. What tends not to work is
*intending* to treat it as ordinary and then finding at 4pm that you have not.

**If you dread it more than the day itself turns out to be:** that is extremely common. The
anticipation is frequently the worst part.

## What we are not going to tell you

**There are no stages.** The five-stage model is widely repeated and was never established as a
sequence that grieving people move through. If you are not where you think you should be by now,
there is no schedule you are behind on.

**Grief does not resolve on a timetable.** Anniversaries, birthdays, songs, seasons and smells
reactivate it years later, and that is not regression. Most people describe it as arriving less
often rather than becoming smaller.

**And most grief is not a disorder.** Intense sadness, disrupted sleep, poor concentration, waves
that arrive without warning — these are the ordinary shape of loss, not symptoms requiring
treatment.

## On writing about it, with a caution

If you want to do something more than plan, the four-day expressive writing protocol is a
reasonable low-cost option: fifteen to twenty minutes a day, four consecutive days, writing
continuously about the loss including how you feel about it. Nobody reads it.

**But the caution matters more here than anywhere else in this series.** The effect is small —
our [article on it](/journal/expressive-writing-pennebaker) traces how it shrank from *d* = 0.47
to around 0.16 as it was studied more carefully. And most people feel **worse immediately after
writing**, with any benefit appearing over subsequent weeks.

For someone in acute grief, that short-term worsening is a real cost. If you do this, do it with
time afterwards, not before bed, and not on the anniversary itself.

**Something to make rather than write** is often better in grief specifically — which is the
premise of [art therapy](/journal/art-therapy-beyond-talking), where the evidence in supportive
care is more consistent than for solitary writing.

## When to talk to someone

Grief is not a medical problem. But a minority of people develop **prolonged grief disorder** — a
recognised condition, with specific treatment — and it is worth knowing the shape of it.

The features that distinguish it: persistent intense yearning or preoccupation, more than a year
after the death, alongside things like difficulty accepting the loss, intense emotional pain,
feeling that life is meaningless, marked difficulty engaging with anything, and significant
impairment in functioning that is not improving.

**The distinguishing feature is not intensity. It is being stuck** — the sense that nothing has
shifted at all across a long period.

[Interpersonal therapy](/clinic/interpersonal-therapy) has grief as one of its four defined
problem areas and treats it directly, and specific prolonged grief treatments exist. Both are more
effective than waiting.

**And separately from grief:** if you have lost interest in everything, cannot function, are
drinking substantially more, or are having thoughts of harming yourself or of joining the person
who died, contact your doctor or a crisis line today. Those thoughts are more common in
bereavement than most people realise, and they are a reason to get support rather than something
to carry privately.
