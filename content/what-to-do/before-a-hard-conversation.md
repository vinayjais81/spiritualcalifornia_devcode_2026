You know what the conversation is about. Your heart rate is up, your breathing has gone high and
shallow, and you have somewhere between one and five minutes.

## Do this now

1. **Sit down.** Feet on the floor. You do not need to leave the building or find anywhere
   quiet.
2. **Breathe in through your nose for four seconds.**
3. **Breathe out through your nose or mouth for six seconds.** The out-breath must be longer
   than the in-breath. This is the part that matters.
4. **Do not breathe deeply.** You are slowing the rate, not filling your lungs. Deep slow
   breathing makes people lightheaded, which is the opposite of useful right now.
5. **Continue for five cycles**, or as long as you have. About a minute.
6. **Stop, and go.** Do not check whether you feel calm. That question undoes the effect.

## If that doesn't fit

**If you're on camera in thirty seconds:** three cycles is enough to matter. Do them with your
eyes open, looking at nothing in particular.

**If breath focus makes you more anxious** — this is true for some people — skip it. Instead,
press your feet hard into the floor for five seconds, release, and name three objects in the
room. You are giving attention something concrete to hold.

## Why it works

Slowing your breathing to around six cycles a minute increases parasympathetic control of the
heart — the branch of the nervous system that brings arousal down. A meta-analysis of voluntary
slow breathing found this reliably raises vagally-mediated heart rate variability both during
practice and immediately afterwards ([Laborde et al., *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*,
2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0149763422002007)).

Three practical details come from that literature. **You do not need a device** — a five-minute
session without any biofeedback equipment produced broadly similar effects
([Laborde et al., *Psychophysiology*, 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/psyp.13952)).
**Longer is not better** — a study comparing 5, 10, 15 and 20 minutes found no differences
between durations ([You et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph182312478)). And
**lengthening the exhale relative to the inhale** further increases high-frequency heart rate
variability ([Bae et al., *Psychophysiology*, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.13905)), which
is why the counts above are uneven.

The effect is modest and it is temporary. What it buys you is a slightly wider gap between what
the other person says and what you say back. That gap is usually where the conversation is won
or lost.

## When this isn't enough

If you are dreading a specific conversation, breathing will not change the fact that you have
not decided what you want from it. Ninety seconds of arousal management is not preparation.
Before the breathing, ask yourself one question and answer it in a sentence: *what do I want to
be true when this is over?*

If you dread most conversations at work, or if anticipating them is costing you sleep over
days, that is not a technique problem. It is worth talking to someone — a therapist, or a coach
if the issue is genuinely situational rather than psychological. Our [article on life
coaching](/journal/life-coaching-evidence) covers how to tell the difference and what the
evidence actually supports.
