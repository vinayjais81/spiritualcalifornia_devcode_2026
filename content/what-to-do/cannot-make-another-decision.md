Which supplier. Which wording. Which candidate. Which of two nearly identical options for
something that will not matter in a month. You have been doing this since nine and you have
arrived at a small, easy question and found that you cannot answer it at all.

## Do this now

1. **Stop deciding. Close the thing.** The next twenty minutes of staring will not produce a
   better answer than the last twenty did.
2. **Drink a glass of water.** Read the next section before dismissing this.
3. **Walk for twenty minutes.** Outside, ideally somewhere with a view further than a corridor.
4. **Do not think about the decision.** This is the actual instruction and the hard part. Not
   "mull it over" — put it down. If it comes back, notice and return your attention to walking.
5. **Come back and decide in under two minutes.** If the two options are still close, they are
   close, and the deliberation was never going to separate them.

## If that doesn't fit

**If you cannot leave:** water, then stand somewhere different for five minutes without your
phone. A stairwell counts.

**If the decision genuinely is consequential:** then it deserves tomorrow morning rather than
this afternoon, and putting it down is still the right move.

## Why walking, and why not thinking about it

**Start with the least interesting explanation, because it is the most likely.** Fatigue,
difficulty concentrating, headache and low mood are the exact symptom cluster that mild
dehydration produces in controlled trials — at losses of 1 to 2% of body mass, which most people
reach across a busy day without noticing
([Ganio et al., *British Journal of Nutrition*, 2011](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-nutrition/article/mild-dehydration-impairs-cognitive-performance-and-mood-of-men/3388AB36B8DF73E844C9AD19271A75BF)).
One analysis found that drinking water after a loss of just **0.72% of body mass** prevented
declines in memory, attention and mood
([Benton et al., 2016](https://ajcn.nutrition.org/article/S0002-9165(22)04582-8/fulltext)).

Before concluding you are cognitively spent, rule out being thirsty. It takes ninety seconds.

**On the walking**, we should be honest about the evidence. Pooled analyses find walking has a
significant effect on depressive symptoms — but a 2024 review of 27 trials found only
[41% of studies beat their control condition](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1755296624000267).
The case for a twenty-minute walk here is practical rather than clinical: it moves you, changes
your visual environment, and interrupts a loop.

**And the not-thinking part** is the piece with the least evidence and the most anecdotal
support. What can be said is that continued deliberation on a decision you have already
deliberated to exhaustion reliably produces the same answer with more fatigue attached. Putting
it down is at minimum not worse.

## The more useful structural point

Decision fatigue is largely a **volume** problem, and volume is often fixable.

Two things that reduce it more than any recovery technique:

- **Decide once, not repeatedly.** A standing rule — a default supplier, a default meeting length,
  a default answer to a category of request — removes a decision permanently rather than easing it
  today.
- **Notice which decisions are actually reversible.** Most are. Reversible decisions deserve
  speed, not deliberation, and treating them as though they were permanent is where most of the
  day's cognitive load goes.

## When this isn't enough

If you cannot make decisions most days, or simple choices produce genuine distress, that is worth
attention. Persistent indecisiveness is a recognised feature of depression, and so is the sense
that everything requires more effort than it should.

If the difficulty is specifically fear of choosing wrongly — replaying decisions afterwards,
seeking reassurance about them, being unable to let a settled decision stay settled — that is
closer to the pattern our [ERP article](/clinic/erp-for-ocd) describes, and it is treatable.

And if the exhaustion is there before the decisions start, see our article on
[being tired despite eight hours' sleep](/what-to-do/slept-eight-hours-still-tired). Several
common medical causes present exactly this way.
