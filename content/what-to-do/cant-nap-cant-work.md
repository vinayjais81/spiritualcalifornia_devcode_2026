Two in the afternoon. You are too tired to produce anything worth keeping and too wired to sleep.
Lying down produces twenty minutes of thinking about email.

## Do this now

Lie down anyway. The instruction is not to sleep.

1. **Set a timer for twenty minutes** so you stop checking.
2. **Bring your attention to your right thumb.** Just notice whatever is there, or the absence of
   anything.
3. **Move through each finger in turn**, then the palm, back of the hand, wrist, forearm, elbow,
   upper arm, shoulder.
4. **Cross to the left hand** and repeat the whole arm.
5. **Then both legs, toe by toe.** Then the torso, the back, the neck, the face.
6. **When you notice you have drifted into thinking — and you will — return to where you left off.**
   The returning is the practice.

Most people do not finish the sequence. Some fall asleep. Either is fine.

**And before you start: drink a glass of water.** See below.

## If that doesn't fit

**If you cannot lie down:** it works seated, with your eyes closed, at a desk. Less well, but it
works.

**If you would rather be talked through it:** guided yoga nidra recordings do exactly this sequence
and are widely available for free.

**If twenty minutes is impossible:** ten does something. The evidence on duration is thin either
way.

## Why this rather than a nap

The practice is yoga nidra, stripped of its framing — a structured rotation of attention through
the body.

**What the evidence supports.** In a Delhi sleep laboratory, 41 people with chronic insomnia were
randomized to either yoga nidra or CBT-I, with polysomnography. Both improved on total sleep time,
sleep efficiency and subjective quality; the yoga nidra group showed
[marked improvement in deep slow-wave sleep and a significant fall in salivary
cortisol](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34825538/).

**And what it does not.** A 2026 systematic review found six randomized trials covering 244
participants in total — a small evidence base for a practice this popular. The same research
group's earlier review found that of 45 studies on yoga nidra, only 13 used randomized group
comparison and only five had an active control.

**One thing to be clear about:** "non-sleep deep rest" is a marketing term, not a research
category. There is no NSDR literature — there is research on yoga nidra, and the newer branding
borrows its credibility while typically shortening the practice and dropping the supervision. The
claim that twenty minutes of this substitutes for hours of sleep has no support in the literature
and we are not going to repeat it.

**On the water.** Fatigue and difficulty concentrating are the exact symptom cluster mild
dehydration produces in controlled trials, at losses most people reach across a busy morning
([Ganio et al., 2011](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-nutrition/article/mild-dehydration-impairs-cognitive-performance-and-mood-of-men/3388AB36B8DF73E844C9AD19271A75BF)).
It takes ninety seconds to rule out and it is the least interesting explanation, which is usually a
point in its favour.

## Afterwards

**Go outside for five minutes** before returning to work. Daylight does more for afternoon alertness
than the rest did, and the two together are better than either.

**And do not chase it with coffee after about two in the afternoon.** Caffeine's half-life is
roughly five to six hours, so a 3pm coffee is still meaningfully present at bedtime — which is how
a bad afternoon becomes a bad night becomes a worse afternoon.

## When this isn't enough

If you need this most afternoons, the problem is upstream. The likely candidates are sleep timing,
sleep quality, or something medical — our article on
[sleeping eight hours and still feeling terrible](/what-to-do/slept-eight-hours-still-tired)
covers what to rule out, with sleep apnoea at the top of the list.

If you are falling asleep unintentionally during the day — in meetings, at traffic lights — that is
not ordinary tiredness and needs assessment promptly.
