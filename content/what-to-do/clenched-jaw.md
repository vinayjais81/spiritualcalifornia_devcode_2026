Your teeth were touching. Your shoulders were somewhere near your ears. Neither was a decision,
and it had probably been going on for hours.

## Do this now

1. **Let your jaw hang.** Teeth apart, tongue resting on the floor of your mouth, lips together.
   This is the resting position and most people's jaw is not in it.
2. **Drop your shoulders once, deliberately**, and let them stay there.
3. **Breathe out for six, in for four**, five cycles.

**Then, for the rest of the week — this is the actual intervention:**

4. **Set three alarms.** Mid-morning, after lunch, late afternoon.
5. **When one goes off, do one thing:** check your jaw, your shoulders, and your hands. Note what
   you find. Release it.
6. **Do not judge what you find.** You are building the noticing, not the relaxing. The noticing
   is the part that generalises.

## If that doesn't fit

**If alarms are impractical:** attach the check to something you already do several times a day —
every time you open a particular app, every time you stand up, every time you drink water.

**If you clench at night:** that is bruxism, and it is a dental question. Ask your dentist to look
for wear on your molars. A night guard prevents damage that is expensive to reverse and does not
undo itself.

## Why noticing is the skill

The tension is not the problem you can act on directly — you cannot decide to stop doing something
you are not aware of doing. What is trainable is **interoception**: the sense of your own internal
state.

This is the mechanism [somatic experiencing](/journal/somatic-experiencing) and related
body-oriented therapies work with. We should be straight about the evidence there — it is a
coherent model with a thin randomized base, and its own trial authors questioned the clinical
importance of what they found. But the narrow claim that attention to bodily state can be trained
is not the contested part.

**On the breathing**, slowing to about six cycles a minute with a longer exhale increases
parasympathetic control of the heart
([Laborde et al., 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0149763422002007);
[Bae et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.13905)). It is a real, modest, temporary effect —
useful in the moment, not a cure for a pattern.

**What we are not claiming:** that three checks a day will resolve chronic tension. What they
reliably do is shorten the interval between clenching and noticing, from hours to minutes. That
is a smaller claim and an achievable one.

## The more useful question

**Tension held all day is usually holding something.** The pattern most people find, once they
start checking, is that the jaw goes at specific times — a particular meeting, a particular
person, the hour before a deadline, the drive home.

That map is worth more than the release technique. If your jaw is clenched at 4pm every Tuesday,
the intervention is probably not about your jaw.

## When this isn't enough

**See a dentist** if you have jaw pain, clicking, locking, headaches on waking, or worn teeth.
Temporomandibular disorders are common, treatable, and worth catching before the dental damage
accumulates.

**See a doctor** if the tension comes with numbness, tingling, weakness, or pain radiating into an
arm — that is a nerve question rather than a muscle one.

And if you are braced most of the time, most days, that is worth looking at as anxiety rather than
posture. Chronic muscular tension is one of the most common physical presentations of generalised
anxiety, it is highly treatable, and our article on
[what CBT actually is](/clinic/what-cbt-actually-is) covers the options.
