It is the middle of the afternoon. You are foggy, mildly irritable, possibly headachy, and
reading the same paragraph repeatedly. The obvious move is more coffee.

Try three things first, in this order. It takes about ten minutes.

## Do this now

1. **Drink a full glass of water.** Not a sip. Most people arrive at this point having had
   coffee and nothing else since breakfast.
2. **Go outside for five minutes.** Not to the window — outside. Do not take your phone.
3. **Walk while you are out there.** Slowly is fine. Around the block, or the length of the car
   park and back.
4. **Then decide about the coffee.** If you still want it, have it. But you will have ruled out
   the two cheapest explanations first, and quite often you won't want it.

## If that doesn't fit

**If you cannot leave the building:** water, then stand by the brightest window you have for
five minutes, then walk a corridor. Indoor lighting is roughly a tenth as bright as an overcast
day outside, so this is a weaker version — but it is not nothing.

**If this happens every single day at the same time:** the intervention is in the morning, not
the afternoon. See below.

## Why it works

**The water.** Fatigue, difficulty concentrating, headache and low mood are precisely the
symptom cluster that mild dehydration produces in controlled trials — at losses of only 1 to 2%
of body mass, which most people reach across a busy morning without noticing. Ganio and
colleagues induced mild dehydration in young men under controlled conditions and found impaired
mood and vigilance ([*British Journal of Nutrition*,
2011](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-nutrition/article/mild-dehydration-impairs-cognitive-performance-and-mood-of-men/3388AB36B8DF73E844C9AD19271A75BF)),
and the parallel study in women found the same effect on mood.

The threshold is lower than people expect. One analysis found that drinking water after a loss of
just **0.72% of body mass** prevented declines in memory, attention and mood
([Benton et al., *AJCN*, 2016](https://ajcn.nutrition.org/article/S0002-9165(22)04582-8/fulltext)).

**The daylight.** Bright light is the strongest signal your body clock receives, and the afternoon
dip is partly circadian. Getting outside also gives you light exposure that indoor work reliably
starves you of.

**The walking.** Pooled analyses find walking has a significant effect on depressive symptoms,
though we should be straight that the picture is mixed — a 2024 review of 27 trials found only
41% of studies beat their control condition
([*Mental Health and Physical Activity*,
2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1755296624000267)). For a ten-minute
afternoon reset, the case is more practical than clinical: it moves you, it changes your visual
environment, and it interrupts the loop.

None of this is impressive. That is rather the point — it costs nothing, takes ten minutes, and
rules out the boring explanations before you reach for a stimulant that will affect tonight's
sleep.

## When this isn't enough

If you are exhausted every afternoon regardless of water, light and movement, stop treating it as
a productivity problem.

Persistent fatigue has medical explanations that no technique addresses: thyroid dysfunction,
anaemia, sleep apnoea, diabetes, depression, medication side effects. All are common, all are
detectable with straightforward tests, and all are worth ruling out before you spend money on
anything else.

If the fatigue is worst in winter, or your sleep has drifted later and later, the fix is more
likely to be about morning light timing than afternoon intervention.
