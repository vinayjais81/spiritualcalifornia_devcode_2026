Nine to three, no gaps, and by the fourth call you have stopped being a person and become a
delivery mechanism for opinions. You are not tired exactly. You are *saturated*.

## Do this now

**Between calls, in the ninety seconds you actually have:**

1. **Stand up.** Every time. The physical change of position does more than anything else on this
   list.
2. **Look at something more than twenty feet away** for twenty seconds. A window, a corridor, the
   far wall. Your eyes have been at fixed close focus for hours.
3. **Breathe out longer than you breathe in** — four in, six out, five cycles. About a minute.
4. **Drink water.** By meeting four most people have had coffee and nothing else.
5. **Do not check your inbox in the gap.** That is the one thing guaranteed to spend the recovery
   window on more input.

**And once during the day, if you can take five minutes:**

6. **Go outside.** Not the window. Even five minutes of daylight and different air resets more
   than a longer break at your desk.

## If that doesn't fit

**If there is genuinely no gap:** do the breathing on the call, with your camera on, while
somebody else is talking. Nobody can tell.

**If back-to-back is every day:** the intervention is a calendar change, not a breathing
technique. Twenty-five minute meetings instead of thirty, as a default, creates five minutes
between every single call. It is the highest-leverage change available and it costs nothing.

## Why it works

Slowing your breathing to roughly six cycles a minute increases parasympathetic control of the
heart, with a longer exhale increasing the effect
([Laborde et al., 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0149763422002007);
[Bae et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.13905)). A device is not required — sessions
without biofeedback equipment produced broadly similar effects
([Laborde et al., *Psychophysiology*, 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/psyp.13952)).

And usefully for this situation, **short works**. A dose-response study comparing 5, 10, 15 and 20
minutes of slow breathing found no differences between them
([You et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph182312478)). You are not getting a diluted
version in ninety seconds — you are getting a short one.

**On the daylight:** bright light is the strongest signal your body clock receives, and a day
spent entirely indoors under 300-500 lux office lighting is a genuinely impoverished light
environment compared with the roughly 10,000 lux of an overcast morning outside. Our article on
[morning light](/journal/morning-light-circadian) covers the mechanism.

**What we are not claiming:** that this fixes an unsustainable schedule. It produces a transient
increase in vagal activity, most pronounced during and shortly after practice. That is real and
small. Six hours of back-to-back meetings will still be six hours of back-to-back meetings.

## When this isn't enough

If this is most days, most weeks, the honest reading is that the schedule is the problem and the
techniques are palliative.

Two things worth trying that are not personal coping:

- **Default to 25-minute meetings.** Most calendar systems support this as a setting, and it is
  the single change most likely to survive contact with an organization.
- **Decline more.** Meetings expand to fill the availability signalled by your calendar.

If you are exhausted rather than saturated — flat in the evenings, unrefreshed by weekends, no
longer enjoying things outside work — read our article on
[burnout](/what-to-do/burnt-out-holiday-didnt-fix-it). Persistent depletion that rest does not
touch is a different problem with a different answer.
