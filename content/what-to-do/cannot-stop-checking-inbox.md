You checked four minutes ago. You know you checked four minutes ago. You are going to check
again in the next ninety seconds and you already know there will be nothing there.

## Do this now

**Today, not as a permanent system — as an experiment:**

1. **Pick three specific times to check.** Not "less often." *9:30, 1:00, 4:30.* Write them down.
2. **Close the tab and the app between them.** Not minimised — closed. Visible-but-ignored does
   not work, because the checking is faster than the deciding.
3. **Put your phone in a drawer, or another room.** Face-down on the desk is not far enough away.
4. **When the urge comes — and it will, within minutes — do not fight it. Note it and continue.**
   *There's the urge.* Then keep working. It passes in well under a minute.
5. **At the scheduled time, check properly** and deal with things rather than scanning.
6. **Notice, at the end of the day, how much you actually missed.** For almost everyone the answer
   is nothing, and knowing that from evidence rather than from reassurance is the point.

## If that doesn't fit

**If your role genuinely requires responsiveness:** shorten the intervals rather than abandoning
the structure. Every 45 minutes, on the clock, is still radically different from every four.

**If the anxiety of not checking is too much:** start with 30 uninterrupted minutes once a day and
build. This is a tolerance you are building, not a rule you are obeying.

## Why the urge does not need to be fought

The loop is simple and unflattering. Uncertainty about what is waiting produces mild anxiety.
Checking resolves the uncertainty and produces immediate relief. The relief reinforces the
checking. The next round of uncertainty arrives faster.

That is a **compulsion in the technical sense** — not a moral failing and not a character defect —
and it is why willpower alone tends to fail. The relief is doing the teaching.

The way out is the same principle behind
[exposure and response prevention](/clinic/erp-for-ocd), the first-line treatment for OCD, scaled
right down: you allow the uncertainty to be there and you do not perform the ritual that
resolves it. The anxiety rises, then falls on its own. Repeat that enough times and the urge stops
commanding a response.

**We are not saying inbox-checking is OCD.** For the overwhelming majority of people it is an
ordinary habit running on an ordinary reinforcement loop. But the mechanism is the same, and the
intervention that works on the severe version works on the mild one.

**On the noticing-without-fighting part**, that is the core skill in mindfulness practice, and
there is a useful finding attached: across randomized trials, programme length and home-practice
volume were **not** significantly associated with outcomes, while programmes lacking a teaching
component produced smaller effects
([Strohmaier, *Mindfulness*, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12671-020-01319-4)). Ten taught
minutes a day is a reasonable dose — our article on
[meditation without sitting still](/journal/meditation-without-sitting-still) covers it.

## When this isn't enough

If the checking extends to things that cannot change — refreshing a page that updates weekly,
re-reading a sent message for errors, checking whether you locked something — and it is eating
real time or causing distress, that is worth taking seriously. Compulsive checking is a common
OCD presentation, it is highly treatable, and generic talk therapy is not the treatment. Our
[ERP article](/clinic/erp-for-ocd) covers what to ask for and why the acronym matters.

And if the underlying feeling is dread about what an email might contain rather than curiosity
about what it does contain, the useful question is what you are anticipating — which is usually a
specific person or a specific unresolved thing rather than the inbox itself.
