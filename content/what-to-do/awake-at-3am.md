You woke at three and your mind started working. Not on anything useful — on the same three
things, in the same order, with increasing conviction that they are worse than you thought.

Trying to stop thinking will not work. Give attention something else to do instead.

## Do this now

Stay lying down. Do not check the time. Do not check your phone.

1. **Bring your attention to your right thumb.** Just notice it — whatever sensation is there, or
   the absence of one.
2. **Move to the second finger. Then the third, fourth, fifth.** One at a time. Slowly.
3. **Then the palm. The back of the hand. The wrist. The forearm. The elbow. The upper arm. The
   shoulder.**
4. **Cross to the left thumb** and do the whole arm again.
5. **Then both legs, one at a time, toe by toe.** Then the torso, the back, the neck, the face.
6. **When you notice you have drifted back into thinking** — and you will, repeatedly — return to
   wherever you left off. The returning is the practice, not the staying.

Most people do not complete the sequence. That is a good sign, not a failure.

## If that doesn't fit

**If you are too agitated to lie still:** get up. Go to another room, keep the lights low, and do
something dull that is not a screen. Return when you feel sleepy. Lying awake in bed for an hour
teaches your brain that bed is where you lie awake.

**If you would rather listen to something:** guided yoga nidra recordings do exactly the sequence
above, in a voice, for free. Many people find the external voice easier to follow at 3am than
their own instruction.

**If your mind keeps returning to one specific unresolved thing:** write one sentence about it on
paper by the bed and put the pen down. Not a plan — a sentence. Then start the body sequence.

## Why it works

The practice above is yoga nidra, stripped of its framing. It is one of the few things in this
area that has been tested against a real comparator.

In a Delhi sleep laboratory, 41 people with chronic insomnia were randomized to either yoga nidra
or cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia — the treatment every clinical guideline names
first — and then slept with electrodes on their scalps. Both groups improved on total sleep time,
sleep efficiency and subjective quality. The yoga nidra group showed
[marked improvement in deep slow-wave sleep as a proportion of total sleep, and salivary cortisol
fell significantly](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34825538/) (Datta et al., *National Medical
Journal of India*, 2021).

A 2026 systematic review of randomized trials found six studies covering 244 participants, most
reporting improvements in sleep onset latency, total sleep time and sleep efficiency
([Dutta et al.](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/27683605251390728)).

We should be honest about the size of that: six trials and 244 people is a small evidence base for
a practice this popular, and the same research group's own review found that of 45 studies on
yoga nidra, only 13 used randomized group comparison and only five had an active control.

Why it plausibly works at 3am: rumination requires attention. A long, sequential, monotonous task
occupies it without demanding effort, and unlike "clear your mind," it gives you something to
actually do.

## When this isn't enough

**One clear thing:** if you are waking most nights, the first-line treatment is not a relaxation
practice. It is **CBT-I** — cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia — which every clinical
guideline recommends ahead of medication. The Delhi trial's own authors said as much while
reporting their positive result.

CBT-I is available through apps, through therapists, and increasingly through primary care. If
insomnia has lasted more than a month, ask about it by name.

Also worth ruling out, because they are common and treatable: sleep apnoea (snoring, waking
unrefreshed, daytime sleepiness), thyroid problems, alcohol — which reliably causes 3am waking —
and depression, which characteristically wakes people in the early hours.

And a note that belongs here rather than buried: **the intervention for most persistent sleep
problems happens in the morning, not at night.** Light exposure within an hour of waking is the
strongest available signal to your body clock about when the night should start.
