You noticed it just now: your breathing is high and shallow, up in the chest, and it has
probably been like that for hours. Your shoulders are somewhere near your ears.

This one is quick and you can do it at your desk.

## Do this now

1. **Put one hand flat on your chest and the other on your stomach**, just below the ribs.
2. **Breathe in through your nose for four seconds.** The hand on your **stomach** should move.
   The hand on your chest should barely move at all.
3. **Breathe out for six seconds.** Let the stomach hand fall.
4. **If the wrong hand is moving** — chest rising, stomach still — sit up straighter, drop your
   shoulders deliberately, and try again. Do not force it.
5. **Ten breaths.** About two minutes.
6. **Then roll your shoulders back and down once**, and carry on with what you were doing.

## If that doesn't fit

**If you can't get the diaphragmatic breathing to happen sitting:** lie on your back with knees
bent and a book on your stomach. Make the book rise. Two minutes on the floor is usually enough
to re-establish the pattern.

**If you're in an open-plan office:** you can do the whole thing without the hands. Sit up, drop
the shoulders, and count four in, six out. Nobody will notice.

## Why it works

Two separate things are happening and both are worth naming.

**The rate.** Slowing to roughly six breaths a minute increases parasympathetic control of the
heart — a meta-analysis of voluntary slow breathing found consistent increases in vagally-mediated
heart rate variability during practice and immediately after
([Laborde et al., *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*,
2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0149763422002007)). Lengthening the
exhale relative to the inhale increases the effect further
([Bae et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.13905)), which is why the counts are uneven.

Usefully, **five minutes is as effective as twenty** — a dose-response study comparing 5, 10, 15
and 20 minutes found no differences between them
([You et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph182312478)). And you do not need any device;
sessions without biofeedback equipment produced broadly similar effects
([Laborde et al., *Psychophysiology*, 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/psyp.13952)).

**The pattern.** Chest-high breathing recruits accessory muscles in the neck and upper chest that
are not designed for sustained work. Hours of it is a reasonable candidate for why your neck and
shoulders ache by evening, though we should be clear this is mechanically plausible rather than
demonstrated by trial evidence.

What we would not claim: that this "resets your nervous system," or that it does anything lasting.
It produces a transient increase in vagal activity, most pronounced during practice and shortly
after. That is a real and modest effect, and the language of resetting oversells it considerably.

## When this isn't enough

If you are genuinely breathless — at rest, on light exertion, or waking you at night — that is a
medical symptom and not a posture habit. See a doctor. Asthma, anaemia, thyroid problems and
cardiac conditions all present this way.

If deliberate breath control makes you anxious rather than settled, stop. This is a known
response, particularly in people with a history of panic, and it is not a failure of technique.

And if your shoulders are back at your ears within twenty minutes every time, the breathing is
downstream of something else — usually screen height, chair, or the fact that you are braced
against your workload. A three-minute reset will not fix an eight-hour posture. It will tell you
how often you need to notice.
