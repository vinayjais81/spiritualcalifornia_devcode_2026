You are not unhappy. You have simply stopped noticing them. They have become the person who
handles the recycling and knows the wifi password, and you cannot remember the last time you
looked at them and saw a person rather than a logistics partner.

## Do this now

**Resist the urge to have a conversation about it.** "I feel like we've lost something" is a
reasonable sentence that lands, almost always, as an accusation.

1. **Go for a walk together, fifteen minutes**, somewhere neither of you goes routinely.
2. **Leave the phones behind.** Both of you.
3. **Do not use it to discuss the relationship.** Or the children, or the house, or the calendar.
4. **Look outward** — at the view, the scale of things, whatever is large or unfamiliar. Talk about
   what you see if you talk at all.
5. **Do it weekly for a month.**

**And separately, once, alone:**

6. **Write down three things they have done in the past month** that you did not comment on at the
   time. Then tell them one of them.

## If that doesn't fit

**If a walk is impossible:** the mechanism is novelty and shared outward attention, not walking.
Anything unfamiliar that you do side by side rather than face to face will serve.

**If you cannot think of three things:** that is itself informative, and it is usually about
attention rather than about them.

## Why an attention exercise

The specific problem here is not conflict. It is that long familiarity makes people **predictively
invisible** — you stop perceiving and start retrieving, running a stored model instead of looking.

The relevant research is on **awe**, and it is more directly applicable than it sounds. Researchers
at UCSF and Berkeley randomized around sixty adults to eight weeks of weekly fifteen-minute
walks; one group was told to attend to the vastness and novelty of their surroundings, the other
simply to walk. The awe group reported
[**increased prosocial positive emotions — gratitude, compassion — and less daily
distress**](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/44/NCT03550144/Prot_000.pdf) (Sturm et al.,
*Emotion*, 2022). The study also tracked participants' selfies and found the awe group
progressively gave more of the frame to the landscape and less to themselves.

The proposed mechanism is the **small self**: awe reduces self-focus and turns attention outward.
Prosocial emotion is what it moves most reliably — which is precisely what has thinned out here.

**Two honest caveats.** The control group received no instruction at all, so some of the effect may
come from having any deliberate focus. And nobody has tested this on couples. We are extending a
finding, and labelling it as such.

**On the novelty**, the instruction to go somewhere new was part of the tested protocol, not a
flourish. Familiar routes are where the stored model runs.

## Why not to lead with the conversation

Long-term couples commonly fall into a **pursue–withdraw cycle**: one person raises the emotional
distance, which the other experiences as criticism, so they withdraw, which reads as confirmation,
so the first escalates. Neither chooses it and both are certain the other started it.

That cycle is what [emotionally focused couple
therapy](/clinic/emotionally-focused-couple-therapy) is built around, and its evidence — with good
maintenance of gains up to two years — is among the better bodies of work in couples therapy,
though the famous effect size of 1.3 is old, small and developer-authored.

Opening with *"I feel like we've lost something"* frequently starts that cycle rather than
interrupting it. Doing something together first, and noticing them out loud once, does not.

## When this isn't enough

**Flatness that has lasted years, or that comes with contempt rather than distance, is a different
problem** — and contempt specifically is the one to take seriously rather than wait out.

Couples therapy is considerably more effective than most people expect, and going before things
are severe is a much better bet than going as a last resort. Our
[EFCT article](/clinic/emotionally-focused-couple-therapy) covers what to look for, including
ICEEFT certification.

**If only one of you is willing to go**, individual therapy focused on the relationship is a real
alternative and can shift a cycle unilaterally.

**And if the flatness is in everything** — work, friends, things you used to enjoy — rather than
specifically in the relationship, consider that it may be depression rather than a relationship
problem. That distinction is worth getting right before making any large decisions.

**One firm line:** if there is violence, coercion or fear in your relationship, couples work is
not the right first step and can increase risk. Contact a domestic violence service or your doctor.
