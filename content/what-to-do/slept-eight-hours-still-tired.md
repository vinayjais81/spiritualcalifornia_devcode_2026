The hours were there. The tiredness is still there. This is a different problem from insomnia and
it usually has a different answer.

Two things to try this week — and then a question that matters more than either.

## Do this now

1. **Get outside within an hour of waking, for fifteen to thirty minutes.** Not the window —
   outside. No sunglasses for that stretch.
2. **Do it at the same time every day**, including weekends. Consistency of timing matters more
   than duration.
3. **Keep your wake time fixed**, even after a bad night. Sleeping in to catch up is the single
   most common thing that keeps this going.
4. **Give it a week before judging.** Circadian timing shifts slowly.

**And the second thing, which takes one minute:**

5. **Ask someone who has slept in the same room as you whether you snore, or whether you ever
   seem to stop breathing.** If you sleep alone, most phones will record audio overnight.

That question is on this page for a specific reason.

## If that doesn't fit

**If you cannot get outside in the morning:** stand at the brightest window you have for thirty
minutes, or use a 10,000 lux light box at the manufacturer's stated distance while you do
something else.

**If your sleep is fragmented rather than short:** you are probably waking repeatedly without
remembering it, which points more strongly toward the medical explanations below.

## Why it might be light

Bright light is the dominant signal your body clock uses to set itself. Specialized cells in the
retina project to the brain's master clock and are particularly sensitive to blue wavelengths
around 450 to 480 nanometres
([review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9511000/)).

**Timing decides the direction.** Morning light advances the clock, pulling sleep and wake
earlier. Evening light delays it.

If your clock is running later than your schedule, you can be asleep for eight hours at the wrong
biological time — which produces exactly this: adequate duration, inadequate restoration.

For scale, a bright office is roughly 300-500 lux. An overcast Bay Area morning outdoors is around
10,000. Stepping outside is almost always the highest-dose option available, and it is free.

## The part that matters more

**Unrefreshing sleep despite adequate duration is a recognised medical symptom.** If a week of
consistent morning light changes nothing, the useful next step is a doctor, not another technique.

The common causes, all detectable with straightforward tests:

- **Obstructive sleep apnoea.** Snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing, morning headache, waking
  unrefreshed, daytime sleepiness. Extremely common, badly under-diagnosed, and very treatable.
  It does not only affect people who are overweight, and it is frequently missed in women, whose
  presentation is often fatigue rather than classic snoring.
- **Thyroid dysfunction** — a single blood test.
- **Anaemia and low ferritin** — also a blood test, and a common cause of exhaustion in
  menstruating women.
- **Restless legs syndrome**, which fragments sleep without waking you enough to remember.
- **Depression**, which characteristically produces unrefreshing sleep and early-morning waking.
- **Alcohol**, which shortens sleep onset and reliably wrecks the second half of the night.
- **Medication side effects**, including some antidepressants, beta blockers and antihistamines.

**How to raise it:**

> *"I'm sleeping seven or eight hours and waking unrefreshed. Can we rule out sleep apnoea,
> thyroid problems and anaemia?"*

Naming sleep apnoea specifically is worth doing. Home sleep tests are now widely available and
far less onerous than a lab study, and this is the diagnosis most often reached years later than
it should have been.

## When this isn't enough

If you fall asleep unintentionally during the day — in meetings, at traffic lights, watching
television — that is not ordinary tiredness and it needs assessment promptly. Drowsy driving is a
genuine risk and worth taking seriously before it becomes an incident.

If your problem is actually difficulty *getting* to sleep or staying asleep, that is insomnia, and
the treatment is different: **CBT-I** is the guideline-recommended first-line option ahead of
medication, and most people are never offered it. See our
[article on CBT-I](/clinic/cbt-i-insomnia), which now includes an FDA-cleared prescription app
route reimbursed under Medicare.

And if the exhaustion comes with low mood, loss of interest, or a sense that nothing is worth
doing, treat that as the primary issue rather than a consequence of poor sleep. It responds well
to treatment.
