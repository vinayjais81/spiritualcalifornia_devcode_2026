Weeks are going past in a corridor of work, screens and logistics. Nothing is wrong exactly.
Nothing registers as large, either.

There is a fifteen-minute version of the fix and it has a randomized trial behind it. The whole
intervention is one instruction about where to look.

## Do this now

1. **Go for a fifteen-minute walk, outdoors, on foot.** Once this week is enough to start.
2. **Go somewhere new**, or take a familiar route in an unfamiliar direction. Novelty is part of
   the instruction, not a nice extra.
3. **Look outward, and look for scale.** The age of a tree. The distance of a hill. The size of
   the sky. Or the other direction — something small enough that its intricacy is surprising.
4. **Leave the phone in your pocket.** One photograph at the end if you want.
5. **Do not try to feel anything.** This is the part people get wrong. You are following a
   behavioural instruction about attention, not attempting to produce an emotion.

## If that doesn't fit

**If fifteen minutes is not available:** the instruction works on the walk you already take.
Change the direction and look up.

**If you are in a city with nothing scenic:** scale is available in architecture, in weather, in
the age of things. The research trigger is *vastness that doesn't fit your existing frame*, not
scenery.

## Why it works

Researchers at UCSF and Berkeley randomized around sixty adults aged 75 and over to eight weeks of
weekly fifteen-minute outdoor walks. One group was told to pay attention to the vastness and
novelty of the environment. The control group was told to walk, with no additional instruction.

The awe group reported increased daily prosocial positive emotions — gratitude, compassion — and
less daily distress across the eight weeks (Sturm et al., *Emotion*, 2022;
[trial protocol](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/44/NCT03550144/Prot_000.pdf)). The
study also tracked selfies participants took on their walks and found the awe group progressively
gave more of the frame to the landscape and less to themselves, with bigger smiles.

The proposed mechanism is what researchers call the **small self**: awe reduces self-focus and
orients attention outward. That is the useful part — it works by reducing the relative size of
your concerns rather than by solving them.

**One honest caveat.** The control group in that trial received *no* instruction, only a walk. So
some of the difference may come from having any deliberate focus at all rather than from awe
specifically. A randomized clinical trial published in *Scientific Reports* in 2025 subsequently
found awe reduced depressive symptoms and improved wellbeing
([full text](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-96555-w)), which strengthens the base
considerably.

**And one thing to know:** you do not need a mountain. The research consistently finds awe in
ordinary settings. Treating it as something requiring travel is both inaccurate and
self-defeating.

## When this isn't enough

Feeling small is beneficial in the context of this research — it correlates with generosity,
connection and less self-preoccupation. It is **not** the same as feeling insignificant, and in a
depressive state that distinction can be hard to hold. If this practice reliably leaves you
feeling worthless rather than connected, stop and talk to someone.

More broadly: if the flatness you are describing has lasted months, comes with disturbed sleep or
appetite, or you have stopped enjoying things you used to, that is worth a conversation with a
GP. Anhedonia is a symptom, not a lifestyle issue, and a fifteen-minute walk is not treatment for
it.

If it has lasted weeks rather than months and is more like drift than depression, the more useful
question may be about alignment rather than mood — what you are spending your days on versus what
you actually value. That is a different problem with a different answer.
