Something in your body has got ahead of you. Your chest feels tight, your thinking has sped up
and narrowed, and trying to reason your way out of it is not working.

**First, one thing worth knowing:** if this is new, severe, or accompanied by chest pain
spreading to your arm or jaw, breathlessness at rest, or fainting — stop reading and get medical
help. Anxiety and cardiac symptoms overlap, and it is not your job to tell them apart.

## Do this now

Start here rather than with breathing. The reason is below.

1. **Put both feet flat on the floor and press down.** Hard enough to feel your legs work. Hold
   five seconds. Release.
2. **Look around the room slowly and name four things you can see.** Out loud if you can, silently
   if you can't. Not categories — specifics. *The chipped mug. The blue cable. The window catch.
   The corner of the rug.*
3. **Name two things you can hear**, and one thing you can feel against your skin.
4. **Do it again** if the first pass didn't land. Most people need two.

This is called orienting. You are giving a narrowed, racing attention something external and
undemanding to rest on, rather than asking it to stop.

## If that doesn't fit

**If you would rather use your breath:** breathe out for longer than you breathe in. Four
seconds in, six out, five times. Do not breathe deeply — slow the rate, not the volume.

**But note the caution**, because most advice omits it: focusing attention on your breathing
makes some people's anxiety worse, particularly people with a history of panic. Breath is one of
the things panic makes you hypervigilant about, and attending to it can amplify rather than
settle. If you have tried breathing exercises before and felt worse, that is a known response and
not a failure on your part. Use the orienting instead.

**If you are somewhere you cannot do either:** cold water on the wrists and the back of the neck,
and walk somewhere, slowly, with a destination.

## Why it works

Orienting has less direct trial evidence than breathing does, and we would rather say so than
overstate it. What it has going for it is that it asks nothing of the system that is currently
overwhelmed and does not require you to control anything.

The breathing alternative has better evidence. Slowing to around six cycles a minute reliably
increases parasympathetic control of the heart, and lengthening the exhale increases the effect
further ([Laborde et al., 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0149763422002007);
[Bae et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.13905)). Five minutes is as effective as twenty,
and no device is needed.

The caution about breath focus is not ours either. In the largest blinded trial of high-ventilation
breathwork, reported side effects included anxiety arising from the pace of the breathing itself
([Fincham et al., *Scientific Reports*,
2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-64254-7)). Attending to respiration is not
neutral for everyone.

Neither technique will stop a panic attack in its tracks, and anyone promising that is selling
something. What they do is occupy the gap while it passes on its own, which it will — typically
within twenty to thirty minutes.

## When this isn't enough

This page is for a moment, not a condition. If any of the following is true, the useful next step
is a clinician rather than another technique:

- This has happened more than a few times.
- You have started avoiding places or situations in case it happens again.
- It wakes you from sleep.
- It is interfering with work, relationships, or leaving the house.

Panic disorder and generalized anxiety are among the **most treatable** conditions in medicine.
Cognitive behavioural therapy has a large evidence base, and so does medication. An eight-week
mindfulness course has been found non-inferior to a first-line antidepressant in a randomized
trial — which is a real option, and also a serious commitment of forty-five minutes a day
([Hoge et al., *JAMA Psychiatry*,
2023](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2798510)).

The point is that there are things that work properly, and a two-minute technique is not a
substitute for them. Start with your GP.
