You love them. You have also been slightly braced since the date was confirmed, and you know
exactly which conversation is going to happen and roughly when.

## Do this now

**Practical, first — this matters more than any inner work:**

1. **Decide your arrival and departure times now**, and say them out loud to someone. Open-ended
   visits are where most of this goes wrong.
2. **Plan one exit per day.** A walk, an errand, taking the dog, collecting something. Not an
   escape — a scheduled twenty minutes of being alone.
3. **Pick the two topics you will not discuss**, and decide your one-line deflection in advance.
   *"I'm not going to get into that this weekend — how's the garden?"* Rehearse it once.
4. **Decide what you actually want from the visit.** Usually it is not to win the argument, and
   naming that in advance makes it easier to not win it.

**Then, ten minutes, once before you go:**

5. **Sit, and bring to mind someone easy** — a friend, a child, a pet. Repeat silently: *may you be
   well, may you be at ease.*
6. **Then someone neutral**, then **the difficult person**. Same phrases. You do not have to mean
   it much.
7. **If it produces irritation rather than warmth, stop and go back to the easy person.** That is a
   documented response, not a failure.

## If that doesn't fit

**If ten minutes of well-wishing is not for you:** the practical list above is the load-bearing
part. Do that and skip the rest.

**If the visit is unavoidable and long:** the exits matter more, not less. Build them in before you
arrive rather than improvising when you need one.

## Why loving-kindness rather than reframing

The claim here is deliberately narrow, because the evidence is narrow.

Meta-analytic work on loving-kindness practice found **medium effects on daily positive emotions**
across 24 studies and 1,759 participants
([Zeng et al., *Frontiers in Psychology*, 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01693/full)),
and a separate analysis found significant reductions in **negative interpersonal attitudes** in
daily life.

That second one is the relevant finding: it moves how you regard other people, modestly.

**What it does not do**, and we cover this properly in our
[full article](/journal/loving-kindness-meditation): a 2022 meta-analysis of 23 studies found that
in pre-post designs it looked good (*g* = 0.312), and when restricted to randomized controlled
trials the life-satisfaction effect
[fell to 0.106 and lost significance](https://iaap-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/aphw.12367).

So: a modest, real shift in how you see someone. Not a transformation, and not a substitute for the
practical planning.

**One thing worth knowing before you try it.** A minority of people feel markedly *worse* directing
goodwill inward or toward a difficult person — particularly where there is unresolved anger, shame
or grief. This is documented well enough that the field built a **Fears of Compassion** scale to
screen for it, which our
[compassion-focused therapy article](/clinic/compassion-focused-therapy) covers. If it happens,
stopping is the right response, not pushing through.

## During the visit

**If something escalates**, the thing that works is a stated pause with a return time — *"I need
twenty minutes, I'll come back at four and we can finish this"* — rather than leaving the room. An
unexplained exit reads as abandonment and raises the temperature. Our
[mid-argument article](/what-to-do/mid-argument-pause) covers why.

**And breathe out longer than you breathe in** before the conversation you know is coming, not
during it. Five minutes, four in and six out.

## When this isn't enough

If you dread family contact for weeks beforehand and recover from it for weeks afterwards, that is
worth more than a coping plan. Therapy is genuinely useful for family patterns, and
[schema therapy](/clinic/schema-therapy) in particular works on exactly the long-standing
relational patterns that family reliably reactivates.

**And a limit worth naming.** Some family relationships involve genuine harm — ongoing
manipulation, abuse, or contact that reliably damages you. In those cases the useful question is
not how to cope better with the visit, but whether the visit should happen. Reducing or ending
contact with a family member is a legitimate choice, it is more common than people admit, and it
is worth working through with a therapist rather than alone.
