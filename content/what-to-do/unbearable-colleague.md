The way they say your name. The reply-all. The particular note in their voice when they ask
whether you have had a chance to look at it yet.

You are now spending real cognitive resource on someone you are not related to and did not choose.

## Do this now

**First, the unglamorous part:**

1. **Write down the specific behaviour**, once. Not "they're impossible" — *interrupts me in
   status meetings*, *sends critical feedback to the group rather than to me*. Specificity is what
   makes it addressable and it also shrinks it.
2. **Ask whether it is one thing or a pattern.** People are frequently unbearable for a season and
   then are not.

**Then, ten minutes, twice this week:**

3. **Sit, and bring to mind someone easy.** Repeat silently: *may you be well, may you be at ease.*
4. **Then someone neutral** — the person at the coffee place, a colleague two teams over.
5. **Then them.** Same phrases. You do not have to mean it.
6. **If it produces irritation, stop and return to the easy person.** That is a known response.

## If that doesn't fit

**If you cannot do it at all:** the alternative that works for some people is simply to find out
one true thing about their life outside work. It is the same mechanism — moving them from
obstacle to person — by a different route.

**If the behaviour is affecting your work rather than your mood:** that is a conversation or an
escalation, not a meditation. Skip to the last section.

## Why this rather than reframing

The evidence is modest and specific, and we are going to describe it accurately rather than
oversell it.

A meta-analysis of loving-kindness and compassion meditations found significant reductions in
**negative interpersonal attitudes in daily life** — *g* = −0.203 in randomized controlled trials,
and a much larger *g* = −0.539 in uncontrolled trials.

**Notice that gap**, because it is instructive. The uncontrolled figure is more than twice the
randomized one, which is the pattern this whole library keeps meeting. The honest number is the
smaller one.

Broader work found medium effects on daily positive emotions across 24 studies and 1,759
participants
([Zeng et al., 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01693/full)),
and — usefully for a busy person — **the length of practice and the duration of the intervention
did not influence effect sizes**, while programmes lacking a teaching component produced smaller
ones.

**What actually changes**, by most reports, is not that you like them. It is that they occupy less
of your attention when they are not in the room. That is a smaller claim than most articles on
this subject make and it is the one the evidence supports.

**And it does not work for everyone.** A minority of people feel worse doing this, particularly
where there is unresolved anger — documented well enough that the field built a
[Fears of Compassion scale](/clinic/compassion-focused-therapy) to screen for it.

## When it is not about your attitude

**Some colleagues are not a mindset problem.** If any of the following is happening, the answer is
documentation and escalation rather than meditation:

- Behaviour targeted at you that others do not receive
- Public criticism, exclusion from information you need, or credit taken for your work
- Anything touching a protected characteristic
- Behaviour that has been raised before and has not changed

**Keep dated notes as things happen**, with wording where you can. Not to build a case — because
memory reconstructs, and a contemporaneous record is worth considerably more than a recollection
six months later.

Then raise it, once, specifically, with your manager or HR: *"In the last month, X happened on
these three dates. I'd like it to stop."*

## When this isn't enough

If one colleague is affecting your sleep, your Sunday evenings, or your willingness to go in, the
issue has outgrown the technique. Our article on
[Sunday dread](/what-to-do/sunday-evening-dread) covers the wider version.

And if the situation involves bullying or harassment, that is a workplace and possibly a legal
matter rather than a personal resilience problem. Speak to HR, a union representative if you have
one, or an employment lawyer — and treat the effect on your mental health as a reason to see your
GP rather than something to absorb.
