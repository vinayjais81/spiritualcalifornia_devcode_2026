You are not going anywhere for eleven minutes and you have decided this is unacceptable. Your
jaw is tight, your grip has changed, and you have started narrating other people's driving.

## Do this now

**Eyes open. Attention on the road. This works fine while driving.**

1. **Unclench your jaw** and drop your shoulders once, deliberately. Most people are holding both
   without noticing.
2. **Loosen your grip on the wheel** to the minimum that is safe.
3. **Breathe in for four seconds, out for six.** Ten cycles, about a minute. Do not breathe
   deeply — slow the rate, not the volume.
4. **Turn off whatever is playing**, especially news or anything you have been half-arguing with.
5. **Look at one thing further away than the car in front.** The hills, the sky, a tree line.
   Anger narrows your visual field to the bumper ahead, and deliberately widening it interrupts
   that.

## If that doesn't fit

**If you are already too wound up to count:** just the exhale. Make every out-breath longer than
every in-breath for a minute and ignore the numbers.

**If this is a daily commute:** the intervention is not in the car. See below.

## Why it works

Slowing your breathing to roughly six cycles a minute increases parasympathetic control of the
heart, and lengthening the exhale relative to the inhale increases the effect further
([Laborde et al., 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0149763422002007);
[Bae et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.13905)). No equipment needed, and five minutes
performs the same as twenty
([You et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph182312478)).

**The looking-further-away part** has a softer basis, and we will label it as such. Research on
awe found that attending to vastness — scale, distance, sky — produces what researchers call the
**small self**: reduced self-focus and attention turned outward. In a randomized trial, adults
instructed to attend to vastness on short weekly walks reported more prosocial positive emotion
and less daily distress than a control group who simply walked
(Sturm et al., *Emotion*, 2022 —
[trial protocol](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/44/NCT03550144/Prot_000.pdf)).

Traffic anger is close to pure self-focus: *my* time, *my* lane, *my* schedule. Deliberately
looking at something large is the cheapest available correction, though nobody has tested it in a
car and we are not going to pretend otherwise.

## The thing worth noticing

**Traffic rage is very rarely about traffic.**

The reliable pattern people report is that the fury is proportional to what is waiting at the
other end — the meeting you are dreading, the evening you do not want, the day you have not
recovered from. The commute is where an emotion with no legitimate outlet finally finds an
acceptable target.

If you are consistently furious on the way *to* work, that is information about the job. If it is
consistently on the way *home*, it is usually information about what happened during the day, or
about what you are arriving into.

Either is more useful to know than a better breathing technique.

## When this isn't enough

**Anger that shows up in your driving is worth taking seriously as a safety matter.** Tailgating,
deliberate braking, accelerating to prevent someone merging, or getting out of the car are not
stress responses — they are risks, to you and to other people.

If any of that describes you, the underlying anger is treatable and worth treating.
[CBT](/clinic/what-cbt-actually-is) has good evidence for anger, and this is not a niche problem.

If the anger is escalating generally — at home, at work, at people who have not done anything —
that is worth a GP conversation. It is a common presentation of depression in men, which is
frequently missed because it does not look like sadness.
