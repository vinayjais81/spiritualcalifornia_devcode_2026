Our [wellness journal](/journal/mindfulness-meditation-eight-weeks) covers mindfulness as a
general practice, where the evidence is good but the claims made for it are frequently much
larger. MBCT is the clinical version, and it is different in one crucial respect: **it has a
specific indication.**

Not stress. Not wellbeing. **Preventing relapse in recurrent depression** — a problem where,
without ongoing treatment,
[as many as four in five people relapse at some point](https://www.ox.ac.uk/news/2016-04-27-mindfulness-based-cognitive-therapy-linked-reduced-risk-depression-relapse).

## What it actually is

Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy is an eight-week group programme combining elements of
cognitive therapy with mindfulness meditation drawn from MBSR. Its aim is described as giving
people
[portable skills that let them disengage from habitual maladaptive responses to negative mood and
stress](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.06.09.25329249.full.pdf).

The theoretical target is precise. In recurrent depression, small dips in mood reactivate the
thinking patterns of a past episode — a mild low triggers the machinery of a full one.
MBCT trains people to notice that reactivation early and relate to it differently, rather than
being carried by it.

**It is not therapy for a current episode**, at least not in its original form. It was designed
to be delivered to people in remission, as a way of staying well.

## What the research shows

### The anchor study

Kuyken and colleagues conducted an **individual patient data meta-analysis** pooling raw data from
**nine randomized trials** — a considerably stronger design than pooling published summaries.

People receiving MBCT had a
[**reduced risk of depressive relapse within a 60-week follow-up period: hazard ratio 0.69 (95% CI
0.58 to 0.82)**](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2517515) — roughly
**31% less likely to relapse**. The comparison included both usual care *and* other active
treatments, including maintenance antidepressants.

That last point is what gives MBCT its guideline standing internationally: it is a genuine
alternative or addition to maintenance medication, not merely better than nothing.

### The moderator finding, which inverts the received wisdom

Early trials restricted MBCT to people in full remission, on the assumption that intensive
mindfulness practice would be too demanding for anyone with current symptoms.

The IPD analysis found the opposite. **The more severe the baseline depressive symptoms, the more
benefit MBCT offered** — participants with lower baseline scores appeared to receive
[less benefit](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/301686138_Efficacy_of_Mindfulness-Based_Cognitive_Therapy_in_Prevention_of_Depressive_Relapse_An_Individual_Patient_Data_Meta-analysis_From_Randomized_Trials).

Effects on relapse prevention
[increase rather than decrease with the level of residual
symptoms](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.06.09.25329249.full.pdf), which fits the
theory: if mindfulness skills buffer the effects of negative mood, the buffer should be most
visible when there is more to buffer.

The practical consequence: **residual symptoms are a reason to be offered MBCT, not a reason to be
excluded from it.** The restriction has since been loosened, and research has extended to
difficult-to-treat depression.

### For current depressive symptoms, the picture is the familiar one

A separate meta-analysis of 13 trials (N = 1,046) in people with *current* symptoms found MBCT
superior to non-specific controls at post-treatment (k = 10, d = 0.71, 95% CI 0.47 to 0.96).

And against **other active therapies**:
[**d = 0.002 (95% CI −0.43 to 0.44)**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6687569/).

Two thousandths of a standard deviation. That is the most literal Dodo bird result in this entire
series — MBCT and other bona fide therapies performed, for practical purposes, identically on
current symptoms.

**Where the evidence stands.** Strong for relapse prevention in recurrent depression, where the
IPD design, the effect size and the guideline endorsement all hold. Weaker for treating a current
episode, where it matches other therapies rather than exceeding them.

## What it does not do

**It is not a treatment for a first episode of depression.** The indication is *recurrent*
depression, and the benefit concentrates in people with a substantial history.

**It has not been shown better than other therapies for current symptoms.** See above.

**It is not general mindfulness.** An eight-week MBCT group taught by a trained instructor is not
the same as an app, and the trials tested the former. Our journal's article on
[meditation dose](/journal/meditation-without-sitting-still) covers what the general mindfulness
literature does and does not support.

**And it is not a reason to stop medication.** MBCT has been studied as an alternative *and* as an
addition to maintenance antidepressants. Any change to medication is a decision for your
prescriber, made deliberately and usually with tapering — not something to attempt because a
course is going well.

**A note on adverse effects.** Research groups are now specifically studying negative effects of
MBCT in difficult-to-treat depression, which is the right thing to be doing. Our journal's
article on [meditation adverse events](/journal/silence-retreat-day-three) covers the broader
finding that meditation-related adverse events occur at roughly the rate reported for
psychotherapy generally.

## What the sessions actually involve

Eight weekly group sessions, typically two hours, with around 8 to 15 participants, plus a
full-day session, plus daily home practice.

**Early weeks** teach attention: the body scan, mindful movement, sitting with the breath. There
is very little discussion of depression as such. Many people find this frustrating — they came for
a depression treatment and are being asked to notice their feet.

**Middle weeks** introduce the cognitive element. You learn to see thoughts as mental events
rather than facts, and to recognize your own early-warning signs — the specific thoughts, moods
and behaviours that have preceded past episodes.

**Later weeks** build the relapse-prevention plan: what your signature warning signs are, what you
will do, whom you will tell.

**The three-minute breathing space** is MBCT's most portable tool — a brief structured practice
designed to be used at the moment mood starts to slide, rather than in a dedicated sitting.

Home practice is substantial, typically 30 to 45 minutes daily during the course.

## Is this available to me?

MBCT is reasonably well disseminated, and the group format makes it more available than
individual therapy.

**Where to look:** NHS-equivalent community services in other countries have adopted it widely; in
the US, look to academic medical centres, mindfulness centres affiliated with universities, and
integrated behavioural health programmes. The Oxford Mindfulness Centre and the University of
California mindfulness programmes are established sources of trained teachers.

**Ask about teacher training.** MBCT teachers should have both mindfulness teacher training and
mental health clinical training — the combination matters, because the material can surface
difficult states.

**Group format is the tested format.** Individual MBCT exists but the evidence base is on groups.

Billed as psychotherapy when delivered by licensed clinicians; some programmes are self-pay.

## How to ask for it

> *"I've had several episodes of depression and I'm currently well (or nearly). I'd like to be
> considered for MBCT for relapse prevention — the individual patient data meta-analysis found
> about a 31% reduction in relapse over 60 weeks, including against maintenance antidepressants.
> Is a group available?"*

And if you are told you have too many residual symptoms to join: the moderator evidence points the
other way, and it is reasonable to say so.

## Who this suits

MBCT is worth asking about specifically if:

- You have had **three or more episodes** of depression — this is the population where the
  evidence concentrates
- You are **currently well or nearly well**, and the question is staying that way
- You are on **maintenance antidepressants** and want either an addition or, with your
  prescriber's involvement, an eventual alternative
- You have **residual symptoms between episodes** — which, per the moderator finding, predicts
  more benefit rather than less

It suits less well someone in a severe current episode, someone unable to commit to daily home
practice for eight weeks, and anyone who wants individual rather than group work.

## When this isn't enough

If you relapse during or after MBCT, that is not a failure of the practice — a 31% risk reduction
is a meaningful shift in odds, not a guarantee. The relapse-prevention plan built during the
course is designed for exactly that moment, and it is worth using rather than abandoning.

For a current episode, [CBT](/clinic/what-cbt-actually-is),
[IPT](/clinic/interpersonal-therapy), [behavioural
activation](/clinic/behavioural-activation) and medication all carry evidence, and MBCT's own
data shows it performing comparably rather than better on acute symptoms.

And if you are in a severe episode with thoughts of harming yourself, this needs a clinician now
rather than an eight-week course.
