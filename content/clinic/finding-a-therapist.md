Most advice about finding a therapist is a list of directories. This one starts somewhere else,
because the research points somewhere else.

Across 295 studies and more than 30,000 patients, the quality of the working relationship between
therapist and client predicted outcome at
[r = .278, equivalent to d = .579](https://www.ovid.com/journals/psythy/fulltext/10.1037/pst0000172~the-alliance-in-adult-psychotherapy-a-meta-analytic).
Across decades of head-to-head trials, the *brand* of therapy predicted much less.

So the practical question is not "which modality?" It is: **how do I find someone I can work
with, confirm they are qualified, and tell within a few months whether this is helping?**

## Step 1: Understand the letters

In California, five credentials cover most therapy:

| Credential | What it is |
|---|---|
| **PhD / PsyD** (Licensed Psychologist) | Doctoral training; can conduct psychological testing |
| **LMFT** | Licensed Marriage and Family Therapist — the most common therapy licence in California |
| **LCSW** | Licensed Clinical Social Worker |
| **LPCC** | Licensed Professional Clinical Counselor |
| **MD / DO** (Psychiatrist) | Medical doctor; prescribes medication, may or may not offer therapy |

**Anything else is not a therapy licence.** "Certified life coach," "trauma-informed
practitioner," "somatic coach," "certified hypnotherapist" — these describe training, not
regulated practice. Some of those people are excellent. None of them are accountable to a state
board, and none can treat a mental health condition.

**Verify the licence.** California licences are searchable online through the Board of
Psychology and the Board of Behavioral Sciences. It takes ninety seconds. Do it.

## Step 2: The telehealth question, and why California is different

This is the most consequential recent change in access to therapy, and it works differently here
than the online-therapy marketing implies.

Under US rules, a therapist generally must be licensed **in the state where you are physically
located** at the time of the session — not where they are. Interstate compacts have been created
to ease this, and their status as of 2026 is uneven:

**PSYPACT** (Psychology Interjurisdictional Compact) lets eligible **psychologists** practise
telepsychology across participating jurisdictions. It is operational and widely adopted — but
[**California is not a participating state**](https://behavioralhealth.careers/blog/psypact-states-explained-telepsychology-in-2026/),
alongside New York, Massachusetts, Oregon, Hawaii, Alaska, Iowa, New Mexico and Louisiana.

**The Counseling Compact** covers LPCs and LPCCs. It is operational but
[only partially live — as of 2026, privileges are issuable in Arizona, Louisiana, Minnesota and
Ohio](https://therapyexpanded.com/licensure-compacts/). Many states have enacted the compact
without being live for privileges, which is a distinction that catches people out.

**The Social Work Licensure Compact** has been enacted in a growing number of states and is
moving toward implementation, but multistate licences are
[not yet being issued](https://telehealth.org/news/social-work-licensure-compact-2026-update/).

**There is no national compact for marriage and family therapists.** MFTs — again, the most
common therapy licence in California — have
[the fewest cross-state options of any profession](https://www.wellnesscollaborative.io/interstate-compacts-for-therapists/),
and generally need full licensure in each state.

**What this means for you, in one sentence:** if you are physically in California, your therapist
almost certainly needs a California licence, regardless of what an online platform implies. And
compacts reduce the licensing barrier, not the underlying law — the client's state scope of
practice still applies.

**One practical consequence people miss.** If you travel, or split time between states, ask your
therapist directly what happens to your sessions. If you move, ask early — this is a common and
avoidable interruption of care.

## Step 3: Match the treatment to the problem

The alliance evidence is real, but it does not mean any therapy suits any condition. For several
problems, a specific protocol substantially outperforms general talk therapy:

| If you have | Ask for |
|---|---|
| **PTSD** | A trauma-focused therapy — [EMDR](/clinic/emdr-ptsd-treatment), cognitive processing therapy, or prolonged exposure |
| **Chronic insomnia** | [CBT-I](/clinic/cbt-i-insomnia), not sleeping pills |
| **OCD** | Exposure and Response Prevention specifically — generic talk therapy can make OCD worse |
| **Panic disorder** | CBT with interoceptive exposure |
| **Depression** | CBT, [behavioural activation](/clinic/behavioural-activation), or IPT |
| **Recurrent depression, in remission** | MBCT for relapse prevention |
| **Eating disorders** | Specialist services — this is not general practice territory |

Ask a prospective therapist directly whether they deliver the protocol, and how many people with
your presentation they have treated.

## Step 4: What to ask in the first call

Most therapists offer a free fifteen-minute consultation. Use it.

- **"What's your licence, and what's your licence number?"** A straightforward question with a
  straightforward answer.
- **"Have you treated a lot of people with [this]?"**
- **"What approach would you take, and roughly how long?"** You want a specific answer, not
  "it depends on you."
- **"How will we know whether it's working?"** The best answer involves a measure and a review
  point.
- **"What do you do if it isn't working?"** A therapist who has a clear answer — adjust, consult,
  refer — is telling you something important.

And notice how the call felt. That is not a soft criterion. It is the variable with the larger
effect size.

## Step 5: Tell whether it's working

This is the part almost nobody explains, and it is where the alliance research becomes practical.

**Sessions 1-3: assessment.** You should leave with a shared understanding of what you are
working on. If after three sessions you cannot say what you are working toward, raise it.

**Ask for measurement.** Standardized measures — the PHQ-9 for depression, GAD-7 for anxiety, PCL-5
for PTSD — take two minutes and can be repeated every few weeks. **Routine outcome monitoring is
itself associated with better results.** A therapist who declines to measure anything is choosing
not to know.

**Weeks 6-8: first review.** Not "do I feel cured," but: *is anything moving?* Sleep, functioning,
the frequency of the worst symptom. Some improvement usually appears by this point in effective
treatment.

**The alliance has three parts, and you can audit them.** Agreement on **goals**, agreement on
**tasks**, and the **bond**. Most people only assess the third. If you like your therapist but
have no idea what the plan is, that is two-thirds of the construct missing — and it is fixable in
one conversation.

**Rupture is normal and repairable.** Feeling irritated with your therapist, or that they missed
something, is common. Saying so is often the most productive session you will have. A skilled
therapist welcomes it.

## When to change therapists

Reasonable reasons to leave, without guilt:

- Three months with no movement on anything, and no plan change
- You cannot be honest with them
- They will not tell you their approach, or dismiss your questions about it
- They talk about themselves more than briefly
- You have a specific condition and they will not deliver or refer for the specific treatment

**Non-negotiable reasons to leave immediately:** any sexual or romantic contact or suggestion of
it; pressure to invest, buy products, or join anything; discouraging you from seeing a doctor or
taking prescribed medication; disclosing your information without consent; or telling you your
illness is caused by your thoughts, energy or insufficient positivity.

The California Board of Behavioral Sciences and Board of Psychology take complaints.

**Changing therapists is not failure.** Given that the relationship is the strongest single
predictor of outcome, a poor fit is the most important variable going wrong — and persisting
through it is worse than starting again.

## If cost or waiting is the obstacle

- **Community mental health** and county behavioural health services operate on sliding scales.
- **University training clinics** offer supervised therapy at low cost, often with strong
  protocol adherence — trainees are frequently more rigorous about manuals than veterans.
- **Open Path Collective** connects people to reduced-fee therapy.
- **Employee Assistance Programs** typically cover a short course free, and most people never use
  theirs.
- **Guided self-help** is a legitimate step, not a consolation prize. Behavioural activation in
  particular has [good evidence delivered this way](/clinic/behavioural-activation).
- **Prescription digital therapeutics** exist for some conditions — see [our CBT-I
  article](/clinic/cbt-i-insomnia) for the insomnia route, now Medicare-reimbursable.

## One last thing

The most reliable finding in this entire series is that the relationship does more work than the
brand. That should be liberating rather than vague: it means you are allowed to choose someone you
can actually talk to, and you are allowed to leave someone you cannot.

It also means the single most useful thing you can do in therapy is say the awkward true thing —
including when the awkward true thing is about the therapy itself.
