Every article in this series about trauma treatment has run into the same number. Roughly half of
people do not finish. In the largest PTSD psychotherapy trial ever conducted, dropout was 56% for
prolonged exposure and 47% for cognitive processing therapy.

A treatment that works slightly less well but that people actually complete may deliver more
recovery in the real world than one that works slightly better and empties the room.

Written Exposure Therapy was designed around exactly that argument. **Five sessions. No homework.
Dropout of 6.3%.**

And the most recent US military guideline gives it a weaker recommendation than the treatments it
was found non-inferior to. That tension is the article.

## What it actually is

WET is a five-session, trauma-focused treatment developed by Denise Sloan and Brian Marx,
built out of James Pennebaker's expressive writing paradigm — which we cover in [its
own journal article](/journal/expressive-writing-pennebaker), and which on its own has only a
small effect.

WET is not that. It is a structured clinical protocol with specific instructions that change
across sessions.

**Session 1:** roughly an hour, mostly psychoeducation and rationale, then 30 minutes of writing
about the traumatic event in detail — what happened, and what you were thinking and feeling.

**Sessions 2-5:** approximately 40 minutes each, of which 30 minutes is writing. The instructions
shift: the earliest sessions focus on the event itself, later sessions on the meaning of it and
its impact on your life now.

The therapist reads nothing aloud and provides no line-by-line analysis. They give the
instruction, sit with you while you write, and afterwards ask a short set of questions about how
it went.

**There is no between-session homework at all.** That is a deliberate design feature, not an
omission.

## What the research shows

### Two non-inferiority trials

The first, published in *JAMA Psychiatry* in 2018, randomized **126 veteran and non-veteran
adults** with PTSD at a VA medical facility to WET or cognitive processing therapy
([Sloan, Marx, Lee & Resick](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1296451)). WET was
non-inferior.

The second, in *JAMA Network Open* in 2022, took the question to **169 active-duty military
service members** — a population where efficient treatment matters most — comparing five-session
WET against ten-session CPT. Again,
[**WET was non-inferior to CPT, and dropout rates for WET were significantly
lower**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8753496).

The authors' stated conclusion was that WET, as a more efficient approach, should be considered
for military service members.

WET has since also been found
[non-inferior to prolonged exposure](https://abpp.org/newsletter-post/written-exposure-therapy-a-brief-evidence-based-psychotherapy-for-ptsd/),
and has now been tested across roughly 17 trials including 7 RCTs.

### The dropout figure

This is the number that makes WET interesting. In the trial literature,
[**only 6.3% of WET participants dropped out, compared with over a third of CPT participants
leaving within the first five
sessions**](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230618618_Written_Exposure_as_an_Intervention_for_PTSD_A_Randomized_Clinical_Trial_with_Motor_Vehicle_Accident_Survivors).

Five sessions, no homework, and a protocol that does not require you to speak the trauma aloud
appears to be something people can finish.

### And one finding that is easy to miss

Researchers report that **the level of recovery reached by session five is not the best it will
be** — improvement continues after the treatment ends. The proposed reason is that WET's
mechanism differs from the other evidence-based protocols, with
[inhibitory learning and approach rather than avoidance as the
key](https://abpp.org/newsletter-post/written-exposure-therapy-a-brief-evidence-based-psychotherapy-for-ptsd/)
— you are learning that you can approach the memory, and that learning keeps generalizing.

### Why the guideline downgraded it anyway

The 2023 VA/DoD Clinical Practice Guideline reviewed each therapy individually rather than as a
class, applied GRADE more rigorously, and cut its strong recommendations from seven trauma-focused
therapies to three. **Written Exposure Therapy was downgraded from strong to a weaker
"suggest/weak for."**

Commentators objected. Their argument, as reported, was that
[no new evidence had called the downgraded therapies into question](https://www.usmedicine.com/clinical-topics/ptsd/critics-strongly-condemn-new-va-dod-guideline-on-ptsd-treatment/)
— rather, disproportionate research investment had gone into PE and CPT, so those accumulated the
trial volume that a stricter GRADE application rewards.

That is the honest reading. WET was not downgraded because it failed. It was downgraded because
it has fewer trials, and a rigorous evidence-grading system counts trials.

**Where the evidence stands.** Non-inferior to two first-line treatments in randomized trials, with markedly better
retention — and a smaller evidence base and a weaker current guideline position than the
treatments it matched.

## What it does not do

**It is not journaling.** The distinction matters as much as it does between sleep hygiene and
CBT-I. WET is a manualized protocol with session-specific instructions, delivered by a trained
clinician. Pennebaker's expressive writing paradigm, done alone, has a small and inconsistent
effect.

**It is not easier because it is shorter.** You will write about the worst thing that has happened
to you, in detail, five times. The absence of homework does not make the sessions gentle.

**It has not been shown superior to anything.** Non-inferior means not worse. The efficiency is
the advantage, not the effect size.

**And the evidence base is younger and smaller** than for PE, CPT or EMDR. That is the substantive
reason for its guideline position, and anyone recommending it should say so.

## What the sessions actually involve

You sit at a desk with paper and a pen. The therapist reads the instruction for that session,
sets a timer for thirty minutes, and stays in the room.

You write continuously. Spelling and grammar are explicitly irrelevant. If you finish before the
time, you start again.

Afterwards the therapist asks a handful of questions — how was that, what was hardest, what did
you notice. Then you leave. There is no assignment.

The five sessions are usually weekly, so the whole treatment runs about five weeks.

What people report: session one is the hardest and many describe leaving it shaken. By session
three the writing tends to become more detailed and less avoidant, and something shifts in how the
event is described — more sequence, less fragmentation.

## Is this available to me?

**This is the honest limitation.** WET is newer and less widely disseminated than PE, CPT or
EMDR, so trained providers are harder to find.

**Through the VA**, where much of the research was conducted and where dissemination is furthest
along. Ask specifically.

**Privately**, through clinicians trained in the WET protocol — Sloan and Marx have published the
manual and training is available. Ask whether they have completed formal WET training rather than
adapting expressive writing themselves.

**Via telehealth**, feasible given the format.

Billed as psychotherapy.

## How to ask for it

> *"I'm interested in Written Exposure Therapy for PTSD. I know it's five sessions with no
> homework and has been found non-inferior to CPT and prolonged exposure, with much lower
> dropout. Do you have anyone trained in it — and if not, what's the closest option?"*

If you are told it is not a recommended treatment, the accurate position is that the 2023 VA/DoD
guideline suggests it at a weaker strength than CPT, PE and EMDR — a suggestion for, not a
recommendation against.

## Who this is most likely to suit

Given everything above, WET is worth asking about specifically if:

- You have **already dropped out** of a longer trauma therapy, or expect you would
- You **cannot commit** to daily homework for two to three months
- **Speaking the trauma aloud** is the barrier that has stopped you starting
- Time, cost or scheduling is the binding constraint

For someone in that position, a five-session treatment with a 6.3% dropout rate and
non-inferiority data behind it is a serious option, and the guideline's cautious rating should not
be read as a warning against trying it.

## When this isn't enough

If five sessions have not produced change, remember that recovery in this protocol continues
after treatment ends — so a review some weeks later is more informative than a verdict at session
five.

If it has not helped at all, CPT, PE and EMDR all carry stronger guideline recommendations and
switching is a normal clinical decision.

Stabilization and safety come before trauma reprocessing if you are in crisis or in an unsafe
situation.
