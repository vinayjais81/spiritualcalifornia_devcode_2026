Every therapy in this series so far has landed in roughly the same place against CBT: comparable.
IPT tied it. ACT tied it. MBCT tied it to two thousandths of a standard deviation. The
[Dodo bird verdict](/clinic/common-factors-dodo-bird) has held with remarkable consistency.

Metacognitive Therapy is the exception — or claims to be. And whether that claim survives is the
most interesting open question in this cluster.

## What it actually is

MCT was developed by Adrian Wells and is grounded in the **self-regulatory executive function
model**, which holds that psychopathology arises from a perseverative thinking style called the
[**cognitive attentional syndrome**](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02211/full)
— the CAS.

The CAS has three parts: **worry and rumination**, **threat monitoring**, and **unhelpful coping
strategies** like avoidance and thought suppression.

The distinction from CBT is clean and is the whole point:

> [CBT focuses on the **content** of thoughts and invites the patient to reality-test that
> content. In MCT, the thinking **processes** are
> addressed.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5258745/)

MCT does not ask whether your worry is realistic. It treats the *act of worrying* as the disorder,
maintained by beliefs about worry itself — positive ones (*worrying keeps me prepared*) and
negative ones (*my worrying is uncontrollable and harmful*).

Change those beliefs, and the perseveration stops, regardless of what it was about.

**Attention Training Technique** is the model's signature exercise: a structured auditory
attention drill — selective attention, switching, divided attention — practised to build the
capacity to disengage from internal preoccupation.

**Detached mindfulness** is its other core skill: observing a thought without engaging it, without
evaluating it, and without doing anything about it. Notably, that includes not analysing it,
which is what most therapy asks you to do.

## What the research shows

### Against waitlists, the numbers are enormous

Normann and colleagues' meta-analysis found within-group effect sizes of
[**Hedges' g = 2.00 pre-to-post and 1.65 at follow-up**](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/da.22273)
across 384 participants with anxiety or depression, with metacognitions themselves shifting at
g = 1.18 and holding at 1.31.

A waitlist RCT in depression found controlled effect sizes of
[**d = 2.51 for depressive and 1.92 for anxious symptoms**, with 70-80% classified as recovered at
post-treatment and six months, against 5% of waitlist
patients](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5258745/). One-year follow-up from a
related trial reported
[67% recovered, 13% improved, 20% unchanged](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6694776/),
with 13% of those in remission relapsing.

### And against CBT

This is the claim that matters. A later meta-analysis compared MCT with cognitive and behavioural
interventions directly and found pooled effect sizes of
[**g = 0.69 at post-treatment (k = 8) and g = 0.37 at follow-up (k =
7)**](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02211/full),
in MCT's favour — alongside g = 2.06 against waitlists.

Their conclusion: MCT *may* be superior to other psychotherapies including CBT, and **more trials
with larger numbers of participants are needed before firm conclusions can be drawn.**

The earlier meta-analysis reached the same result with the same hedge — superior to waitlist and
to CBT, *although the latter finding should be interpreted with caution*.

### Why the caution is warranted

**Three reasons, and they compound.**

**The trials are small.** Eight studies in the CBT comparison. The waitlist RCT above had 39
patients.

**The effect shrinks with time.** g = 0.69 at post-treatment falls to g = 0.37 at follow-up. That
is the shape of an advantage that may be partly about speed rather than endpoint.

**And the developer is an author on a large share of the literature.** Wells appears on the
foundational trials, the meta-analyses' source studies, and much of the follow-up work. We flag
the same pattern in many other articles, including [EFT tapping](/journal/eft-tapping-examined),
[Transcendental Meditation](/journal/mantra-meditation) and
[grounding](/journal/grounding-earthing-examined). It does not make findings wrong. It means
independent replication carries disproportionate weight, and here it is thin.

There is also a live intellectual context worth naming. MCT researchers argue explicitly that
[CBT's effect has been overestimated and its superiority over other therapies is less clear than
once thought](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12443011/), making the case that new
treatments with larger effects are needed. That is a fair reading of the literature — our
[CBT article](/clinic/what-cbt-actually-is) covers the falling-effects controversy — but it is
also a motivated framing from a competing camp.

**Where the evidence stands.** A genuine and repeated superiority signal over CBT, from small trials with substantial
developer involvement, an effect that attenuates at follow-up, and meta-analysts who say
themselves that firm conclusions are premature.

## What it does not do

**It does not examine whether your thoughts are true.** For some people that is liberating; for
others it feels like the central question is being sidestepped.

**It has not been established as superior to CBT**, despite the pooled figures. The people who
produced those figures say so.

**It is not mindfulness.** Detached mindfulness overlaps in name only with mindfulness meditation.
There is no cultivation of present-moment awareness as a practice, no sitting, no daily
meditation.

**And it is not widely available**, particularly outside the UK and Scandinavia where most of the
research has been conducted.

## What the sessions actually involve

MCT is brief — typically 8 to 12 sessions.

**Early sessions** map your CAS. Not what you worry about, but how much time you spend worrying,
what you believe worry achieves, and what you do to control it. Many people find this
disorienting: the therapist keeps declining to discuss the content.

**Then the beliefs about thinking** are examined. Positive beliefs are tested — does worrying
actually improve outcomes, and can you find a case where it did not? Negative beliefs are tested
more directly, sometimes by deliberately postponing worry to a set period, which demonstrates
that it is controllable after all.

**Attention Training Technique** is practised in session and at home: usually around 12 minutes of
structured attention exercises with external sounds.

**Detached mindfulness** is taught as a stance rather than a practice — noticing a thought, and
choosing not to engage with it in either direction.

The absence of homework thought-records, and the refusal to litigate content, makes MCT feel
lighter than CBT. Whether that lightness is efficiency or avoidance is, again, the open question.

## Is this available to me?

**Availability is MCT's main practical problem in the US.** The research base is concentrated in
Norway and the UK, and trained practitioners are correspondingly scarce here.

**Where to look:** the MCT Institute maintains a directory of trained therapists. Some CBT
clinicians incorporate metacognitive techniques without delivering the full model.

**Group formats** exist and have been used for treatment-resistant depression.

Billed as psychotherapy.

## How to ask for it

> *"My main difficulty is rumination or worry — not so much what I think about as how much time I
> spend thinking. I'm interested in metacognitive therapy. Do you practise it, or know anyone who
> does?"*

If MCT is unavailable, that description still routes usefully: rumination-focused CBT and
[ACT](/clinic/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy) both target process rather than content, and are
far more widely available.

## Who this suits

MCT is worth asking about specifically if:

- **Rumination or worry is the presenting problem**, more than mood itself
- You have done CBT and found that **you can dismantle a thought and simply produce another one**
  — the content changes, the process doesn't
- You hold a **belief that worrying is useful**, or that it is uncontrollable
- You want a **short course** — 8 to 12 sessions is genuinely brief

It suits less well anyone whose difficulty is circumstantial, relational or grief-related, where
[IPT](/clinic/interpersonal-therapy) fits better, and anyone who would find the refusal to discuss
content frustrating rather than freeing.

## When this isn't enough

If MCT is unavailable or unhelpful, rumination-focused approaches within CBT and ACT cover
similar ground with much larger evidence bases and far better access.

And the general point holds: given that MCT's advantage over CBT attenuates from g = 0.69 to
g = 0.37 by follow-up, the practical difference between them for most people is likely smaller
than the headline suggests. Availability and fit should probably decide this one.
