A patient arrives at their doctor with low mood, isolation, chronic pain, and a life that has
contracted. There is no pill for most of that. Social prescribing is the formal answer: the
doctor refers them not to a specialist but to a **link worker**, who spends time understanding
what they need and connects them to something in their community — a walking group, a choir, a
gardening project, a class, a volunteering role.

It is a genuinely appealing idea. It has been adopted at national scale in the UK. And this
journal has to report that the controlled evidence is considerably weaker than the enthusiasm.

We are including this article partly because it is the closest formal analogue to what a wellness
marketplace does — and the honest reading has implications for both.

## What it actually is

**Three components:**

1. **A referral** from a GP, nurse or other health professional — sometimes a self-referral.
2. **A link worker** (also called a community connector or wellbeing coordinator) who meets the
   person, works out what matters to them, and helps them get to it. This is the distinctive part;
   without it, social prescribing is just a leaflet.
3. **A community activity** — the categories that appear in the research are
   [exercise-based or loneliness-prevention initiatives, coaching programmes, nature-based
   interventions, and dance or movement-based
   programmes](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1753435/full).

The theory is straightforward and plausible: a large share of what brings people to primary care
is social rather than medical, and medicine handles it badly.

## What the research shows

### The controlled evidence

The most rigorous synthesis is a systematic review of link workers following Cochrane EPOC
guidance. It included **eight studies with 6,500 participants** — five randomized controlled
trials at low risk of bias, three controlled before-after studies at high risk.

The results:
[**four studies (n = 2,186) reported no impact on health-related quality of life; four (n = 1,924)
reported mental health outcomes, with three reporting no
impact**](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36253037/).

The same research team, writing later, put their own conclusion plainly: they identified only
eight controlled studies, found no cost-effectiveness evaluations, and
[**concluded that there was very limited high-quality evidence to support the effectiveness of
social prescribing**](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11212363/).

A separate exploratory randomized trial of link workers for people with multimorbidity in
deprived urban communities found the intervention was
[generally perceived as safe, deemed acceptable by many participants, and regarded as highly
beneficial by a subset — but reported **no statistically significant improvements in
health-related quality of life, mental health, or
loneliness**](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1753435/full).

Hold on to that sentence. It is the shape of the whole field: people like it, a subset find it
valuable, and the measured outcomes do not move.

### The wider literature is enthusiastic and much weaker

A systematic review assessing social prescribing's effect on loneliness, isolation, wellbeing and
connectedness reviewed **51 studies** and reported something startling:
[**exactly one peer-reviewed study presents a randomised controlled
trial**](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8156788/). Several studies did not
distinguish between core concepts or report what measures they used.

A review focused specifically on loneliness found nine eligible articles from 4,415 citations,
which
[did not use uniform measures or randomised samples — so no meta-analysis was
possible](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34159848/). All nine reported positive individual
impacts.

That combination — universally positive uncontrolled reports, near-absent randomized evidence —
is a pattern this journal has met many times, including in
[reflexology](/journal/reflexology-foot-map) and [Reiki](/journal/reiki-two-reviews).

### The economics, where the case is usually made

Because the clinical outcomes are unconvincing, advocacy for social prescribing frequently rests
on cost.

A 2026 systematic review of the international health economics evidence found **18 studies**, of
which five were RCTs. Social Return on Investment analyses reported
[consistently positive returns ranging from **£1.17 to £7.08 per £1
invested**](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1753435/full).

But the same review's conclusion is the important part: use of standard economic methods and
tools was limited, and
[**robust economic evidence on social prescribing remains limited** — established health economic
methods are rarely applied, limiting the usefulness of existing studies for healthcare planning
and
commissioning](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12891220/).

Social Return on Investment is a legitimate technique and a permissive one. A £7-per-£1 figure
from an SROI analysis is not equivalent to a cost-effectiveness result from a randomized trial,
and it should not be quoted as though it were.

**Where the evidence stands.** A plausible, popular, widely implemented idea with high acceptability
and consistently positive uncontrolled reports — and controlled trials that mostly show no effect
on the outcomes they measured.

## What it does not do

**It has not been shown to improve quality of life or mental health** in the controlled evidence.
That is the central finding and it should not be softened.

**It is not a substitute for treatment.** If someone has a diagnosable condition with an
evidence-based treatment, a referral to a gardening group is not that treatment. Social
prescribing works alongside care, not instead of it.

**And there is a structural risk worth naming.** Social prescribing is attractive to health
systems partly because it is cheap and moves demand out of primary care. Where community
provision is underfunded, "referral to community activity" can become a way of discharging people
into a service that does not exist. The link worker is the component that prevents this, and the
link worker is the component most likely to be cut.

## What might actually be happening

The honest possibility is that social prescribing works — for some people, sometimes — through
mechanisms the trials are poorly designed to detect.

The trial evidence measures health-related quality of life and standardized mental health scales
across whole referred populations. But the qualitative findings consistently report that a
**subset** find it highly beneficial, and reviews note that
[individuals and service providers view it as a helpful tool](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34159848/).
Averaging a large effect in a few people across a heterogeneous population produces exactly the
null results seen here.

There is also a matching problem. "Social prescribing" is not one intervention — the same
category covers a choir, a walking group, a coaching programme and a gardening project. Our
articles on [group drumming](/journal/group-drumming-cortisol) and
[group therapy](/clinic/group-therapy) both suggest that *the group* is a real mechanism. Whether
a specific person gets to a group that fits them is probably the whole ballgame, and it is not
what these trials measured.

That is a hypothesis, not a finding, and we are labelling it as such.

## What it involves in practice

**The referral conversation** is brief — a GP notices that the problem is social and offers the
route.

**The link worker meeting** is the substantive part, and typically runs 45 minutes to an hour.
Good link workers ask what someone used to enjoy, what they have stopped doing, and what is
actually in the way — transport, money, confidence, care responsibilities.

**Then the connection**, which is where quality varies enormously. A name and a phone number is a
weak intervention. Going with someone the first time is a much stronger one, and services that do
this report better attendance.

**Follow-up** over several weeks to a few months.

## Is this available to me?

**In the US there is no national programme**, so this exists in fragments and under different
names.

**Where to look:**

- **Community health workers** and **patient navigators** attached to primary care or health plans
  — functionally the same role
- **Area Agencies on Aging**, which do exactly this for older adults
- **Hospital community health programmes** and Medicaid-funded care coordination
- **211**, the national referral line, which is the closest free equivalent to a link worker
- Some **Medicare Advantage** plans now fund social-needs support

**Ask your GP:** *"Is there a community health worker or care navigator here?"* Many practices
have one and never mention it.

## Where this leaves a wellness marketplace

We should be straightforward, since this site is one.

Social prescribing is the healthcare system's formal version of connecting people to community
activities for wellbeing. The controlled evidence for it is weak. It would be inconsistent of us
to report that honestly and then imply that the same activities work better when someone finds
them through a website.

What the evidence does support:

- **The activities themselves** often have their own evidence — [group
  drumming](/journal/group-drumming-cortisol), [tai chi](/journal/tai-chi-evidence),
  [yoga](/journal/yoga-evidence-base), [walking](/what-to-do/attention-narrowed) and
  [group therapy](/clinic/group-therapy) are all covered in this library on their own merits.
- **Being connected to other people** recurs as a mechanism across this journal more often than
  almost anything else.
- **Getting to the thing** is the hard part, and the link-worker research suggests that
  accompaniment beats a phone number — which is also, precisely, the finding from our
  [guided self-help article](/clinic/guided-self-help).

What it does not support is the claim that referral to community activity reliably improves
mental health outcomes at population scale. It has been tested at national scale, and it largely
did not.

## When this isn't enough

If you have a diagnosable condition, social prescribing is an addition rather than a treatment.
The [treatments in this series](/clinic/finding-a-therapist) are where to start.

If you are isolated and a group has been suggested, the practical questions are the ones the
research points to: is there someone who will help you get there the first time, and is this
group actually a fit?

And if you are in crisis, this is not the pathway. Contact your doctor, a crisis line, or
emergency services.
