Depression makes problems feel unsolvable, so you stop trying to solve them, so they accumulate,
so the depression deepens. Problem-Solving Therapy interrupts that at the most literal possible
point: it teaches problem-solving as a procedure.

It is brief, structured, deliverable by non-specialists, and endorsed by the World Health
Organization's Mental Health Gap Action Programme for exactly that reason. It is also the
treatment in this series with the widest gap between its best and worst versions — and that gap
matters, because health systems tend to scale the weaker one.

## What it actually is

PST teaches a sequence and then has you run it on your actual life:

1. **Identify and define the problem** — specifically. Not "my job is awful" but "I have three
   deadlines this week and no plan."
2. **Set a realistic, achievable goal.**
3. **Generate multiple solutions** — deliberately including bad ones, because the constraint in
   depression is usually the narrowing of options, not the quality of them.
4. **Weigh the options.**
5. **Choose one and make a concrete plan** — what, when, where.
6. **Do it, then review what happened**, and revise.

The theoretical claim is that depression impairs the capacity to solve problems, and that
restoring the capacity restores mood and function. The practical claim is that a structured
procedure works better than trying harder.

**Three versions exist and the research distinguishes them:**

- **Extended PST** — 10 or more sessions, usually group, addressing not only the skills but the
  attitudes and beliefs that stop people using them.
- **Brief PST (PST-PC)** — developed for primary care, focused on the core steps, and
  [deliverable by trained nurses](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-psychiatry/article/problemsolving-therapy-for-adult-depression-an-updated-metaanalysis/812B78E160F488F8B859C0EA800B7B48).
- **Self-examination therapy** — a distinct variant emphasising re-evaluating what actually
  matters and disengaging from unattainable goals.

## What the research shows

Cuijpers and colleagues' updated meta-analysis in *European Psychiatry* pooled studies covering
**3,357 participants** and confirmed PST as an effective treatment for adult depression.

**In older adults the evidence is particularly clear.** A meta-analysis of nine studies with 569
participants — mean ages between 65 and 80 — concluded
[PST is an effective treatment for older people with major depressive disorder, deliverable by a
variety of healthcare practitioners in different
formats](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1064748114005053). Treatment ran
5 to 12 sessions.

A separate systematic review examined PST for
[primary care patients with depressive and/or anxiety
disorders](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK74902/), and PST has been studied in
post-stroke depression, where five of eight RCTs across 1,249 patients suggested benefit — though
four found no effect on functioning.

### The finding that should shape how you ask for it

Here is the uncomfortable part. In an earlier Cuijpers meta-analysis, the primary-care version —
PST-PC, the brief one designed for scale — showed
[**no significant effect on depression (d = 0.14) compared with control
conditions**](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/6605382_Problem_solving_therapies_for_depression_A_meta-analysis).
Self-examination therapy, by contrast, was far more effective.

The same authors were careful about it: heterogeneity was very high in almost all analyses, and
subgroup analyses did not identify what caused it. Their conclusion was that PST has
**varying effects on depression, and it is not known what determines whether those effects are
large or small**.

That is an unusually honest thing for a meta-analysis to say, and it is the most useful sentence
in this literature. PST is not a reliably-dosed treatment. Two people receiving "PST" may receive
substantially different things.

**Where the evidence stands.** Consistent evidence that PST works for adult and particularly older-adult depression,
with high heterogeneity, unclear moderators, and at least one analysis in which the most widely
deployable version did not separate from control.

## Why health systems love it, and what to watch for

PST is endorsed by the WHO's mhGAP programme and is
[explicitly suited to task-sharing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/322506526_Problem-solving_therapy_for_adult_depression_An_updated_meta-analysis)
— delivery by trained non-specialists rather than psychologists. In settings with no psychiatrists
for hundreds of miles, that is a genuine public health achievement and this article is not an
argument against it.

But the same property makes PST attractive to systems for reasons that have nothing to do with
you: it is cheap, short, and does not require a specialist. When a service offers you four brief
sessions of problem-solving with a practice nurse, two things may be happening at once — you are
being offered a legitimately evidence-based treatment, *and* you are being offered the cheapest
one.

The [COBRA trial](/clinic/behavioural-activation) raised the same tension for behavioural
activation. The difference is that COBRA showed BA delivered by junior workers was non-inferior
to CBT delivered by experts. The PST literature does not contain an equivalent result for the
brief version.

## What it does not do

**It does not address why you feel unable to solve problems.** Extended PST does, which may be
part of why it performs better. The brief version teaches the steps and assumes the barrier is
skill.

**It does not suit problems that cannot be solved.** For grief, terminal illness, or an
irreversible loss, a procedure for generating solutions can feel insulting. Self-examination
therapy's emphasis on disengaging from unattainable goals is the more appropriate relative here,
and [IPT's grief work](/clinic/interpersonal-therapy) is better matched still.

**It has not been shown superior to other therapies**, and the heterogeneity means it is harder
to predict who it will help than it is for CBT or IPT.

## What the sessions actually involve

Four to twelve sessions, typically 30 to 60 minutes, often in primary care.

**Session one:** explanation of the depression-and-problem-solving link, and a problem list. Most
people arrive with a vague global sense of everything being wrong and leave with six specific
items, which is itself often a relief.

**Then, each session:** pick one problem. Define it in a sentence. Set a goal that is achievable
this week. Brainstorm — the therapist will push you to keep generating options past the point of
comfort, including impractical ones, because the narrowing of perceived options is the target.
Weigh them. Pick one. Write a plan with a day and a time.

**Next session:** what happened. Not "did it work" but what actually occurred, and what that tells
you. Then repeat with the next problem.

By the end, the intended outcome is that you run the sequence yourself.

## Is this available to me?

Availability is PST's strength. Because it can be delivered by nurses, social workers and trained
non-specialists, it appears in primary care, integrated behavioural health, older-adult services,
and stepped-care programmes where waits for specialist therapy are long.

**Worth asking about specifically if** you are older, in primary care, or facing a long wait for
CBT or IPT.

**In California**, integrated behavioural health in primary care settings is the most likely
route. Ask your GP whether the practice has a behavioural health clinician.

Billed as psychotherapy where delivered by a licensed clinician.

## How to ask for it — and the question that matters

> *"Is problem-solving therapy available here? How many sessions, and is it the brief
> primary-care version or an extended course?"*

That second question is the point of this article. If the answer is a short brief-format course,
it is still worth doing — particularly while waiting for something else — but you should know that
the evidence for that specific version is weaker than the evidence for PST generally.

And if you are offered brief PST *instead of* a fuller therapy you were waiting for, it is
reasonable to ask to stay on the waiting list as well.

## When this isn't enough

If your problems are genuinely unsolvable rather than unsolved, PST is the wrong tool and a
different focus is indicated.

If four to six sessions have not moved anything, the alternatives with comparable or stronger
evidence — [CBT](/clinic/what-cbt-actually-is), [behavioural
activation](/clinic/behavioural-activation), [IPT](/clinic/interpersonal-therapy) — are all
reasonable next steps, and asking to step up rather than repeat is a normal request.

For severe depression, or if you are having thoughts of harming yourself, this needs a clinician
now rather than a self-management protocol. Contact your doctor or emergency services.
