Two things are being sold in the United States under closely related names, and most people
cannot tell them apart. That confusion is the reason this article exists.

**Esketamine** — brand name Spravato — is an FDA-approved nasal spray, administered only in
certified healthcare settings, with two hours of monitoring after every dose, and covered by
Medicare and most major commercial insurance.

**Racemic ketamine**, given as an IV infusion in independent clinics, is FDA-approved as an
**anaesthetic**. Its use for depression is off-label.

Off-label prescribing is legal, common and often entirely appropriate. But the two products carry
different evidence, different oversight and different safeguards, and they are frequently
marketed as interchangeable.

## What they actually are

Both act primarily as **NMDA receptor antagonists**, affecting the glutamate system and
downstream synaptic plasticity — a mechanism entirely different from the serotonin pathway used
by SSRIs. Both are **Schedule III** controlled substances.

**IV ketamine** is the racemic mixture of R- and S-enantiomers, delivered by infusion, typically
40 minutes, usually six infusions over two to three weeks.

**Esketamine** is the S-enantiomer, formulated as an intranasal spray.

The Spravato approval is described as
[the first truly novel mechanism for depression in decades](https://www.lumin.health/blog/johnson-johnson-spravato-fda-approval-a-new-path-for-ketamine-for-depression),
which is not marketing — it is the first genuinely new pharmacological route in psychiatry since
the SSRIs.

## The regulatory picture, as of the date above

### Esketamine (Spravato)

FDA-approved for **two indications**: treatment-resistant depression — defined as failure of two
adequate antidepressant trials in the current episode — and **major depressive disorder with
acute suicidal ideation or behaviour**.

In **January 2025**, the FDA approved a supplemental application making Spravato
[the first and only **monotherapy** for adults with major depressive disorder who have had an
inadequate response to at least two oral
antidepressants](https://www.jnj.com/media-center/press-releases/spravato-esketamine-approved-in-the-u-s-as-the-first-and-only-monotherapy-for-adults-with-treatment-resistant-depression)
— meaning it no longer has to be taken alongside an oral antidepressant. Approval followed
Priority Review, on data showing it met its primary endpoint at four weeks with improvement
against placebo as early as 24 hours.

**The REMS programme is the part that matters practically.** Spravato is dispensed and
administered only at REMS-certified centres, with
[a two-hour post-dose monitoring window for sedation and dissociation](https://www.lumin.health/blog/johnson-johnson-spravato-fda-approval-a-new-path-for-ketamine-for-depression)
at every session. You cannot take it home, and you cannot drive afterwards.

### Racemic ketamine

Approved as an **anaesthetic**. Not FDA-approved for any psychiatric disorder. Use for depression
is
[off-label, guided by clinical studies and expert consensus rather than a formal psychiatric
indication](https://advancedpsychiatryassociates.com/resources/blog/psychiatric-guide-trd-treatment-esketamine-vs-ketamine),
and consequently there is no REMS programme attached to it.

**And a specific warning worth carrying:** the FDA has warned about the risks of
[**compounded ketamine products**, including nasal and oral formulations used for psychiatric
conditions, emphasising that these compounded drugs are not FDA-approved](https://advancedpsychiatryassociates.com/resources/blog/psychiatric-guide-trd-treatment-esketamine-vs-ketamine).
At-home ketamine delivered by mail is the highest-risk version of this treatment and the least
supervised.

## What the research shows

The efficacy question — does ketamine work for treatment-resistant depression — is answered more
confidently than the comparison question.

**Esketamine has the more robust trial data**, which follows from having gone through the
approval process. Reviewers note that
[while there is more robust clinical trial data for intranasal esketamine, its high cost
frequently precludes its use](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10801061/).

**Head-to-head RCTs between IV ketamine and intranasal esketamine are lacking.** What exists are
meta-analyses of observational studies. One showed
[**no difference in efficacy up to one month**; two more recent studies suggested that while each
had similar response and remission rates, **IV ketamine required fewer treatments** to achieve
it](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10801061/).

That is a genuinely useful and under-reported finding: comparable outcomes, possibly fewer
sessions with IV — from observational data, not randomized comparison.

**The speed is the distinctive property.** Both act within hours to days rather than the four to
six weeks typical of oral antidepressants. For someone with acute suicidal ideation, that
difference is not a convenience.

**Where the evidence stands.** Esketamine is FDA-approved on the basis of randomized trials, with
a defined indication, defined dosing and mandatory monitoring. IV ketamine has supportive
observational and trial evidence for depression, no psychiatric approval, no standardized
protocol, and no mandated safeguards. The treatments are probably comparably effective. They are
not comparably regulated.

## What they do not do

**They are not a cure, and the effect is not permanent.** Repeated dosing or maintenance is
usually required, and relapse after stopping is common. Nobody should enter this expecting one
infusion to resolve a depression.

**They are not first-line.** The indication is depression that has not responded to two or more
adequate antidepressant trials.

**And they are not a substitute for psychotherapy.** Rapid symptom relief creates a window;
what happens in that window matters. Our article on [finding a
therapist](/clinic/finding-a-therapist) covers the other half of the work.

### Safety and contraindications

**Documented medical contraindications** include
[aneurysmal vascular disease, arteriovenous malformation, history of intracerebral haemorrhage,
and hypersensitivity to esketamine or ketamine](https://baltimoreketamineclinic.com/spravato-resistant-depression-maryland/).
Active substance use disorders may require evaluation or stabilisation first.

**Expected acute effects:** dissociation — a sense of detachment from body or surroundings —
sedation, raised blood pressure, nausea, and altered perception. These are why the two-hour
monitoring window exists in the REMS protocol, and why the absence of an equivalent requirement
in off-label settings is worth asking about.

**Abuse potential is real.** Ketamine is a Schedule III controlled substance with recognised
recreational use and dependence potential. Long-term frequent use is associated with bladder and
urinary tract damage. Anyone with a substance use history needs this discussed openly rather than
avoided.

**Bipolar disorder** requires a proper diagnostic assessment first, as with any antidepressant
treatment.

## What a session involves

**Spravato:** you self-administer the nasal spray under supervision at a certified centre, then
stay for two hours while blood pressure and mental state are monitored. Dissociation typically
peaks around 40 minutes and settles well before you leave. You cannot drive home — this is not
optional, and you need to arrange it.

Schedule: twice weekly for four weeks, then weekly, then weekly or fortnightly for maintenance.

**IV ketamine:** an infusion over roughly 40 minutes in a reclining chair, with monitoring.
Dissociation is generally more pronounced than with the nasal spray. Typically six infusions over
two to three weeks, then maintenance as needed.

**What people report:** the dissociative experience varies enormously — some find it pleasant or
interesting, some find it frightening. Being told in advance what to expect substantially changes
how it is experienced, and a good clinic prepares you.

Mood change, where it happens, often arrives within 24 hours and can feel abrupt in a way people
find disorienting.

## Cost and coverage — the practical difference

**Spravato** is
[covered by Medicare and most major commercial insurance](https://www.lumin.health/blog/johnson-johnson-spravato-fda-approval-a-new-path-for-ketamine-for-depression),
which is one of the strongest practical arguments for it.

**IV ketamine** is generally **not covered**, because it is off-label. Expect $400-$800 per
infusion in California, with an initial course of six running $2,400-$4,800 out of pocket, plus
maintenance.

So the choice frequently comes down to cost and access rather than to evidence — with the
counter-intuitive result that the **approved, monitored, insured option is often the cheaper
one**. Many people assume the opposite.

## How to ask about it

To a psychiatrist:

> *"I've had inadequate response to [number] antidepressants. Am I a candidate for esketamine?
> Is there a REMS-certified centre you can refer me to, and would my insurance cover it?"*

To any ketamine clinic:

- **"Is this FDA-approved esketamine under REMS, or off-label racemic ketamine?"** This is the
  single most important question and you are entitled to a direct answer.
- **"Who supervises, and what's their specialty?"** Psychiatric involvement matters. Anaesthesia
  training alone does not cover the psychiatric assessment.
- **"What monitoring happens, and for how long after dosing?"**
- **"What's the plan after the initial course?"** A clinic without a maintenance and relapse plan
  is selling infusions rather than treating depression.
- **"Do you require a psychiatric assessment before starting?"** The correct answer is yes.

**Avoid at-home compounded ketamine by mail.** The FDA has warned about exactly this, there is no
monitoring, and the safeguards built into both legitimate pathways are entirely absent.

## Who this suits

Worth discussing with a psychiatrist if:

- You have **treatment-resistant depression** — two or more failed adequate antidepressant trials
- You have **major depressive disorder with acute suicidal ideation**, where speed of onset is the
  point and esketamine carries a specific approved indication
- You **cannot tolerate** conventional antidepressants
- You can arrange **transport home** and attend regularly

It suits poorly anyone with the contraindications above, anyone with active untreated substance
use disorder without stabilisation, and anyone hoping for a single-session fix.

## When this isn't enough

If ketamine or esketamine does not help, or the benefit does not hold, other routes remain:
[rTMS](/clinic/rtms-depression), medication combinations, and ECT — which despite its reputation
remains the most effective treatment available for severe treatment-resistant depression.

If it does help, the important question is what you do with the window. Rapid relief is an
opportunity to engage with psychotherapy, restart activity and rebuild, not an endpoint.
