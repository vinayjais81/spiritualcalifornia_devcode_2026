If PTSD were only a fear problem, exposure would be the whole answer. But a great deal of what
keeps people stuck after trauma is not fear. It is **what they concluded**.

*I should have seen it coming. If I'd done something differently. It happened because of what I
am. Nobody can be trusted.*

Cognitive Processing Therapy targets those conclusions. It is the third of the three
trauma-focused therapies carrying a **"Strong For"** recommendation in the 2023 VA/DoD guideline,
and it is the one most people have never heard of.

## What it actually is

CPT is a structured 12-session cognitive therapy built around what Patricia Resick called **stuck
points** — beliefs formed at the time of the trauma that have not been revisited since, and that
now organize how a person sees themselves and the world.

The treatment does not aim to make you think positively. It aims to make you examine whether a
conclusion drawn during the worst hour of your life, under extreme stress, with incomplete
information, still holds up when looked at carefully.

The work happens through **worksheets** — a sequence of structured forms that move from
identifying a stuck point, to examining the evidence for and against it, to considering
alternative accounts, to arriving at a more balanced belief. It sounds mechanical, and to a degree
it is. That structure is what makes it teachable, deliverable at scale, and testable.

**The key practical distinction from prolonged exposure:** CPT can be delivered with or without a
written trauma account. The version without it involves **no detailed retelling of the traumatic
event at all**. For many people that is the difference between starting treatment and not.

## What the research shows

### The head-to-head trial

The definitive comparison is Schnurr and colleagues' trial of 916 veterans across 17 VA medical
centres — the largest PTSD psychotherapy trial ever conducted — randomizing to CPT or prolonged
exposure under a flexible 10-to-14-session protocol
([*JAMA Network Open*, 2022](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2788220)).

Prolonged exposure came out slightly ahead on the primary outcome, but
[**the difference did not reach the predetermined threshold for clinical
significance**](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8771295/), and the treatments did not
differ on other symptoms, functioning or quality of life. The authors' stated conclusion was that
**patient preference should be considered**, because both produced meaningful improvement.

An earlier meta-analysis found a standardized mean difference of **1.35 for CPT** against 1.23 for
exposure therapy — a large effect, and if anything nominally larger.

And the original 2002 comparison by Resick and colleagues found no difference between CPT and PE
on PTSD or depression outcomes, though
[CPT produced greater reductions in some domains of
guilt](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8771295/). That guilt finding fits the
treatment's design.

### Retention: the practical advantage

Here is where CPT may matter most.

In the 916-veteran trial, dropout was **47% for CPT against 56% for PE**. A separate randomized
trial examining dropout directly found
[45.8% in CPT versus 52.3% in PE, with better retention in CPT over time and the difference
becoming significant after session three](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13048278/) —
the session at which PE introduces imaginal exposure.

Mean sessions attended in the large trial were 9.1 for CPT and 8.2 for PE.

That same dropout study found something informative about *why* people leave CPT, and it is the
opposite of PE. In CPT, **slower reduction in PTSD symptoms predicted dropout**. In PE, symptom
change was unrelated to dropout. So CPT loses people who aren't seeing progress; PE loses people
who are dreading the next session.

Higher depression severity predicted dropout in both.

**Where the evidence stands.** Strongly recommended by the most recent US military guideline, supported by the
largest trial in the field, with a large effect size and better retention than its closest rival.

## What it does not do

**It is not positive thinking.** The worksheets examine evidence in both directions. If the
evidence supports a painful conclusion, CPT does not ask you to pretend otherwise — it asks
whether the conclusion is as total as it feels.

**It does not avoid the trauma.** You will think about it in detail. What you may not have to do
is narrate it aloud repeatedly.

**It is not effortless.** There is written homework every week, and people who do not do it
generally do not improve. The dropout finding — that slow progress predicts leaving — cuts both
ways, since progress and homework completion are related.

**Safety note.** In the large trial, PE and CPT were comparable on safety with one exception:
psychiatric hospitalization was somewhat more likely in CPT at 5.0%, which the authors observe is
close to the 4.2% background rate among VA patients with PTSD generally.

## What the sessions actually involve

Twelve sessions, usually weekly, 50 to 60 minutes.

**Session 1:** education about PTSD and the treatment rationale. Homework: write an **impact
statement** — what you believe about why the event happened and how it changed your view of
yourself, others and the world. This is the document the whole treatment works from.

**Sessions 2-3:** identify stuck points from the impact statement. Begin noticing the connection
between event, thought and feeling.

**Sessions 4-5:** the first worksheets. Take one stuck point and ask the structured questions.
*What is the evidence for and against? Am I thinking in all-or-nothing terms? Am I confusing a
possibility with a likelihood?*

**Sessions 6-12:** work through progressively broader themes — safety, trust, power and control,
esteem, intimacy. Each has its own module and worksheets.

**Final session:** rewrite the impact statement. Reading the first version against the last is,
for many people, the most striking moment of the treatment.

**Where the written account fits.** In CPT with account (CPT+A), you write a detailed narrative
of the trauma and read it aloud in session. In standard CPT, you do not. The evidence supports
both, and this is a genuine choice you can make at the outset.

## Is this available to me?

**Through the VA**, which has trained CPT clinicians system-wide alongside PE.

**Privately.** CPT training is delivered through official CPT training programmes; ask a
prospective therapist whether they have completed it and are rostered.

**Via telehealth**, well established. California readers: your therapist generally needs a
California licence — see [finding a therapist](/clinic/finding-a-therapist).

**Group format.** CPT has been delivered in groups, which can shorten waits.

Billed as psychotherapy.

## How to ask for it

> *"I'd like a trauma-focused therapy for PTSD. I'm particularly interested in cognitive
> processing therapy — I understand it can be delivered without a written trauma account, and
> that retention rates are better than for prolonged exposure. Is a CPT-trained clinician
> available?"*

If a clinician tells you the only real option is exposure, the 2023 VA/DoD guideline places CPT,
PE and EMDR at the same strength of recommendation, and the largest head-to-head trial concluded
patient preference should be considered.

## Choosing between CPT, PE and EMDR

All three carry the same guideline recommendation. On the evidence:

- **Choose CPT** if the dominant experience is guilt, shame, self-blame or shattered assumptions
  about trust and safety — and if the prospect of repeatedly narrating the event is what has
  stopped you starting.
- **Choose [PE](/clinic/prolonged-exposure-ptsd)** if the dominant experience is fear and
  avoidance, and if you can commit to daily homework.
- **Choose [EMDR](/clinic/emdr-ptsd-treatment)** if you want to work with the memory without
  detailed verbal recounting and prefer a less homework-intensive protocol.

And if the first one you try does not fit, switching is a normal clinical decision. Given that
roughly half of people leave these treatments, finding the one you can stay in is not a
consolation prize — it is the whole game.

## When this isn't enough

If twelve sessions produce no movement, that is worth reviewing rather than repeating. Slow
progress is the specific predictor of dropout in CPT, and the right response is a conversation
about switching rather than quietly disappearing.

Stabilization and safety come before trauma reprocessing if you are in crisis or in an unsafe
situation.

And the 2023 guideline is explicit that comorbidities, including substance use disorders, should
not preclude someone from receiving a recommended PTSD treatment.
