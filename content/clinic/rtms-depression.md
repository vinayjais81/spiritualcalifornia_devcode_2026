Most of this series is about talking. This one is about a machine.

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation delivers focused magnetic pulses through the skull
to a specific region of the brain, while you sit in a chair, awake, and afterwards drive home.
It is FDA-cleared, it is covered by most US insurance for treatment-resistant depression, and it
is genuinely useful for people whom antidepressants have failed.

It is also surrounded by numbers that vary enormously depending on which study you read, and by
a fast-moving accelerated-protocol industry making claims that outrun the trials.

## What it actually is

A coil is positioned against the scalp, usually over the **left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex**,
and delivers rapid magnetic pulses that induce electric currents in the cortex beneath. The
proposed effect is
[altered connectivity within and between large-scale brain networks involved in emotion
regulation](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10427506/).

You are awake throughout. There is no anaesthesia and no sedation — which is the single biggest
practical difference from electroconvulsive therapy, with which it is frequently confused. ECT
induces a seizure under general anaesthesia and carries
[risk of anterograde and retrograde memory loss](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10427506/).
rTMS does neither.

**Two main protocols:**

- **Conventional rTMS** — 3,000 pulses of rhythmic 10-20 Hz stimulation, once daily, typically
  **six weeks**, around 40 minutes a session.
- **Intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS)** — 600 pulses, roughly **10 minutes**, with
  comparable outcomes. This is now the more common choice for obvious practical reasons.

**And a third, newer category: accelerated protocols.** The FDA has cleared a protocol using
[10 sessions of prolonged iTBS per day for five days](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12393489/)
— compressing six weeks into one. Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy (SNT, formerly SAINT) is the
best-known version.

## What the research shows

### The core case

rTMS is described in the literature as a non-invasive neuromodulation treatment for major
depressive disorder that
[can be highly effective even after multiple medications have failed to provide
benefit](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165032726001965?via=ihub).

That framing matters because of the comparison. The same paper notes that the *initial*
antidepressant selected produces remission in
[**less than one-third of individuals**](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165032726001965?via=ihub),
leading to a long trial-and-error process across multiple medications. rTMS is not competing
against a highly effective standard.

### The numbers, and why they disagree

Here is where readers get misled, and it is worth being precise.

**Naturalistic studies** — real-world clinics, patients who chose the treatment, no control group
— report rates
[up to 70% response and 40% remission](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10427506/).

**Controlled and clinical-cohort data are more modest.** A UCLA analysis of 175 clinical patients
comparing accelerated against conventional rTMS found remission at treatment completion of
[**24% for conventional and 22.5% for accelerated**](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165032726001965?via=ihub).

An accelerated iTBS trial reported
[response and remission of **35.4% and 27.1%**](https://www.floridatmsclinic.com/saint-depression-treatment),
and an Australian randomized trial comparing rTMS against accelerated iTBS in unilateral and
bilateral configurations found overall response of **43.7%** and remission of **28.2%**, with
**no significant differences across the three groups**.

Roughly a quarter of people with treatment-resistant depression reaching remission is a genuinely
valuable result for that population. It is not 70%. If a clinic quotes you the naturalistic
figure, ask which study it comes from.

### Accelerated protocols: faster, not better

The most consistent finding across the accelerated literature is that compressing the course
speeds up response **without improving the endpoint**.

The UCLA comparison found near-identical remission (24% vs 22.5%). A randomized single-blind
trial comparing accelerated TMS against standard rTMS, with pulses matched across arms, found
that
[accelerated TMS outperformed standard rTMS **in the early phase**](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S3050529125000935)
— and the authors' conclusion was framed around **faster** response, not larger.

That is still worth a great deal. Five days instead of six weeks matters enormously for someone
who is severely unwell, working, or travelling to a clinic. But it is a convenience and speed
argument, and it is frequently sold as a superiority argument.

**Durability is the open question.** A study of Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy in 46 patients
notes that the duration of remission after a single course
[is unknown, which poses a significant limit on clinical decision-making](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1935861X25000877)
— and that follow-up in this field is typically reported only on short-term timescales.

### Recent developments

**Adolescents.** The FDA has cleared TMS for adolescent depression — described in *Molecular
Psychiatry* as introducing a non-pharmacological intervention with potential to
[accelerate remission and mitigate progression to treatment-resistant
depression](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41380-026-03455-0), against a backdrop of persistent
gaps in adolescent treatment. At least one major US insurer has added first-line TMS coverage for
adolescents.

**Late-life depression** may respond less well. rTMS-induced electric field strength
[may be attenuated by cortical atrophy in the prefrontal
cortex](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10427506/), which is an active research area
rather than a reason to rule it out.

**Where the evidence stands.** FDA-cleared, insurance-covered, with replicated randomized evidence
in treatment-resistant depression and a strong safety profile. The realistic remission figure is
around a quarter to a third — not the naturalistic 70%. Accelerated protocols are faster rather
than more effective, and their durability is not established.

## What it does not do

**It is not ECT.** No anaesthesia, no seizure, no memory loss. This is the most common
misconception and it stops people accessing a treatment that might help them.

**It has not been shown to work better when accelerated.** Faster, on current evidence. Not
better.

**Durability after a single course is not established**, and relapse does occur. Maintenance
protocols exist and are not standardized.

**And "neuronavigation" is an open question, not a settled upgrade.** MRI-guided targeting is
marketed heavily and priced accordingly; whether it outperforms scalp-measurement targeting is
described in the field as an unresolved and expensive question.

**Safety.** rTMS is well tolerated. The common effects are scalp discomfort during stimulation and
headache afterwards, both usually settling within the first week. The serious risk is **seizure**,
which is rare. Screening covers seizure history, metal implants in the head, and cochlear
implants or other devices. As with any antidepressant treatment, there is a possibility of
switching into mania in people with bipolar disorder, so a proper diagnostic assessment matters.

## What the sessions actually involve

**First session — mapping.** The clinician determines your **resting motor threshold** by
stimulating the motor cortex until your thumb twitches, then sets your treatment dose relative to
that. This takes longer than a normal session and is oddly memorable.

**Then the course.** You sit in a chair, awake. The coil is positioned and held against your
scalp. The pulses are loud — a rapid clicking — so you wear earplugs. The sensation is a tapping
or knocking on the scalp, uncomfortable at first for many people and usually tolerable within a
few sessions.

Conventional protocols run around 40 minutes; iTBS runs about 10. You can read, listen to
something, or talk. Afterwards you leave and drive.

**Frequency:** five days a week for four to six weeks conventionally. Accelerated protocols
compress this to multiple sessions daily across five days, with breaks between.

Most people report nothing for the first two weeks. Improvement, where it happens, tends to
appear in weeks three to five — and is often noticed by family before the patient.

## Is this available to me?

**Coverage is one of rTMS's real advantages.** Most US insurers, including Medicare, cover it for
treatment-resistant depression — typically after documented failure of two or more antidepressant
trials at adequate dose and duration.

**Where to look:** psychiatric practices with TMS services, academic medical centres, hospital
outpatient psychiatry, and dedicated TMS clinics.

**Ask what protocol they use** — conventional, iTBS, or accelerated — and what the evidence is for
that specific one.

**Ask who supervises.** rTMS should be delivered under psychiatric supervision, with a proper
diagnostic assessment first.

**Be cautious with heavily-marketed premium add-ons.** Neuronavigation and accelerated protocols
both command higher prices, and neither is established as producing better final outcomes.

## How to ask for it

To a psychiatrist or GP:

> *"I've tried [number] antidepressants without adequate response. I'd like to discuss rTMS. Am I
> likely to meet my insurer's criteria, and can you refer me?"*

To a TMS clinic:

> *"What are your response and remission rates from your own patients, and how do you define
> them? Which protocol do you use? What happens if I relapse after the course?"*

That first question is the useful one. A clinic that can give you its own outcome data, defined
clearly, is a clinic that measures.

## Who this suits

rTMS is worth asking about specifically if:

- You have **depression that has not responded to two or more antidepressants** — the standard
  criterion, and where the evidence sits
- You **cannot tolerate antidepressant side effects**
- You want a treatment that **does not affect memory**, unlike ECT
- You can commit to **daily visits for four to six weeks**, or can access an accelerated protocol

It suits poorly anyone who cannot attend daily, anyone with the contraindications above, and
anyone expecting the naturalistic 70% figure.

## When this isn't enough

If a course does not help, other options remain: different rTMS protocols, medication
combinations, [esketamine](/clinic/ketamine-and-esketamine), and ECT — which, despite its
reputation, remains the most effective treatment available for severe treatment-resistant
depression.

If you relapse after an initial response, that is common and does not mean the treatment failed.
Ask about maintenance or a repeat course.

And rTMS is a treatment for depression, not a substitute for psychotherapy. The combination is
frequently better than either alone — our article on [finding a
therapist](/clinic/finding-a-therapist) covers the other half.

**If you are in crisis or having thoughts of harming yourself**, contact your doctor, a crisis
line, or emergency services.
