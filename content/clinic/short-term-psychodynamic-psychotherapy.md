Psychodynamic therapy occupies a strange position. It is widely practised, widely taught, and
widely dismissed in evidence-based circles as the unevidenced one — the tradition that never
submitted to trials.

That dismissal is out of date. Short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy has been through
meta-analyses, individual patient data analyses and large randomized trials. The result is more
interesting than either side usually reports.

## What it actually is

**Short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy** is time-limited — typically 16 to 30 sessions — and
distinguishable from open-ended psychoanalytic work by having a focus, a formulation and an
endpoint.

Its working assumptions: that current difficulties are shaped by patterns formed in earlier
relationships; that some of what drives distress operates outside awareness; that avoidance of
painful feeling produces symptoms; and that the therapeutic relationship itself will reproduce
those patterns and can therefore be used to work on them.

**What that looks like in practice:** attention to the relationship in the room, to what is being
avoided, to recurring relational themes, and to affect — psychodynamic therapists tend to slow
down and stay with feeling where CBT would move toward a technique.

The distinctive claim is about **transference**: that how you relate to the therapist is a live
sample of how you relate generally, available for examination as it happens rather than
reconstructed from a report.

## What the research shows

### The efficacy picture

Driessen and colleagues' meta-analysis update, covering the large-scale trials conducted since the
earlier reviews, found that STPP
[produces symptom reduction and functional improvement during treatment, that gains are maintained
or further improved at follow-up, and that it is efficacious compared with control
conditions](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0272735815001038).

And the line that matters: **individual STPP does not differ from other psychotherapies on
depression outcomes.**

That conclusion is corroborated across the field. Multiple meta-analyses have found
[no significant differences in efficacy for depressive disorders between CBT, psychodynamic
therapy, interpersonal therapy and supportive
therapy](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165032714004832), and a
large-scale randomized trial found psychodynamic therapy and CBT equally effective for depression.
Steinert and colleagues went further, publishing a meta-analysis in the *American Journal of
Psychiatry* explicitly designed to test **equivalence** rather than difference.

### Where it gets genuinely contested

The picture is not uniformly flattering, and this journal is not going to present it as such.

An earlier Driessen meta-analysis found a
[possible superiority of other psychotherapies over STPP at one-year follow-up (d = −0.29,
p = .07)](https://hewittlab.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2020/04/Driessen-2010.pdf) — borderline,
not statistically significant, but pointing the wrong way. Churchill and colleagues found patients
receiving CBT were more likely to *recover* than those receiving STPP, while finding no
differences in post-treatment symptoms, symptom reduction or dropout. Leichsenring's earlier
review of six studies found the two equally effective and called that preliminary.

Outside depression the gaps are clearer: behavioural therapy has shown stronger short-term effects
in social anxiety disorder, and there is no established evidence for psychodynamic therapy in OCD
— where [ERP](/clinic/erp-for-ocd) is the treatment.

### The allegiance finding, which is the best thing in this literature

One analysis in this area reported something this whole series has been circling. Comparing CBT
against other therapies:

> [Researchers' self-reported allegiance was positively correlated with the strength of CBT's
> superiority; however, when controlling for allegiance ratings, CBT was still associated with a
> significant
> advantage.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0272735809001160)

Read both halves. **Allegiance inflates the result** — how much a research team believes in CBT
predicts how much better CBT looks in their trial. **And controlling for it does not make the
advantage disappear.**

The same analysis found that methodological strength of studies was *not* associated with larger
or smaller differences between CBT and other therapies — so the gap is not simply a matter of
better-conducted CBT trials.

This is the most honest treatment of researcher bias we have found in any literature covered by
this journal. It neither dismisses the bias nor uses it to explain away an inconvenient finding.

### A moderator worth watching

An individual participant data meta-analysis found that
[**depressive episode duration might moderate STPP
outcome**](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0272735823000272) — with the
speculation that longer episodes are more shaped by personality structure, producing more complex
alliances and transference, which psychodynamic therapists are specifically trained to work with.

The authors are appropriately restrained: the evidence for this moderator is limited because the
p value exceeded the Bonferroni correction. But it is a plausible hypothesis about *who* this
therapy suits, and — like the causal-forest work in our [schema
therapy](/clinic/schema-therapy) article — it is the direction the field needs to go.

**Where the evidence stands.** Efficacious against controls, broadly equivalent to other therapies for depression in
most analyses, with a minority of analyses favouring CBT, weaker evidence outside depression, and
a fragmented literature.

## What it does not do

**It is not one treatment.** The field's own reviewers describe the evidence for psychodynamic
therapy as
["scattered" between different forms](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165032714004832),
which creates real problems for training and for translating research into practice. "I'm doing
psychodynamic therapy" is less informative than "I'm doing CPT."

**It is not the treatment for OCD, and probably not first choice for social anxiety.** Where
specific protocols exist and outperform, use them.

**Short-term is not always enough.** One study found the evidence extends the base for
psychodynamic therapy in depression while indicating that time-limited treatment is
**insufficient for a substantial number of patients** in psychiatric outpatient settings.

**And open-ended psychoanalytic therapy is a different thing** with a much thinner evidence base
than STPP. This article is about the time-limited, focused version that has been trialled.

## What the sessions actually involve

Less structured than anything else in this series, and that is the point rather than a flaw —
though it is why some people find it frustrating.

There is no agenda set at the start and usually no homework. You talk; the therapist listens for
patterns, for what is being avoided, and for what is happening between the two of you.

**Silences are used deliberately.** A psychodynamic therapist will let a pause run where a CBT
therapist would fill it. What surfaces in that space is treated as material.

**The relationship is discussed openly.** If you arrive irritated with the therapist, or find
yourself performing for them, that will be named and examined — not as a distraction from the
work but as the work.

**Endings matter.** In time-limited psychodynamic therapy the ending is planned from the start and
worked with throughout, on the grounds that how you handle this ending will echo how you handle
others.

Typically 16 to 30 weekly sessions.

## Is this available to me?

Psychodynamic therapy is widely available in the US — probably more available than most of the
specific protocols in this series.

**Ask whether it is time-limited and focused.** The evidence base is on STPP, not on open-ended
work. A therapist who can describe a formulation and a rough session count is offering the
trialled version.

**Ask about training.** Psychoanalytic institutes provide extensive training; many licensed
therapists describe themselves as psychodynamic with considerably less.

Billed as psychotherapy. Longer courses raise coverage questions worth resolving early.

## How to ask for it

> *"I'd like time-limited psychodynamic therapy — around 16 to 20 sessions with a focus, rather
> than open-ended. My difficulty is a pattern in relationships rather than a discrete symptom.
> Is that something you offer?"*

## Who this suits

STPP is worth asking for specifically if:

- Your difficulty is **relational and recurring** rather than a discrete symptom set
- **Long-duration depression** — per the moderator hypothesis, though it remains a hypothesis
- You have done **CBT and found the structure constraining**, or felt the technique addressed the
  surface
- You are curious about **why**, and would find a therapy that declines that question
  unsatisfying

It suits less well anyone who wants structure, measurement and homework, anyone with a condition
that has a specific protocol, and anyone who would find silence and open-ended exploration
anxiety-provoking rather than generative.

## When this isn't enough

If time-limited work proves insufficient — which the literature says it will for a substantial
minority — the options are extension, a change of modality, or medication alongside.

For OCD, PTSD, insomnia and panic, the targeted treatments in this series have stronger and more
specific evidence, and psychodynamic therapy is not the first call.

And the broader point stands: on the depression evidence, most analyses find these therapies
equivalent. If you would engage more readily with this one, that is a legitimate basis for
choosing it — which is exactly what the [alliance
literature](/clinic/common-factors-dodo-bird) would predict.
