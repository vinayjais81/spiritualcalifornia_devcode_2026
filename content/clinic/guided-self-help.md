There is a step between doing nothing and seeing a therapist, and almost nobody is told about it.

**Guided self-help** means working through a structured treatment programme — a workbook, a
course, a set of modules — with brief, regular contact from a trained supporter. Not therapy.
Not an app you downloaded alone. Something in between, with an evidence base that surprises
people.

## What it actually is

The format is deliberately minimal. You do the work; a supporter keeps you doing it.

- **The material** is a proper structured programme — usually CBT-based, sometimes
  [ACT](/clinic/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy) or
  [behavioural activation](/clinic/behavioural-activation) — delivered as a workbook, a website
  or a set of printed modules.
- **The support** is brief and scheduled: fifteen to thirty minutes weekly, by phone, video,
  email or in person. The supporter is often not a psychologist. In the UK's stepped-care system
  they are Psychological Wellbeing Practitioners, trained specifically for this role.
- **The supporter's job is not to deliver therapy.** It is to check what you did, help with what
  got stuck, and expect you back next week.

**Bibliotherapy** is the older name for the book-based version, and it is not a metaphor —
specific manualized workbooks, tested in trials.

**Where it sits.** Guided self-help is the middle step in a stepped-care model: low intensity
first, escalating to individual therapy for those who need it. That model exists to shorten
waiting lists and increase service capacity, which is a real institutional motive — but as we
will see, the evidence for the step itself is genuinely good.

## What the research shows

### Guided self-help versus face-to-face therapy

The key comparison is Cuijpers and colleagues' systematic review and meta-analysis of
**comparative outcome studies** in *Psychological Medicine* — not against waiting lists, but
directly against face-to-face treatment.

Their conclusion:
[**"It seems safe to conclude that guided self-help and face-to-face treatments can have
comparable effects. It is time to start thinking about implementation in routine
care."**](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychological-medicine/article/is-guided-selfhelp-as-effective-as-facetoface-psychotherapy-for-depression-and-anxiety-disorders-a-systematic-review-and-metaanalysis-of-comparative-outcome-studies/4C7DA862B658641E1A648299A19186D1)

That is an unusually direct statement for a meta-analysis, and it was published in 2010. Fifteen
years later, most people are still offered a waiting list instead.

### The low-intensity CBT evidence

A meta-analysis of NICE-recommended **low-intensity CBT for generalised anxiety disorder**
reviewed 23 years of randomized trials and identified 12 RCTs from 1,205 papers, covering
non-facilitated self-help, guided self-help and psychoeducational groups.

Adjusted effect sizes were
[**g = −0.63 for anxiety, −0.48 for depression and −0.64 for worry** — all in the medium range,
favouring low-intensity CBT over
controls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10763350/). Heterogeneity was significant
for anxiety and worry, with no moderators identified.

For anxiety disorders more broadly, a meta-analysis and meta-regression covering 56 articles and
82 comparisons found a
[**moderate effect size of 0.78 for self-help against waiting-list controls**](https://www.academia.edu/14960976/Self_help_treatment_of_anxiety_disorders_A_meta_analysis_and_meta_regression_of_effects_and_potential_moderators),
concluding that self-help should be offered as part of stepped-care models in community services.

And for depression, an analysis of cognitive bibliotherapy found that 17 studies with stronger
designs — pretest-posttest waiting-list control — yielded
[**an effect size of 0.77**, described as comparing favourably with outcomes from individual
psychotherapy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/43226466_Is_guided_self-help_as_effective_as_face-to-face_psychotherapy_for_depression_and_anxiety_disorders_A_systematic_review_and_meta-analysis_of_comparative_outcome_studies).

### And now the number that defines the article

Cuijpers and colleagues meta-analyzed **self-guided** psychological treatment — no contact with a
therapist or coach at all — across seven RCTs and 1,362 participants, all of high study quality.

The overall effect at post-test was
[**d = 0.28**](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0021274).

Set that against 0.77 for guided bibliotherapy and "comparable to face-to-face" for guided
self-help. **The material is often identical. The difference is whether a person is expecting
you back.**

A more recent systematic review of self-guided interventions for depressive symptoms adds a
useful and honest detail: no relationship was found between number of sessions and effects
(sessions ranged from 3 to 20), suggesting these can be delivered at minimal intensity — while
noting that
[only 12 studies (13%) were at low risk of bias, with potential to overestimate the effect
sizes](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/ebiom/article/PIIS2352-3964(24)00243-3/fulltext).

**Where the evidence stands.** Guided self-help performs comparably to face-to-face therapy for
depression and anxiety across multiple meta-analyses including direct comparative studies.
Unguided self-help performs considerably worse. This is a legitimate first step, not a
consolation prize — provided the guidance is actually there.

## What it does not do

**It is not for severe presentations.** The evidence is in mild-to-moderate depression and
anxiety. Stepped care exists precisely so that people who need more get more.

**Unguided is not the same product.** Buying a self-help book and reading it alone is a different
intervention with roughly a third of the effect.

**It is not a reason to be denied therapy.** Stepped care has a real institutional incentive
attached — shorter lists, higher capacity — and the correct use of guided self-help is as a
*first step with a route upward*, not a substitute for the step above. If you complete a
programme and are still unwell, escalation is the design working, not you failing.

**And "self-help" as a publishing category is not this.** The evidence is on specific manualized
programmes tested in trials, not on the self-improvement shelf.

## What it actually involves

**The material** typically runs 6 to 12 modules across as many weeks. Reading or video, then a
task: a thought record, an activity schedule, a graded exposure, a worry-postponement exercise.

**The sessions** are short and structured — often 20 to 30 minutes. What did you do, what
happened, what got stuck, what next. A good supporter does not stray into therapy; the boundary
is part of the model.

**The realistic experience:** week one is straightforward. Week three is where unsupported people
stop — which is precisely the week the supporter earns their place. Knowing that in advance is
useful.

Most people who complete report the accountability as the active ingredient, which the evidence
above broadly supports.

## Is this available to me?

Availability in the US is patchier than it should be, because there is no equivalent to the UK's
national stepped-care programme.

**Where to look:**

- **Integrated behavioural health in primary care.** Ask your GP whether the practice has a
  behavioural health clinician — many now do, and this is exactly what they deliver.
- **Employee Assistance Programs.** Frequently include a short guided programme, and most people
  never use theirs.
- **University counselling centres**, which often run guided workbook or online programmes to
  manage demand.
- **Health plans**, some of which contract guided digital programmes — see our
  [digital CBT article](/clinic/digital-cbt-and-apps) for how to evaluate those.
- **Community mental health**, where low-intensity options are usually the shortest wait.

**Prescription digital therapeutics** are a related and more regulated category — our
[CBT-I article](/clinic/cbt-i-insomnia) covers the insomnia products, now reimbursable under
Medicare.

## How to ask for it

> *"While I'm waiting for therapy, is there a guided self-help programme available? I know guided
> self-help performs comparably to face-to-face treatment for mild-to-moderate depression and
> anxiety, and that unguided is considerably weaker — so I'd want something with regular check-ins
> rather than a workbook on my own."*

Two follow-ups worth making explicit:

- **"Can I stay on the waiting list while I do this?"** Yes is the correct answer.
- **"What happens if I finish and I'm not better?"** The answer should describe stepping up.

## Who this suits

Guided self-help is worth asking for specifically if:

- Symptoms are **mild to moderate**
- You are **facing a long wait** — this is its single best use
- **Access is the constraint** — cost, transport, schedule, rural location
- You **respond to accountability** more than to insight, which describes a lot of people

It suits poorly anyone with severe symptoms, anyone in crisis, and anyone who has already
abandoned several structured programmes without support — for whom the guidance component matters
most and should be non-negotiable.

## When this isn't enough

Completing a programme without improvement is **information, not failure**, and it is the trigger
for stepping up. Say so plainly: *I've completed the guided programme and my symptoms haven't
changed — what's next?*

For conditions with specific protocols — [OCD](/clinic/erp-for-ocd),
[PTSD](/clinic/prolonged-exposure-ptsd) — low-intensity work is not the right first step, and the
targeted treatments in this series are.

And if you are in crisis or having thoughts of harming yourself, contact your doctor, a crisis
line, or emergency services rather than a workbook.
