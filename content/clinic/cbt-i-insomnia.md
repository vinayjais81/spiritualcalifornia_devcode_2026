This journal has now told readers three separate times to ask their doctor about CBT-I by name —
in our articles on [yoga nidra](/journal/yoga-nidra-non-sleep-rest), on [waking at
3am](/what-to-do/awake-at-3am), and on [being exhausted but
wired](/what-to-do/exhausted-but-wired). Each time we said it was the guideline-recommended
first-line treatment and moved on.

Here is the article that should have existed.

## What it actually is

Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Insomnia is a structured programme, usually six to eight
sessions, built from four components. It is not sleep hygiene, and confusing the two is the most
common mistake people make.

**Sleep restriction** is the engine, and it is counter-intuitive enough that people resist it.
You track how long you actually sleep, then deliberately restrict your time in bed to
approximately that amount — often initially less than you want. This builds sleep pressure and
consolidates fragmented sleep. As efficiency improves, the window widens.

**Stimulus control** rebuilds the association between bed and sleep. Bed is for sleep only. If
you are awake more than about twenty minutes, you get up and go elsewhere until sleepy.

**Cognitive restructuring** targets the beliefs that keep the system running — *if I don't sleep
tonight tomorrow is ruined*, the arithmetic people do at 3am about how many hours remain.

**Relaxation and education** round it out.

The core insight is that acute insomnia is usually caused by something identifiable — stress,
illness, a new baby — while *chronic* insomnia is maintained by what people did to cope with it:
going to bed earlier, lying there longer, napping, worrying about it. CBT-I dismantles the
coping.

## What the research shows

CBT-I is recommended as first-line treatment for chronic insomnia — ahead of medication — by the
American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the American College of Physicians, in guidelines dating
from
[2016, 2017 and 2021](https://www.amerihealthcaritasvipcareplus.com/content/dam/amerihealth-caritas/vip-care-plus/pdf/provider/resources/clinical/policies-202510/ccp1530-Prescription-digital-therapeutics-insomnia.pdf.coredownload.inline.pdf).

Note what "first-line" means here. Not first-line among behavioural options. **First-line,
full stop, ahead of prescription hypnotics.** The reason is durability: medication works while
you take it, and CBT-I effects persist after the course ends.

We covered a striking demonstration of the comparison in our yoga nidra article. In a Delhi sleep
laboratory, 41 people with chronic insomnia were randomized to CBT-I or yoga nidra, with
polysomnography. Both improved — but the trial's authors were explicit in reporting their
positive yoga nidra result that
[CBT-I remains the effective non-pharmacological treatment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34825538/).

### The access problem, and what changed

The reason most people are handed a prescription instead is not clinical. It is supply. Reviews
of digital CBT-I state the problem plainly: the treatment is guideline-recommended and
[availability of trained CBT-I therapists is
limited](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33226269/).

Three developments have shifted this.

**FDA-cleared prescription digital therapeutics.** **Somryst** became the first FDA-cleared
prescription digital therapeutic for chronic insomnia in March 2020 — a nine-week app-delivered
CBT-I programme for adults 22 and over, cleared on the basis of two randomized trials in more
than 1,400 adults. **SleepioRx** was subsequently cleared on the basis of substantial equivalence
to Somryst; in its supporting trial, participants had
[2.52 times the odds of response and 5.8 times the odds of remission at ten weeks compared with
sleep hygiene
education](https://www.amerihealthcaritasvipcareplus.com/content/dam/amerihealth-caritas/vip-care-plus/pdf/provider/resources/clinical/policies-202510/ccp1530-Prescription-digital-therapeutics-insomnia.pdf.coredownload.inline.pdf).

**Medicare reimbursement.** SleepioRx is reimbursed under Medicare's **Digital Mental Health
Treatment (DMHT) codes, effective January 2025**, making it covered for Medicare patients when
prescribed by a physician. Commercial coverage still varies by plan. This is the most
consequential recent change in access to CBT-I and almost nobody knows about it.

**International endorsement.** UK guidance recommends Sleepio as a cost-saving option compared
with sleep hygiene and sleeping pills.

**Two honest caveats.** Some US payer policies still classify these products as investigational
and not medically necessary, so coverage is genuinely inconsistent. And at least one favourable
network meta-analysis of Somryst was authored by people affiliated with the manufacturer — a
disclosure we would flag anywhere in this journal.

**Where the evidence stands.** Guideline first-line status across multiple professional bodies, durable effects, and
now multiple regulator-cleared delivery routes.

## What it does not do

**It does not work immediately, and it makes things worse first.** Sleep restriction means
deliberately spending less time in bed. Weeks one and two are commonly harder — more daytime
sleepiness, more irritability. This is the treatment working as designed, and it is the reason
people drop out. Knowing it in advance is most of the battle.

**It is not sleep hygiene.** Sleep hygiene advice — dark room, no caffeine, no screens — is what
most people have already tried, and on its own it performs poorly. The digital CBT-I trials
generally use sleep hygiene education as the *control condition*.

**It does not treat what it is not for.** CBT-I addresses insomnia. It does not treat sleep
apnoea, restless legs, narcolepsy or circadian rhythm disorders, all of which present as poor
sleep and require different management.

### Who should not do sleep restriction unsupervised

This matters and is rarely stated on consumer-facing pages. Prescribing information for the
digital products lists conditions in which sleep restriction is contraindicated:

- **Bipolar disorder** — sleep restriction can precipitate mania
- **Schizophrenia and other psychotic-spectrum disorders**
- **Untreated obstructive sleep apnoea**
- **Parasomnias** (sleepwalking, night terrors)
- **Epilepsy** — sleep deprivation lowers seizure threshold
- **High risk of falls**
- **Pregnancy**
- Any medical condition worsened by sleep restriction, including degenerative illness

If any of these apply to you, CBT-I may still be appropriate — but delivered by a clinician who
can adapt it, not through an app you bought yourself.

## What the sessions actually involve

**Session one** is assessment and a sleep diary. You will be asked to keep it for one to two
weeks before anything changes, which frustrates people who came for a solution.

**Session two** delivers the number: your prescribed sleep window, calculated from your diary.
For many people it is startlingly short — a window of five or five and a half hours is common at
the start. You are given a fixed rise time, which does not move regardless of how the night went.

**Sessions three to six** adjust the window upward as sleep efficiency improves, typically by
fifteen to thirty minutes at a time, and work through the cognitive material.

Throughout, you keep the diary. The diary is not admin; it is the instrument the treatment is
titrated against.

Most programmes run six to eight weekly sessions. The digital versions run six to nine modules
over the same period.

## Is this available to me?

**Through a therapist.** Look for a clinician with training in behavioural sleep medicine. The
Society of Behavioral Sleep Medicine maintains a provider directory, and the **DBSM** credential
(Diplomate in Behavioral Sleep Medicine) is the specialist qualification. Expect a wait.

**Through a prescription app.** Somryst and SleepioRx require a prescription from a physician.
Ask your GP directly — many do not know these exist.

**Through Medicare.** Since January 2025, the DMHT codes cover SleepioRx when prescribed. If you
are on Medicare, this is likely your fastest route.

**Through commercial insurance.** Variable. Some plans cover digital CBT-I; some still classify
it as investigational. Ask specifically, and ask about behavioural sleep medicine as a covered
benefit separately.

**Through the VA.** The VA has invested substantially in CBT-I training and it is widely
available across the system.

## How to ask for it

To a GP:

> *"I've had insomnia for more than three months. I know CBT-I is the guideline-recommended
> first-line treatment ahead of medication. Can you refer me to a behavioural sleep medicine
> provider, or prescribe a digital CBT-I product like Somryst or SleepioRx?"*

If you are offered a sleeping pill instead, a reasonable follow-up:

> *"I'd prefer to try CBT-I first, given the guidelines and the durability of the effect. If
> there's a wait, could we discuss short-term medication as a bridge rather than the primary
> plan?"*

That second sentence matters. This article is not an argument against medication — it is an
argument against medication being the only thing offered.

## When this isn't enough

If a full course of CBT-I has not helped, the most likely explanation is that something else is
driving the sleep problem. Worth investigating: **sleep apnoea** (snoring, witnessed pauses,
waking unrefreshed — extremely common and very treatable), thyroid dysfunction, chronic pain,
restless legs, medication effects, alcohol, and depression, which characteristically causes
early-morning waking.

Circadian problems are a separate category with a separate fix. If your issue is that sleep
arrives at 3am and would happily continue until noon, that is a timing problem and light exposure
is the lever — see our [article on morning light](/journal/morning-light-circadian).
