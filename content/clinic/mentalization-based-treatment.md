Most psychotherapy trials stop measuring at six or twelve months. This journal has repeatedly had
to write some version of *durability was not assessed*.

Mentalization-Based Treatment has an **eight-year follow-up** — conducted five years after all
treatment had finished, by interviewers blind to original allocation. That alone makes it worth
your attention, whatever you conclude about the rest.

## What it actually is

**Mentalizing** is the capacity to recognize and understand mental states — your own and other
people's — and to hold in mind that behaviour is driven by beliefs, feelings and intentions that
are not directly visible.

MBT, developed by Anthony Bateman and Peter Fonagy, proposes that this capacity is fragile in
borderline personality disorder and collapses under attachment stress. When it goes, other people
stop being minds with reasons and become simply threatening or abandoning — and the reactions that
follow look like BPD.

The treatment is therefore unusual in what it does *not* do. It does not primarily teach skills,
challenge thoughts or process trauma. It works to **restore and stabilize mentalizing in the
moment it fails**, which mostly means slowing the session down and getting curious about what was
going on in each person's mind.

The therapeutic stance is described as *not-knowing*: the therapist does not interpret from
expertise but wonders alongside, modelling the very capacity being rebuilt.

A more recent theoretical development frames BPD in terms of
[**epistemic trust** — the willingness to accept information from another person as relevant and
generalizable](https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-3-319-24612-3_912) — and casts therapy's
job as restoring the ability to learn from other people at all.

## What the research shows

### The trials

The first randomized controlled trial randomized **44 patients** to MBT delivered in partial
hospitalization or to treatment as usual. Both groups improved; MBT improved
[significantly more, reducing impulsive symptoms including suicidality and self-harm, as well as
depression](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4467231/). Gains at 18 months were maintained
at 36 months, and health costs were recovered over that period.

The **eight-year follow-up** is the striking one. Assessed by research psychologists blind to
original allocation, five years after all MBT had ended,
[many benefits were still evident in suicide attempts and hospitalization, with social functioning
— employment and service use — also favouring the MBT
group](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18347003/).

The larger and better-designed trial randomized **134 patients** to 18 months of outpatient MBT or
**structured clinical management** — an active, credible comparator delivered by
[eleven therapists equal in years of experience and training, with independent evaluators blind to
allocation assessing every six
months](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2009.09040539). The primary outcome was
crisis events: a composite of suicidal and severe self-injurious behaviours and hospitalization.

Summarizing that body of work, one group notes that
[MBT is the only treatment for which superiority to clinical management was demonstrated across
all primary outcome variables, plus significantly higher employment or occupational training eight
years after randomization](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11992892/).

### The caveat, published by the field itself

A review states it plainly:
[**the outcome of MBT is supported by only two randomized controlled trials, both carried out by
the developers of the
treatment**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4467231/) — and notes that the day-hospital
trial had a small sample and included multiple components beyond the manualized therapy.

We flag developer-authored evidence in many other articles, including [metacognitive
therapy](/clinic/metacognitive-therapy) and [EFT tapping](/journal/eft-tapping-examined). Two
things make this instance different from most.

**The caveat comes from inside the field**, in a review discussing MBT's own evidence base, rather
than from a critic.

**And independent replication exists.** A separate randomized trial by Jørgensen and colleagues
confirmed positive effects for MBT against supportive therapy on general functioning and BPD
symptoms — suggesting MBT addresses core BPD problems beyond self-injury and suicidality. A
multi-centre German trial comparing MBT against bona fide treatment is underway.

### One trial that didn't work, reported honestly

MBT was tested for eating disorders with comorbid BPD symptoms in the NOURISHED trial: 68
participants randomized to MBT-ED or specialist supportive clinical management across five NHS
centres.

[**Only 15 participants (22%) completed the 18-month follow-up.**](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5114835/)
Early drop-out occurred significantly more in the comparison group.

An attrition rate that severe makes a trial largely uninformative, and we include it because
selective reporting of successful extensions is how treatments acquire reputations they have not
earned.

**Where the evidence stands.** Genuinely impressive long-term outcomes, an active-comparator trial with blind
assessment, and independent replication — resting on a small number of trials, most from the
originating group, with extensions to other populations unproven.

## What it does not do

**It is not a skills-based treatment.** If you want concrete techniques for managing a crisis,
[DBT](/clinic/dialectical-behaviour-therapy) is the better-matched option and has far more trials
behind it.

**It has not been shown superior to DBT.** They have not been compared head-to-head at scale, and
the [Dodo bird pattern](/clinic/common-factors-dodo-bird) in this field would predict rough
parity.

**Its extensions are unproven.** MBT is now offered for antisocial personality disorder,
substance use, eating disorders, families and adolescents. Some of that work is ongoing and
serious — including a randomized trial in comorbid BPD and antisocial personality disorder, an
area with almost no established treatments — but it is not established.

**And it is not brief.** The trials ran 18 months.

## What the sessions actually involve

MBT typically combines **weekly individual therapy with a weekly group**, over 12 to 18 months.

The texture is unlike most therapy. When something happens — you felt dismissed, you walked out,
you were furious with your partner — the therapist does not interpret it and does not move to a
technique. They stop and ask what was in your mind at that moment. Then what you imagined was in
the other person's mind. Then whether there might be another possibility.

The point is not to arrive at the correct account. It is to rebuild the *habit* of wondering,
because in the moments that go wrong the wondering has stopped entirely.

**The group is where it gets demanding.** Mentalizing fails fastest under interpersonal pressure,
and a group provides plenty. That is deliberate: the capacity is practised where it actually
collapses.

**The therapist stance is the treatment.** A therapist who is certain about your motives is not
doing MBT — they are modelling the opposite of what is being taught.

## Is this available to me?

MBT is well established in the UK and parts of Europe and less so in the US, though availability is
growing.

**Where to look:** the Anna Freud Centre in London is the training hub, and maintains information
on trained practitioners internationally. In the US, look to academic medical centres and
specialist personality disorder services.

**Ask about format.** The trials used combined individual and group. Individual-only MBT is
offered but is not what was tested.

**Ask about supervision**, which is emphasized in this model.

Billed as psychotherapy. Length raises coverage questions worth resolving early.

## How to ask for it

> *"I'm interested in mentalization-based treatment for BPD. Is it available here, in the combined
> individual and group format? If not, what specialist personality disorder treatments do you
> offer?"*

That second question matters. Where MBT is unavailable, DBT and
[schema therapy](/clinic/schema-therapy) both have stronger US availability and comparable or
better evidence.

## Who this suits

MBT is worth asking about specifically if:

- You have a **BPD diagnosis or traits**, particularly with a history of self-harm or crisis
  presentations
- Your difficulty is **losing your grip on other people's intentions** under stress — certainty
  that someone is against you, arriving instantly and feeling like fact
- **Relationships collapse rapidly** and the collapse is bewildering afterwards
- You have found skills-based approaches too mechanical, or completed DBT and want something that
  works on understanding rather than management

It suits less well anyone in acute crisis needing immediate stabilization skills, and anyone
wanting brief focused work.

## When this isn't enough

**If you are in immediate danger, this is not a referral question** — contact your doctor, a
crisis line, or emergency services.

If MBT is unavailable, DBT and schema therapy are the two best-evidenced alternatives for BPD, and
the honest position is that no one of the three has established superiority over the others.

If you have tried one specialist BPD treatment without benefit, switching to another is a
reasonable clinical decision. The eight-year data above is a reminder that gains in this
population can continue long after treatment ends — which is an argument for finding a treatment
you can complete rather than one that looks best on paper.
