Paul Gilbert developed Compassion-Focused Therapy from a specific clinical frustration. Some
patients — reliably, the ones highest in shame and self-criticism — could
[engage perfectly well with cognitive and behavioural tasks and still respond poorly to
therapy](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4413786/).

They could complete a thought record. They could produce the balanced alternative thought. And
they did not believe it, because the voice generating it was still hostile. The technique was
working; the *tone* was not.

CFT was built to address that gap. It is one of the more theoretically elegant models in this
series, and — we should say this early — one of the less well evidenced.

## What it actually is

CFT draws on evolutionary psychology, attachment theory, Buddhist philosophy and neuroscience,
and organizes itself around **three affect regulation systems**:

- **Threat protection** — detecting and responding to danger. Fast, dominant, and overdeveloped in
  people with high shame.
- **Drive and resource-seeking** — striving, achieving, acquiring.
- **Soothing and affiliation** — the system associated with safeness, connection and rest.

The central claim is that many people are stuck oscillating between threat and drive, with the
soothing system underdeveloped or actively inaccessible — often because their early experience
provided little of it.

CFT's aim is to move people from
[a competitive, social-rank motivation system toward a compassionate
one](https://self-compassion.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Wakelin-et-al.-Effectiveness-of-self-compassion-related-intervent.pdf),
using imagery, meditation, letter-writing, chair work and role play — plus direct physiological
work with breathing, posture, facial expression and voice tone.

**Compassion here is not niceness.** Gilbert's formulation involves courage and wisdom: the
capacity to turn toward suffering and act, not to soothe it away.

## What the research shows

An early systematic review by Leaviss and Uttley found CFT
[may be more effective than no treatment, or as effective as standard treatments, across
schizophrenia spectrum disorders, bipolar disorder, PTSD, depression, personality disorders,
social anxiety, OCD and eating
disorders](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10087030/) — and that it was **especially
effective in people with high levels of self-criticism**, which is the target population.

Their conclusion in the same breath: there is **insufficient evidence that CFT is more effective
than standard treatments, because of the lack of randomized controlled trials.**

A later review by Craig and colleagues was more favourable, suggesting CFT is likely more
effective than other treatments across several clinical populations, and that group CFT may
outperform other psychological interventions.

A meta-analysis focused on the mechanism found CFT
[decreases self-criticism and increases self-soothing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10087030/)
— then noted that the number of studies is small, **only half were controlled trials**, and the
results must be interpreted cautiously.

**And the number that matters most:** a 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis of CFT in
clinical populations found
[**only three randomized controlled trials eligible for
inclusion**](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0165032723000228).

**Where the evidence stands — revised down from our forecast of B.** For consistency with how we assessed [Internal
Family Systems](/clinic/internal-family-systems), a model with three eligible RCTs and a
mechanism literature that is half uncontrolled cannot claim more. The theory is strong, the
signal is consistent, and the randomized base is thin.

Larger trials are underway, including a seven-centre randomized study of group CFT for
self-stigma in 336 participants with severe mental illness or autism.

## The finding that makes this article worth reading

Here is what CFT research has established most clearly, and it is not an effect size.

**Compassion is frightening for some people, and predictably so.**

Gilbert and Procter observed that developing self-compassion
[elicited shame, doubt and resistance in a group with long-term mental health
difficulties](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0165032723000228). Later work
found that **fear of compassion moderated the effectiveness** of a brief CFT intervention in
postpartum mothers.

The field takes this seriously enough to have built a **Fears of Compassion scale**, used to
[guide pacing and safeguarding](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/364039615_Effect_of_compassion-focused_therapy_on_self-criticism_and_self-soothing_A_meta-analysis)
before treatment begins.

Why would kindness be threatening? The clinical account is that for someone whose early
experience paired warmth with danger, or who has organized a whole identity around
self-criticism as protection, activating the soothing system removes a defence and surfaces grief
about what was missing.

This is the most useful thing in the article, and it extends well beyond CFT. Our journal's piece
on [loving-kindness meditation](/journal/loving-kindness-meditation) notes the same phenomenon —
that a minority of people feel markedly worse directing goodwill inward. If self-compassion
practices have consistently made you feel worse, that is a documented, named, expected response.
It is not a failure on your part, and a trained CFT practitioner will slow down rather than push.

## What it does not do

**It has not been shown superior to established treatments.** See Leaviss and Uttley's own
qualification.

**It is not self-esteem work.** CFT is not about thinking well of yourself. It is about relating
to your own suffering without hostility, which is a different and in some ways more achievable
target.

**It is not "be nicer to yourself."** People high in self-criticism have usually been told that
many times, and it has usually not helped. The model's whole premise is that the instruction
fails without addressing why the soothing system is inaccessible.

**And it may be less suited to general populations.** One review found CFT was *more* acceptable
in clinical samples than in general ones — unusual, and consistent with its design for a specific
difficulty.

## What the sessions actually involve

CFT front-loads **psychoeducation**, more than most therapies. The three-systems model is drawn
out and taught explicitly, along with an evolutionary framing: your threat system is not a defect,
it is old equipment doing its job in an environment it was not built for. Many clients describe
this as the first genuinely de-shaming explanation they have received.

**Compassionate imagery** follows — visualizing a compassionate figure, or receiving compassion
from an imagined source. This is where fears of compassion typically surface, and a competent
therapist expects it.

**Compassionate letter writing** — writing to yourself from a compassionate perspective about a
current difficulty. Frequently reported as the most affecting exercise.

**Chair work** externalizes the critic: you speak from the self-critical voice, then from the
compassionate one, and the difference in tone becomes audible rather than theoretical.

**Physiological practices** run throughout: soothing-rhythm breathing, posture, facial expression,
voice tone. This overlaps directly with the [slow breathing
evidence](/journal/slow-breathing-nervous-system) in our journal.

Typically 12 to 16 sessions, individual or group.

## Is this available to me?

CFT is more established in the UK and Europe than in the US, and availability here is patchy.

**Where to look:** the Compassionate Mind Foundation maintains information on trained
practitioners. Many CBT and third-wave therapists incorporate CFT components without delivering
the full protocol.

**Group formats** exist and one review suggested they may be particularly effective.

**Ask about training** — CFT has a defined training pathway, and "uses compassion-based
techniques" is not the same as CFT.

Billed as psychotherapy.

## How to ask for it

> *"My main difficulty is self-criticism and shame — I can do the CBT exercises but I don't
> believe the outputs. I'm interested in compassion-focused therapy. Do you practise it, and are
> you familiar with fears of compassion?"*

That last clause is a good competence filter. A practitioner trained in CFT will know exactly
what you mean.

## Who this suits

CFT is worth asking about specifically if:

- **Self-criticism is the dominant feature** — a running internal commentary that is hostile
  rather than merely negative
- **Shame** rather than guilt is what you carry: not *I did a bad thing* but *there is something
  wrong with me*
- You have **done CBT and found it technically fine and emotionally inert**
- **Warmth from others is difficult to receive**, and you deflect it

It suits less well anyone wanting a symptom-focused protocol with a strong randomized evidence
base — for which the treatments earlier in this series are better supported.

## When this isn't enough

If compassion practices reliably increase your distress, that is data rather than failure — and
it may indicate that trauma work should come first. Say so directly; the response should be
pacing, not persistence.

For specific conditions with specific protocols, the treatments with stronger evidence in this
series remain the first call. CFT is best understood at present as a promising approach to a
particular obstacle — self-criticism and shame — rather than as a first-line treatment for a
diagnosis.
