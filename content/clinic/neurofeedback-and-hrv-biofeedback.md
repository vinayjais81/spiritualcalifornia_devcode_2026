These two are usually sold together, often by the same clinics, under the same heading. Their
evidence bases are not comparable, and separating them is the point of this article.

**EEG neurofeedback** costs thousands of dollars over dozens of sessions and has an unusually
clear problem in its research literature. **HRV biofeedback** costs almost nothing, can be done
without a device at all, and holds up better.

## What they actually are

**EEG neurofeedback** places electrodes on the scalp, measures brain electrical activity in real
time, and feeds it back as a game, a bar, or a film that plays when your brain produces the
target pattern. The theory is operant conditioning: reward the pattern, and the brain learns to
produce it. Protocols usually run 30 to 40 sessions.

**HRV biofeedback** measures the variation in time between heartbeats — usually with a finger
clip or chest strap — and shows it to you while you breathe slowly, so you can find the rate at
which the oscillations are largest. That rate is around six breaths a minute for most people, and
our [slow breathing article](/journal/slow-breathing-nervous-system) covers the underlying
physiology in full.

**The key difference:** neurofeedback trains something you cannot otherwise perceive. HRV
biofeedback trains something you can do without any equipment at all — which turns out to matter.

## What the research shows: neurofeedback

### The blinding problem, stated as plainly as the field states it

The largest synthesis is a systematic review and meta-analysis of **38 randomized clinical trials
with 2,472 participants**, examining neurofeedback for ADHD.

Its conclusion:
[**analyses with probably blinded reports or neuropsychological outcomes provided no evidence of
meaningful benefits of neurofeedback as a treatment for
ADHD**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11800020/) — with small but statistically
significant positive effects appearing only when analyses were restricted to trials using
well-established standard protocols and specifically targeting processing speed.

An earlier meta-analysis of 13 trials found the same pattern with unusual clarity. Significant
effects appeared on ADHD symptoms
[rated by assessors most proximal to the treatment setting — that is, **the least blinded outcome
measure**](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27238063/?dopt=Abstract) — with SMDs of 0.35 for total
symptoms, 0.36 for inattention, 0.26 for hyperactivity.

And then: **effects were not significant when probably blinded ratings were the outcome, or in
trials with active or sham controls.** The authors' conclusion was that evidence from
well-controlled trials with probably blinded outcomes
[currently fails to support neurofeedback as an effective treatment for
ADHD](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27238063/?dopt=Abstract).

A separate methodological critique of a more favourable meta-analysis found that of fourteen
RCTs, only two blinded participants — and that
[**the twelve unblinded RCTs were responsible for all of the small-to-medium effect size
improvement**](https://www.adhdevidence.org/blog/a-lesson-in-cautious-interpretation-meta-analysis-suggests-neurofeedback-improves-adhd-symptoms).
The same critique noted widely varying comparators and strong evidence of publication bias.

An analysis of blinded versus unblinded assessment found EEG neurofeedback effective for
inattention in both parent (probably unblinded) and teacher (probably blinded) ratings — but
effective for hyperactivity/impulsivity
[**only in probably unblinded parent assessments**](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2014.00906/full).

**Read that pattern once more.** The treatment works when the person rating it knows the child
received it. It largely stops working when they don't. That is what a placebo response looks like
in a trial, and it is the most consistent finding in this literature.

**In adults**, the picture is similar: studies in exclusively adult patients found that while
surface EEG neurofeedback alleviated ADHD symptoms, it
[offered no additional benefit over sham controls](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-14220-y).

**Where the evidence stands for neurofeedback.** A large randomized literature in which the effect
is reliably present in unblinded assessments and reliably absent in blinded ones. There is a
narrow exception — standard protocols targeting processing speed — and it does not support the way
neurofeedback is generally sold.

## What the research shows: HRV biofeedback

A systematic review and meta-analysis by Lehrer and colleagues screened 1,868 papers and included
**58 randomized controlled studies** across a wide range of targets.

It found
[**a significant small-to-moderate effect favouring HRV biofeedback, which does not differ from
that of other effective treatments**](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32385728/).

The detail is useful:

- **Largest effects:** anxiety, depression, anger, and athletic or artistic performance
- **Smallest effects:** PTSD, sleep and quality of life
- **Effects were larger against inactive than active controls** — but significant against both,
  which is more than most things in this library manage
- **No significant differences by number of sessions or weeks of treatment**

That last point recurs across this journal — mindfulness, coaching, slow breathing all show the
same absence of a dose-response relationship.

A separate meta-analysis of bio- and neurofeedback for depression found a between-group effect of
[Hedges' g = 1.050 in patients with major depressive disorder, and a small but significant
g = 0.303 in the second group of studies](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8842225/),
concluding that HRV biofeedback and neurofeedback are associated with reductions in self-reported
depression. Note *self-reported* — the same blinding caveat applies.

**Where the evidence stands for HRV biofeedback.** A large randomized literature with consistent
small-to-moderate effects that hold against active controls, described by its own reviewers as
comparable to other effective treatments and useful as a complementary approach.

## What they do not do

**Neurofeedback has not been shown to treat ADHD** in blinded assessment. If a clinic tells you
otherwise, ask specifically about the blinded-outcome findings.

**Neurofeedback is expensive.** Thirty to forty sessions at typical US rates runs into thousands
of dollars, usually not covered by insurance. Given the blinded-outcome evidence, that is a
substantial amount to spend.

**HRV biofeedback is not a treatment for a psychiatric condition.** It is described by its own
meta-analysts as complementary, and the effects are small-to-moderate.

**And a device is not required for the HRV effect.** This is the practical headline. A study
specifically testing whether the biofeedback equipment was doing the work found that
[a five-minute slow-breathing session without any device produced broadly similar
effects](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/psyp.13952) — covered in full in our
[slow breathing article](/journal/slow-breathing-nervous-system). Some people find the visual
feedback motivating, which is a legitimate reason to use one. It is not a mechanism.

**Safety.** Both are low risk. Neurofeedback occasionally produces transient headache, fatigue or
irritability. HRV biofeedback's main caution is that deliberate breath focus increases anxiety in
some people, particularly those with panic history — see our [article on acute
anxiety](/what-to-do/chest-tight-thoughts-racing).

## What the sessions involve

**Neurofeedback:** electrodes are applied with conductive gel, and you watch a screen — a game,
a bar, a film that dims when the target pattern drops. Sessions run 30 to 60 minutes, two to
three times weekly, across three to five months. It is undemanding and, by most accounts,
somewhat boring.

**HRV biofeedback:** a finger or ear sensor, a screen showing your heart rate oscillating, and
the task of breathing slowly until the wave gets as large and smooth as possible. Most people
find their resonance frequency in the first session — usually around six breaths a minute.
Sessions run 20 to 30 minutes, and the point is to learn the breathing pattern well enough to do
it without the equipment.

Typical courses: 4 to 10 sessions, plus daily home practice. That difference — 40 sessions versus
under 10 — is most of the cost story.

## Is this available to me?

**HRV biofeedback** is offered by psychophysiologists, some psychologists, cardiac rehabilitation
programmes and sports performance services. Consumer devices run $100-$300. Free breathing pacers
work for the core practice.

**Neurofeedback** is offered by psychologists, some clinics and a large number of unregulated
providers. Insurance coverage is uncommon.

**Credentials:** the Biofeedback Certification International Alliance (BCIA) certifies both
biofeedback and neurofeedback practitioners, and is the meaningful signal. Ask what the
practitioner's underlying licence is as well.

## How to ask about it

For HRV biofeedback:

> *"I'd like to learn HRV biofeedback. How many sessions, and will you teach me to do the
> breathing without the device?"*

The second half is the test. A practitioner whose goal is for you to stop needing them is
practising this correctly.

For neurofeedback:

> *"What does the evidence show for blinded outcomes specifically? What's the total cost, and what
> would tell us it isn't working?"*

If the answer to the first question is vague or dismissive, that tells you what you need to know.

## Our honest recommendation

**Start with the free version.** Slow breathing at around six breaths a minute, longer exhale
than inhale, five minutes. That is the core of HRV biofeedback and it requires nothing. Our
[slow breathing article](/journal/slow-breathing-nervous-system) has the protocol.

**If you want the device**, a session or two with a BCIA-certified practitioner to find your
resonance frequency is a reasonable, bounded expense.

**Before committing to neurofeedback**, weigh the cost against a literature in which the effect
largely disappears under blinded assessment. For ADHD specifically, the treatments with the
strongest evidence are medication and behavioural approaches, and those conversations belong with
a psychiatrist or paediatrician first.

## When this isn't enough

For ADHD, this article is not a reason to avoid treatment — it is a reason to ask about the
better-evidenced options first.

For anxiety and depression, HRV biofeedback is reasonable as a complement, and the
[treatments in this series](/clinic/what-cbt-actually-is) are where the substantive evidence sits.

And if you are in crisis, contact your doctor, a crisis line, or emergency services.
