Most couples therapy targets communication: learn to listen, learn to phrase things better, learn
to negotiate. Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy starts somewhere else.

Its premise is that the arguments are almost never about what they appear to be about. Underneath
the row about the dishes is a question — *are you there for me?* — and the whole treatment is
organized around getting that question said out loud and answered.

## What it actually is

EFCT was developed by Sue Johnson and Les Greenberg in the 1980s, and
[integrates systems theory, experiential methods and humanistic family therapy, grounded in
attachment theory](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/363783246_A_comprehensive_meta-analysis_on_the_efficacy_of_emotionally_focused_couple_therapy).

The core idea: adult romantic partners function as attachment figures, in the same way caregivers
do for children. When the bond feels insecure, people default to protest behaviours — pursuing,
criticizing, demanding — or to withdrawal. Those two responses trigger each other, producing the
**negative cycle** that couples arrive describing as "we always have the same fight."

EFCT works in three stages: **de-escalating** the cycle by naming it as the shared enemy;
**restructuring** the bond by helping each partner express the vulnerable emotion beneath their
usual position and helping the other respond to it; and **consolidating** the new pattern.

**Two versions exist**, which is worth knowing when you book:
[the Johnson version focuses on attachment processes, the Greenberg version on affect
regulation](https://paulbuerkner.com/publications/pdf/2018__Rathgeber_et_al__Journal_of_Marital_and_Family_Therapy.pdf).
Both have been shown effective in randomized trials.

## What the research shows

### The famous number, and where it comes from

You will see it everywhere: an effect size of **1.3**, described on EFT centres' own websites as
[larger than any other couple intervention has
achieved](https://sfceft.com/eft-resources/eft-research/).

Here is its provenance. That figure comes from a meta-analysis of the four most rigorous outcome
studies conducted **before the year 2000** — and its lead author was Sue Johnson, the model's
developer. A later independent meta-analysis describes it as
[d = 1.31, computed from seven partly randomized studies (Johnson,
1999)](https://paulbuerkner.com/publications/pdf/2018__Rathgeber_et_al__Journal_of_Marital_and_Family_Therapy.pdf).

Four to seven studies, partly randomized, more than twenty-five years old, analysed by the person
who created the treatment. We flag the same problem in many other articles, including [EFT
tapping](/journal/eft-tapping-examined), [Transcendental Meditation](/journal/mantra-meditation),
[grounding](/journal/grounding-earthing-examined), [metacognitive
therapy](/clinic/metacognitive-therapy) and [schema therapy](/clinic/schema-therapy). This is the
one most widely quoted as though it were settled.

### The better evidence

A **comprehensive meta-analysis** published in 2022 evaluated
[the entirety of the EFCT efficacy literature — randomized controlled trials, quasi-experimental
studies and
dissertations](https://www.ovid.com/journals/cfprp/fulltext/10.1037/cfp0000233~a-comprehensive-meta-analysis-on-the-efficacy-of-emotionally),
across a range of presenting concerns including depression, PTSD and sexual dissatisfaction, and
found **good maintenance of treatment gains up to two years** after treatment.

That durability finding is repeated elsewhere: results have been reported as stable at two-year
follow-up, with a separate study examining attachment change and sexual satisfaction outcomes at
two years.

Beyond relationship satisfaction, a randomized controlled trial compared EFCT against treatment as
usual **for depression**, examining outcomes and mechanisms of change — an unusual and valuable
design, since it tests whether treating the relationship treats the individual.

And a **multicentre randomized waitlist-controlled trial** is underway across
[Argentina, Costa Rica, Guatemala, Mexico and Spain](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9587591/),
with 70 couples receiving 19-21 sessions — addressing a real gap, since most of this literature
comes from North America.

**Where the evidence stands.** Consistent efficacy across a reasonable body of trials with unusually good long-term
maintenance — resting on a headline figure that is old, small and developer-authored, with
independent replication at scale still accumulating.

### What EFCT does have that is genuinely unusual

Its advocates make one claim this journal finds credible: EFCT has
[a body of process research showing that change occurs in the way the theory
predicts](https://sfceft.com/eft-resources/eft-research/), linking in-session process to outcome.

That matters. Most therapies demonstrate that something works without demonstrating *why*. Where
this series has repeatedly found mechanism claims unsupported, EFCT has done more than most to
test its own.

## What it does not do

**It does not save every relationship, and it should not try to.** A legitimate outcome of couples
therapy is a clearer, kinder decision to separate.

**It is not communication skills training.** People arriving expecting techniques for phrasing
things better are often surprised by how emotionally direct the sessions are.

**It is not appropriate where there is intimate partner violence.** This is the firm line in all
couples work. Conjoint therapy in the presence of ongoing abuse can increase risk, and a competent
therapist screens for it — usually in separate individual sessions — before agreeing to proceed.
If this applies to you, individual support and safety planning come first.

**And the individual-outcome evidence is younger** than the relationship-satisfaction evidence.
EFCT for depression is a promising application, not an established treatment for it.

## What the sessions actually involve

The first two or three sessions are assessment, including individual sessions with each partner —
which is where screening for violence, affairs and hidden agendas happens.

**Then the cycle gets mapped**, usually explicitly and often drawn out. *You withdraw, which reads
to her as not caring, so she escalates, which reads to you as attack, so you withdraw further.*
Most couples find this stage a relief: the problem stops being the other person and becomes the
pattern.

**Stage two is the emotionally demanding part.** The therapist slows things down and works with
one partner to reach the softer emotion underneath the familiar position — the fear beneath the
criticism, the shame beneath the withdrawal — and then turns to the other partner and asks them
to respond to it. These sessions are described by therapists as choreographed and by clients as
exposing.

**Enactments** are central: rather than describing a feeling to the therapist, you are asked to
turn and say it to your partner, now.

**Stage three** consolidates — revisiting old problems from the new position and building a
narrative of the change.

Typically 8 to 20 sessions; the multicentre trial protocol uses 19 to 21.

## Is this available to me?

EFCT is one of the most widely disseminated couples therapies internationally.

**Where to look:** ICEEFT — the International Centre for Excellence in Emotionally Focused
Therapy — maintains a directory of certified therapists and affiliated regional centres,
including in California.

**Ask about certification.** ICEEFT certification requires supervised cases and is a meaningful
credential. Many therapists describe themselves as EFT-informed with far less training.

**Ask which version** — Johnson or Greenberg — if you have a preference between an attachment
focus and an affect-regulation focus.

Couples therapy is **frequently not covered by insurance**, because relationship distress is not a
billable diagnosis. Where one partner has a diagnosable condition it sometimes is. Ask directly.

## How to ask for it

> *"We'd like emotionally focused couple therapy — the Sue Johnson attachment model, not the
> tapping one. Are you ICEEFT certified, and roughly how many sessions?"*

That first clarification is not a joke. Practitioners of both exist, and the acronym does real
damage.

## Who this suits

EFCT is worth asking about specifically if:

- You have **the same fight repeatedly** and can predict its whole course
- The pattern is **one pursuing, one withdrawing** — the classic configuration this model was
  built around
- **Emotional distance** rather than practical conflict is the presenting problem
- You are willing to be **emotionally exposed in front of your partner**, which is the actual
  demand of the treatment

It suits less well couples wanting practical negotiation of concrete disputes — money, in-laws,
division of labour — where behavioural couple therapy is the better-matched option, and it is not
appropriate where there is ongoing violence.

## When this isn't enough

If one partner is unwilling to attend, individual therapy focused on the relationship is a
legitimate alternative and can shift a cycle unilaterally.

If a course produces no change, behavioural couple therapy has its own evidence base and a
different mechanism.

And if the honest answer is that the relationship should end, a good EFCT therapist will help with
that rather than treating it as failure. Discernment counselling — a brief, explicitly
decision-focused format — exists for couples who are not sure whether they are trying to repair or
to separate.
