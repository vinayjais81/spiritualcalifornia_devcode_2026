Prolonged exposure asks you to do the thing every instinct tells you not to: describe the worst
event of your life, out loud, in the present tense, repeatedly, and then listen to a recording
of yourself doing it.

It is one of three trauma-focused therapies carrying a **"Strong For"** recommendation in the
2023 VA/DoD clinical practice guideline. It is also, in the largest trial ever run, the one more
than half of participants did not finish.

Both of those facts belong in the same article, and most descriptions of PE include only one.

## What it actually is

Prolonged exposure has four components:

**Psychoeducation** about how avoidance maintains PTSD. The theory is simple: after trauma, you
avoid reminders; avoidance provides immediate relief and prevents the fear from ever
extinguishing; the relief reinforces more avoidance. PTSD is maintained by the coping.

**Breathing retraining**, taught early as a stabilization tool.

**In vivo exposure** — approaching avoided but objectively safe situations in a graded way,
between sessions. Driving again. The supermarket. Crowds.

**Imaginal exposure** — the distinctive component. You recount the traumatic memory aloud, in the
first person, present tense, with eyes closed, for 30 to 45 minutes. The therapist records it.
You listen to the recording daily between sessions.

The rationale is emotional processing: repeated, prolonged contact with the memory in a safe
context allows the fear response to habituate and the memory to be reorganized as something that
happened rather than something that is happening.

## What the research shows

### The trial that settled the comparison

Schnurr and colleagues ran the **largest psychotherapy trial for PTSD ever conducted**: 916
veterans across 17 VA medical centres, randomized to prolonged exposure or cognitive processing
therapy under a flexible protocol of 10 to 14 sessions, with the primary outcome — CAPS-5, a
clinician-administered interview — assessed under centralized masking
([*JAMA Network Open*, 2022](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2788220)).

The result was carefully worded and worth quoting precisely:
[**PE was more effective than CPT for reducing PTSD symptoms, but the difference did not reach the
predetermined threshold for clinical significance**](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8771295/).

PE was more likely to produce treatment response, loss of diagnosis and remission — but the
authors flag that, owing to an administrative error, those outcomes
[were not preregistered and must be interpreted with
caution](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/357941061_Comparison_of_Prolonged_Exposure_vs_Cognitive_Processing_Therapy_for_Treatment_of_Posttraumatic_Stress_Disorder_Among_US_Veterans_A_Randomized_Clinical_Trial).
Treatments did not differ on other symptoms, functioning or quality of life.

The trialists also ruled out the obvious confound: the greater effect in PE was not explained by
higher therapist adherence or competence.

An earlier meta-analysis had found standardized mean differences of **1.23 for exposure therapy
and 1.35 for CPT** — both large, both in the same territory.

### The dropout problem, stated plainly

In that trial, dropout was **56% in the PE arm and 47% in CPT**, using a strict definition —
failure to complete 100% of protocol sessions. Mean sessions attended were 8.2 for PE and 9.1
for CPT. The researchers note these rates were
[comparable to other recent VA psychotherapy studies and to real-world
practice](https://www.research.va.gov/currents/0122-Prolonged-exposure-or-cognitive-processing-therapy.cfm).

A separate randomized trial examining dropout directly found the same pattern —
[52.3% in PE versus 45.8% in CPT, with the difference becoming significant after session
three](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13048278/) — and identified *when* people leave.
PE dropout peaks **after the first imaginal exposure session**. Notably, symptom worsening was
*not* associated with dropping out of PE, which suggests the driver is anticipatory anxiety about
the next session rather than the treatment making people worse.

Higher depression severity at any session predicted dropout in both treatments.

**Where the evidence stands.** Strongly recommended by the most recent US military guideline, supported by the
largest trial in the field, with a large effect size — and a completion problem that is a
genuine feature of the treatment rather than a footnote.

## What it does not do

**It does not require you to relive the trauma unsupported.** The stabilization phase comes
first, sessions are structured, and the therapist is present throughout the imaginal work.

**It does not work if you avoid the homework.** Between-session listening is not optional; it is
where much of the exposure dose is delivered.

**It is not the only option, and the trial says so.** The authors' own conclusion was that
patient preference should be considered, because both treatments produced meaningful improvement
and did not differ on most outcomes. If you cannot face imaginal exposure, [cognitive processing
therapy](/clinic/cognitive-processing-therapy) and [EMDR](/clinic/emdr-ptsd-treatment) carry the
same guideline recommendation.

**Safety.** PE and CPT were comparable in safety in the trial, with one exception: psychiatric
hospitalization was somewhat more likely in CPT (5.0%) — though the authors note this is close to
the 4.2% background rate among VA patients with PTSD generally.

## What the sessions actually involve

**Sessions 1-2:** history, rationale, breathing retraining, and building the in vivo hierarchy —
a ranked list of avoided situations.

**Session 3** is the one that matters. The first imaginal exposure. You close your eyes and
describe the memory in the present tense — *I am walking toward the vehicle* — for 30 to 45
minutes, usually going through it several times. You rate distress every few minutes. It is
recorded.

Most people describe this session as among the hardest things they have done. It is also, per the
dropout data, the point at which people are most likely to leave.

**Sessions 4 onward:** repeat imaginal exposure, focusing progressively on the worst moments,
plus review of in vivo assignments. What typically happens across sessions is that distress
ratings during the recounting fall, and the narrative becomes more detailed and less fragmented.

**Homework:** listen to the recording daily, and complete in vivo assignments.

Sessions run 60 to 90 minutes. A 2015 randomized trial found 60-minute sessions
[statistically non-inferior to 90-minute sessions](https://www.ptsd.va.gov/professional/articles/article-pdf/id1586155.pdf),
so shorter appointments are legitimate.

Typical course: 8 to 15 sessions.

## Is this available to me?

**Through the VA**, which has trained clinicians in PE system-wide and promotes it as a
front-line treatment. Ask at your VA medical centre or Vet Center.

**Privately**, through therapists trained in PE — training is delivered through the Center for
the Treatment and Study of Anxiety at the University of Pennsylvania and affiliated programmes.
Ask specifically whether they have completed PE training and how many courses they have
delivered.

**Via telehealth**, which is well established for PE. If you are in California, note that your
therapist generally needs a California licence — see [our article on finding a
therapist](/clinic/finding-a-therapist).

Billed as psychotherapy, so standard coverage applies.

## How to ask for it

> *"I'd like a trauma-focused therapy for PTSD. I know the VA/DoD guideline strongly recommends
> prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy and EMDR. Can you tell me which are available
> here, and help me choose?"*

And one question to ask yourself honestly before starting: **can I commit to daily homework for
two to three months?** The dropout data suggests that is the real determinant, and it is better
to know before session three than after it.

## When this isn't enough

If you drop out, that is common — not a personal failure, and not a reason to conclude that
treatment cannot help you. The most useful thing is to say *why* to your clinician. If it was
anticipatory dread of the imaginal sessions, CPT involves no imaginal exposure and has better
retention.

Trauma reprocessing is generally not the first step if you are in crisis, actively suicidal, or
in an unsafe living situation. Stabilization and safety come first, and a competent trauma
therapist will say so.

If you are managing substance use alongside PTSD, note that the 2023 VA/DoD guideline states
comorbidities should **not** preclude you from receiving a recommended treatment — a point worth
raising if you are turned away.
