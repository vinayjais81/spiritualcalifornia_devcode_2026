In 1936, a psychologist named Saul Rosenzweig published a short paper suggesting that the
competing schools of psychotherapy might all be working for reasons none of them had named. He
borrowed a line from *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland*, where the Dodo adjudicates a chaotic
race by declaring that everybody has won and all must have prizes.

Ninety years later, the **Dodo bird verdict** — the claim that bona fide therapies produce
roughly equivalent outcomes — remains one of the most argued-over findings in clinical
psychology. It is also the single most useful thing for a person choosing a therapist to
understand, and almost nobody explains it to them.

This article is the frame for everything else in this series. It is also, we should say plainly,
the same conclusion our fifty-article journal on wellness modalities kept arriving at from the
opposite direction.

## What "common factors" means

Common factors are the elements shared by all psychotherapies, regardless of school: the
relationship, the expectation of improvement, a credible explanation for what is wrong, a
structured ritual for addressing it, and a professional paying sustained attention to you.

**Specific factors** are the branded techniques — the exposure hierarchy, the thought record, the
eye movements, the empty chair.

The question is how the work is divided between them. Grencavage and Norcross reviewed the
literature in 1990 and identified **89 candidate common factors**, grouped into five categories:
patient characteristics, therapist qualities, change processes, treatment structure, and
relationship elements. It is a crowded field.

## What the research shows

### The alliance is the best-supported single predictor

The **therapeutic alliance** — broadly, agreement on goals, agreement on tasks, and the bond
between therapist and client — is the most investigated variable in psychotherapy research.

Flückiger, Del Re, Wampold and Horvath conducted the definitive synthesis: a three-level
meta-analysis of **295 independent studies covering more than 30,000 patients**, published
between 1978 and 2017, across both face-to-face and internet-delivered therapy. The overall
alliance-outcome association for face-to-face psychotherapy was
[**r = .278 (95% CI .256 to .299), equivalent to d = .579**](https://www.ovid.com/journals/psythy/fulltext/10.1037/pst0000172~the-alliance-in-adult-psychotherapy-a-meta-analytic).

That is a moderate effect, found consistently, irrespective of theoretical orientation. It holds
when controlling for patients' intake characteristics and for therapists' adherence and
competence. A parallel meta-analysis in child and adolescent therapy found
[r = .19 across 28 studies and 2,419 young people](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30335449/),
consistent with the adult literature.

To be honest about it: there was heterogeneity, and **2% of the 295 effect sizes were negative**.
This is a robust average, not a law.

### The brand of therapy predicts much less

The Dodo bird conjecture has been tested directly. A meta-analysis comparing bona fide treatments
against each other found effect sizes
[homogeneously distributed about zero, with an upper bound on the true difference of about
.20](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51708456_How_Central_Is_the_Alliance_in_Psychotherapy_A_Multilevel_Longitudinal_Meta-Analysis).

Two details from that analysis are what make it persuasive. Effect sizes were **not** related to
publication date — improving research methods were not gradually uncovering differences. And they
were **not** related to how dissimilar the treatments were — comparing two very different
therapies did not produce bigger gaps, as it should if technique were doing the heavy lifting.

There is a beautiful illustration of how this plays out. A 1998 meta-analysis found CBT superior
to other therapies for depression. When the comparison therapies were separated into **bona
fide** treatments and **non-bona-fide** ones — conditions designed to be inert, or delivered
without genuine therapeutic intent — CBT was
[no more effective than the other bona fide therapies](https://www.hhs.nd.gov/sites/www/files/documents/BH/cuijpers_2019_annurev-clinpsy-common-factors.pdf).
The apparent superiority came from comparisons with things nobody was seriously offering.

### The counter-argument, which is strong

We are not going to present this as settled, because it is not.

Pim Cuijpers, reviewing the field for the *Annual Review of Clinical Psychology*, makes an
objection that lands: the alliance-outcome relationship is **correlational**. Factor-outcome
correlations
[cannot establish the relative importance of those factors, let alone their causal
impact](https://www.hhs.nd.gov/sites/www/files/documents/BH/cuijpers_2019_annurev-clinpsy-common-factors.pdf).

The direction of causation is genuinely open. A good alliance might produce improvement — or
improving might produce a good alliance. Someone whose symptoms are lifting will plausibly rate
their therapist more warmly.

Cuijpers also notes evidence that cuts directly against the strong common-factors position: when
a specific component is **added** to an existing therapy, a small but significant effect appears
— and it is larger at follow-up. If technique were inert, adding technique should do nothing.

**Where the evidence stands:** a body of evidence that is large, replicated and genuinely contested in its
interpretation. What is not seriously disputed: the relationship matters a great deal, and the
differences between established therapies are smaller than their marketing implies.

## What this does not mean

**It does not mean all therapies are interchangeable for all problems.** This is the most common
misreading and it is wrong in ways that matter. Exposure and response prevention for OCD,
CBT-I for insomnia, and trauma-focused therapies for PTSD have specific evidence for specific
conditions, and generic supportive therapy is not equivalent for those. The Dodo bird verdict
concerns *bona fide* therapies compared for *general* outcomes — not whether any therapy works
for any diagnosis.

**It does not mean technique is irrelevant.** See the component-addition evidence above. It means
technique explains less of the variance than the field's branding suggests.

**It does not mean anyone warm can do this.** A credible rationale, a coherent structure and
professional training are themselves common factors. "Common" does not mean "free" or "easy."

**And it does not license the wellness industry's favourite conclusion** — that since the
relationship is what matters, credentials do not. Our journal's article on [Somatic
Experiencing](/journal/somatic-experiencing) covers what happens when trauma work is delivered by
people without an underlying clinical licence.

## What this means for you, practically

Four consequences, and they are the reason this article opens the series.

**1. Fit matters more than brand.** If you are choosing between a CBT therapist and a
psychodynamic one, the evidence says worry less about the school and more about whether you can
work with the person. That is not a soft recommendation — it is where the larger effect size is.

**2. You are allowed to change therapists.** If the relationship is not working after several
sessions, that is not a failure of therapy and not a character flaw. It is the single most
important variable going wrong.

**3. Agreement on goals and tasks is half the alliance.** The bond gets all the attention, but
the construct also includes agreeing on *what you are working toward* and *how*. If you do not
know what your therapist thinks you are doing, say so. That conversation is itself the
intervention.

**4. For specific conditions, get the specific treatment.** OCD, PTSD, insomnia, panic disorder
and eating disorders all have targeted protocols with evidence that general therapy does not
match. Fit still matters — but so does the protocol.

## Why this article opens the series

We spent fifty articles assessing wellness modalities against the evidence, and a pattern
appeared that we did not plan.

The needles mattered less than the practitioner and the setting. Real crystals performed
identically to plastic ones. Foot reflexology and hand reflexology produced the same result.
Repetitive drumming did not lower cortisol more than instrumental music. The float tank did not
clearly beat a comfortable chair in a dark room.

What survived, repeatedly, was ordinary: attention, ritual, expectation, unhurried time, and
being taken seriously by someone for an hour.

The psychotherapy literature has been describing the same phenomenon for ninety years, with far
better data — and, crucially, with the honesty to name it. When Cochrane reviewed dance/movement
therapy, its authors wrote that the therapy very likely works primarily through non-specific
factors, notably the therapeutic relationship, *and then* made the case for what was distinctive
about the embodied part.

That is the right posture for anyone offering anything. Name the common factor first. Then argue
for your specialty.
