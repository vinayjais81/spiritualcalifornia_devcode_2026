The evidence base for treating traumatized children is thinner than the adult literature and much
more crowded with well-intentioned approaches. In that context, one finding stands out: in a
meta-analysis of psychosocial treatments for children and adolescents exposed to traumatic
events, TF-CBT was
[**the only treatment meeting the "well-established criteria" threshold**](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1077559512451787).

It is recommended for paediatric PTSD by NICE, by ISTSS and by Phoenix Australia — and every one
of those guidelines specifies the same qualifier: **manualized, and with caregiver
participation.**

## What it actually is

TF-CBT was developed by Judith Cohen, Anthony Mannarino and Esther Deblinger, with the manual
published in 2006 and revised in 2017 to cover DSM-5, complex trauma and group settings. It is
designed for children and young people roughly **ages 3 to 21** who are showing emotional or
behavioural difficulties after traumatic events.

Its components form the acronym **PRACTICE**:

- **P** — Psychoeducation and Parenting skills
- **R** — Relaxation
- **A** — Affect identification and regulation
- **C** — Cognitive coping
- **T** — Trauma narration
- **I** — In vivo mastery of trauma reminders
- **C** — Conjoint child-caregiver sessions
- **E** — Enhancing safety and future development

These run across
[three phases, with roughly a third of treatment given to each](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8787755/):
skills-building for the child *and* parenting work for the caregiver, with gradual exposure to
trauma reminders woven through; then the **trauma narrative**, where the child describes and
processes what happened; then closure, conjoint sessions and safety planning.

**The structural feature that defines it:** sessions typically run 90 minutes, with
[45 minutes for the child and 45 for the caregiver](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0145213422004331),
partly separate and partly together. The caregiver is not an observer. They are receiving a
parallel treatment.

Typical course: 12 to 20 sessions, with a minimum of eight to cover the components.

## What the research shows

The most comprehensive synthesis pooled **4,523 participants from 28 randomized controlled trials
and 33 uncontrolled studies**. TF-CBT produced
[**large improvements across all outcomes from pre- to post-treatment (PTSS: g = 1.14, CI 0.97 to
1.30)**, and favourable results against any control condition — waitlist, treatment as usual, and
active treatment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36155943/).

**And the effects hold, or grow.** A follow-up meta-analysis from the same group examined 12-month
outcomes and found
[**PTSS g = 1.71 (CI 1.27 to 2.15) from pre-treatment to 12-month follow-up**, and g = 0.35 (CI
0.13 to 0.56) against active treatments and treatment as usual at that
point](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10775595231167383).

That durability is unusual in this journal. Most of what we have covered shows effects that fade
or go unmeasured past six months.

A long-term follow-up of 158 children aged 4 to 11 who had experienced sexual abuse found
improvements across 14 outcome measures sustained at 6 and 12 months — with
[**additional** improvement at 12 months on child-reported anxiety and on parental emotional
distress](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1077559512451787). Higher pre-treatment
internalizing and depressive symptoms predicted the small minority who continued to meet full
criteria.

**One counter-intuitive finding:** the meta-analyses reported **more pronounced effects in group
settings**. Group delivery is usually a cost compromise; here it may not be.

**Where the evidence stands.** The strongest evidence base in child trauma treatment, replicated, durable at twelve
months, and recommended across international guidelines.

## What it does not do

**The evidence is skewed toward sexual abuse.** TF-CBT was developed for children who had
experienced sexual abuse, and
[the literature remains concentrated on those samples](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0190740925000428).
It has since been applied to physical abuse, community violence, grief and disaster, and reviews
of those applications are more recent and thinner. The treatment is very likely appropriate; the
evidence for each specific trauma type is not equally deep.

**It requires a participating caregiver, and that is a real constraint.** The model rests on the
caregiver being able to hear the trauma narrative, manage their own distress, and support the
child afterwards. Where the caregiver is the person who caused the harm, is unavailable, or is
too overwhelmed to participate, the standard model does not straightforwardly apply. Adaptations
exist — for foster care, for residential settings — but this is the honest limitation, and any
service should discuss it openly rather than proceeding as though a caregiver slot will fill
itself.

**It is not play therapy.** It is a structured cognitive-behavioural protocol with a manual,
components and a sequence.

**And it does not avoid the trauma.** The trauma narrative is the core exposure component, and the
child will describe what happened.

## What the sessions actually involve

**Early sessions** are skills work and rarely touch the trauma directly. The child learns to name
feelings, to use relaxation, to notice thoughts. In parallel, the caregiver learns about trauma
responses — why the child is aggressive, or clingy, or not sleeping — and gets behaviour
management support. Many parents describe this phase as the first time anyone explained what was
happening to their child.

**The middle third is the trauma narrative.** The child builds an account of what happened —
often as a book, with chapters, sometimes illustrated, sometimes dictated for younger children.
It is built gradually across sessions, revisited, and expanded into the harder parts. Cognitive
processing happens here: examining the beliefs the child formed, especially self-blame.

**The caregiver is prepared separately** to hear the narrative, and this preparation is treated as
serious clinical work. Then, in a conjoint session, the child shares it. That session is
frequently described as the pivot of the whole treatment.

**Final sessions** cover safety planning, future development, and ending.

**In vivo mastery** runs alongside where relevant — gradually approaching avoided reminders, like
returning to a place the child now refuses to go.

## Is this available to me?

TF-CBT is among the most widely disseminated child trauma treatments in the US, largely through
the **National Child Traumatic Stress Network**.

**Where to look:** children's advocacy centres, child and adolescent mental health services,
county behavioural health, and university-affiliated child trauma clinics. In California,
children's advocacy centres are usually the fastest route for abuse-related trauma and are
accustomed to coordinating with child protective services and law enforcement.

**Ask whether the clinician has completed formal TF-CBT training** — there is a recognized web
course and certification pathway — and whether they follow the full protocol including the
caregiver component.

**Group formats** exist and, per the meta-analyses, may perform particularly well.

Billed as psychotherapy. Many children's advocacy centre services are free.

## How to ask for it

To a paediatrician, school counsellor or child mental health service:

> *"My child experienced [event] and is having difficulties since. I'd like a referral for
> trauma-focused CBT — the manualized Cohen, Mannarino and Deblinger model with caregiver
> participation, which is what NICE and ISTSS recommend for children. Is there a TF-CBT-trained
> clinician available?"*

Two questions worth asking the clinician:

- **"Do you use the full PRACTICE protocol, including the trauma narrative and conjoint
  sessions?"** Some services deliver the skills components and stop before the narrative, which
  is the exposure element.
- **"What's my role, and how much of each session am I in?"** If the answer does not involve you
  substantially, this is not TF-CBT as tested.

## A note for parents

Two things worth knowing before you start.

**Your distress is part of the treatment target**, not a distraction from it. The follow-up data
showed parental emotional distress continuing to improve at twelve months. If you are struggling
with what happened to your child, that is expected, it is addressed, and it is not something to
conceal from the clinician.

**Hearing the narrative is hard, and you will be prepared for it.** Parents frequently report
dreading the conjoint session and then describing it afterwards as the moment things changed —
for the child, because it was heard, and for them, because it was survivable.

## When this isn't enough

If your child's symptoms are severe, if there is self-harm or suicidal thinking, or if the child
is still in an unsafe situation, safety and stabilization come before trauma processing.

If a caregiver cannot participate, say so early — adaptations exist and a good service will
problem-solve rather than exclude.

And if the trauma involved multiple or continuing events over years rather than a discrete
incident, [narrative exposure therapy](/clinic/narrative-exposure-therapy) has a child adaptation
(KIDNET), though its evidence in children is of lower quality than the adult work.
