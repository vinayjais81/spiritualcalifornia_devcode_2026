Prolonged exposure asks you to work on the worst event. Cognitive processing therapy asks what
you concluded from it. Both assume there is an *it*.

For someone who fled a war, survived torture, or spent years in a situation where violence was
ordinary, that assumption fails. The traumatic events are numerous, their edges blur, and — as
the NET literature puts it — the fear networks of different events overlap, so it becomes
impossible to identify the worst one before treatment even begins.

Narrative Exposure Therapy was designed for exactly that problem.

## What it actually is

Instead of selecting one event, **you construct a narrative of your entire life**, from birth to
the present, in chronological order.

The physical form this takes is unusual and, by most accounts, powerful. A **lifeline** is laid
out on the floor: a length of rope, with objects placed along it in chronological order.
[**Flowers for happy events, stones for traumatic ones, candles for grief**](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4265174/)
— and in the version adapted for people who have committed violence, sticks for acts of
aggression.

Then, session by session, you narrate the whole line in order. Not only the atrocities — the good
events too, in detail, because part of the point is restoring a life story rather than a
catalogue of harm.

The therapist writes as you speak. At each traumatic point, you slow down and elaborate: the
sensory, physiological, emotional and cognitive details, told in the present tense, until the
memory becomes an organized narrative rather than a fragmented set of intrusions.

**And it ends with a document.** On the final day, the biography is read back, and the patient,
interpreter and therapist
[review and sign it](https://www.thepermanentejournal.org/doi/10.7812/TPP/12-058). Many people
choose to send a copy to human rights organizations as testimony.

That artifact is unique among the treatments in this series. No other therapy produces a signed
record of what happened to you.

## What the research shows

NET grew out of testimony therapy and cognitive behavioural approaches and was developed for
low-resource crisis settings where long-term treatment was impossible. It is typically
[**12 sessions or fewer**, individual or in small
groups](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9476084/).

A 2014 review reported good evidence from
[**18 RCTs (N = 950)**, and described NET as the most evidence-based trauma treatment for
culturally diverse populations alongside culturally adapted
CBT](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4265174/), with trials conducted across Africa,
Europe and Asia.

The most thorough synthesis is Raghuraman and colleagues' 2021 meta-analysis in *Clinical
Psychology & Psychotherapy*, which pooled
[**24 RCTs** and examined PTSD diagnosis, PTSD symptoms and depression symptoms at short-term
(3-4 months), midterm (6-7 months) and long-term (≥12 months)
follow-up](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/cpp.2486). It found a positive overall
effect on PTSD symptoms — and the authors
[advise caution in interpreting it, because of high heterogeneity and low quality of evidence
across trials](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/cpp.3044).

An earlier meta-analysis found large uncontrolled effect sizes at post-treatment and follow-up.

**Two other consistent findings.** NET has
[**low dropout rates**](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7043101/), which matters
enormously in a field where half of people leave. And it can be delivered by **lay counsellors** —
it has been evaluated this way with Sudanese refugees in Cairo, among others — which makes it
deployable where there are no psychologists at all.

### Where the guidelines disagree

**NICE recommends NET** as one of its interventions for adult PTSD, though NET trials are
frequently analysed under the broader trauma-focused CBT umbrella rather than separately.

**The 2023 VA/DoD guideline moved the other way.** In its stricter GRADE review, narrative
exposure therapy was downgraded from a strong recommendation to *insufficient evidence — neither
for nor against*, alongside brief eclectic psychotherapy.

As with [Written Exposure Therapy](/clinic/written-exposure-therapy), commentators argued no new
evidence had called the downgraded therapies into question — the issue was trial volume, and
research investment has gone disproportionately to prolonged exposure and CPT.

Reviewers elsewhere put it plainly: NET is
[a suggested intervention for adult PTSD, at a lower level of evidence than established guideline
treatments such as TF-CBT](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/cpp.3044).

**Where the evidence stands.** A substantial and international RCT base, low dropout, NICE recommendation — with
high heterogeneity, quality concerns, and a US guideline that currently declines to recommend it
either way.

## What it does not do

**It is not general narrative therapy.** Narrative therapy in the family-therapy tradition is a
different thing entirely. NET is a manualized exposure-based protocol.

**It is not gentle because it is chronological.** You narrate every traumatic event on the
lifeline in detail. The structure makes it tolerable; it does not make it mild.

**The children's version is weaker.** KIDNET exists and is used, but studies in children
[have been of lower quality than those in adult populations](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7043101/).

**And it has not been shown superior to TF-CBT.** Where both are available, TF-CBT has the larger
evidence base. NET's argument is fit for a specific population, not general superiority.

## What the sessions actually involve

**Session 1:** psychoeducation and the lifeline. This is often described as the most striking
session — laying out a rope and placing objects for every significant event, good and bad, in
order. Many people see the shape of their own life for the first time.

**Sessions 2 onward:** narration in chronological order. The therapist writes continuously. At
traumatic points you slow down and go into detail, staying with it until distress reduces within
the session. Then you move forward along the line.

**Final session:** the completed biography is read aloud, corrected, and signed.

Twelve sessions or fewer is typical, often 90 minutes each. With an interpreter, sessions are
longer.

What people report: the writing matters more than expected. Watching someone record your account
carefully, and receiving a document at the end, is described by many as the point at which the
experience stopped being something happening inside them and became something that happened,
documented, in the past.

## Is this available to me?

**This is the honest constraint in the US.** NET is far more established in Europe and in
humanitarian settings than in American clinical practice, and the VA/DoD position has not helped
dissemination.

**Where to look:** refugee and asylum-seeker mental health services, torture-survivor treatment
programmes, university-affiliated trauma centres, and organizations working with resettled
populations. In California — with among the largest refugee and immigrant populations in the
country — these services exist in the major metropolitan areas.

**Ask about interpreters.** NET was designed to be delivered through interpreters and there is a
substantial literature on doing it well. A service that treats interpretation as an afterthought
is not set up for this work.

Billed as psychotherapy where delivered by a licensed clinician.

## How to ask for it

> *"I've experienced multiple traumatic events over a long period rather than one incident. I'd
> like to ask about Narrative Exposure Therapy — it was developed for exactly that, and NICE
> recommends it for adult PTSD. If it's not available here, what trauma-focused option would you
> suggest for multiple trauma?"*

That final clause matters. NET may not be findable near you. Naming *multiple and continuous
trauma* still routes the clinician correctly.

## Who this suits

On the evidence and the design, NET is worth asking about specifically if:

- You experienced **many traumatic events**, or ongoing danger over months or years
- You **cannot identify a single worst event** — which is a common reason people are turned away
  from or fail in other protocols
- You are a **refugee, asylum seeker, or survivor of organized violence or torture**
- A **written record** of what happened has value to you — for an asylum claim, for a human
  rights organization, or simply for yourself

That last point is worth weighing seriously. For someone whose account has been doubted by
immigration authorities, a signed clinical testimony is not just a therapeutic artifact.

## When this isn't enough

If you are still in danger, or your immigration status is unresolved and precarious, trauma
reprocessing may not be the right sequence. Stabilization, safety and practical advocacy usually
come first, and a good service will say so rather than starting narration.

If NET is unavailable, [TF-CBT](/clinic/what-cbt-actually-is),
[CPT](/clinic/cognitive-processing-therapy) and [EMDR](/clinic/emdr-ptsd-treatment) all carry
stronger US guideline positions, and adapted versions exist for complex presentations.

And if the events are ongoing rather than past — current violence at home, active exploitation —
that is a safety situation before it is a treatment question.
