Almost everyone offered group therapy has the same reaction: *I'd rather have the real thing.*
The assumption is that group is what you get when the system cannot afford individual work — a
budget substitute, delivered to strangers.

The evidence says otherwise, and it says so from an unusually well-designed body of research.

## What it actually is

Group therapy means a small number of people — typically six to twelve — meeting regularly with
one or two trained therapists. Sessions usually run 75 to 120 minutes, weekly.

**Formats vary more than people expect:**

- **Structured / psychoeducational** — a curriculum taught across weeks. Group
  [CBT](/clinic/what-cbt-actually-is), [MBCT](/clinic/mbct-relapse-prevention),
  [DBT skills](/clinic/dialectical-behaviour-therapy) all work this way. Closer to a class than
  a conversation.
- **Process / interpersonal** — no curriculum. The material is what happens between members in
  the room. This is what most people picture and it is the more demanding format.
- **Support / condition-specific** — organized around a shared situation: bereavement, cancer,
  new parenthood, chronic pain.

They are genuinely different experiences, and "group therapy" as a phrase flattens them.

## What the research shows

### The comparison that matters

The key study is Burlingame and colleagues' 25-year meta-analytic review in *Psychotherapy*
(2016), which did something earlier reviews had not: it separated comparisons where the
**treatment, patients and dose were identical** across formats from comparisons where they were
not.

That distinction matters enormously. Much of the earlier literature compared, say, twelve
individual sessions against six group ones and concluded individual worked better — which tests
dose, not format.

When the comparison was made properly, the result was:
[**no differences between formats for rates of treatment acceptance, dropout, remission, or
improvement**](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27918191/) — and no differences in outcome by
patient diagnosis.

Differences in pre-to-post improvement *were* explained by diagnosis, with depression, anxiety and
substance use disorders posting the highest outcomes, and medical and childhood disorders the
lowest. But that pattern applies to both formats equally.

**Note the dropout finding in particular.** The most common objection to group is that people
leave. In matched comparisons, they do not leave more than they leave individual therapy.

Group psychotherapy has been shown equivalent to individual therapy for
[anxiety, depression, grief, eating disorders and
schizophrenia](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9893048/).

### Cohesion: the group's version of the alliance

Our [flagship article](/clinic/common-factors-dodo-bird) covers the therapeutic alliance — the
strongest single predictor of outcome in individual therapy, at r = .278 across 295 studies.

Group therapy has its own equivalent construct: **cohesion**, the bond between members and with
the therapist. Burlingame's meta-analysis of the cohesion–outcome relationship found an effect
of
[**0.56 — almost identical to the alliance–outcome relationship found in individual
psychotherapy**](https://findings.org.uk/PHP/dl.php?f=Burlingame_GM_2.cab&s=eb&sf=rel), across
55 studies.

Two things follow. **The relationship is doing the work in group as much as in individual** — it
is simply distributed across more people. And **the group is not incidental to the treatment.**
Where a health system treats group as a container for delivering content efficiently, it is
missing the mechanism.

The same caution applies as with the alliance: it is not possible to determine whether better
cohesion *causes* better outcomes, and findings in substance use therapy have been inconsistent.

### What group offers that individual cannot

Beyond symptom reduction, group has been described as offering members
[a sense of belonging, purpose, hope, altruism and
meaning](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9893048/) — the curative factors Yalom
identified decades ago.

There is a critique worth knowing here, and it comes from inside the field. The push to prove
equivalence with individual therapy through randomized trials has, researchers argue, produced a
literature that **prioritizes symptom reduction while neglecting the relational and emotional
mechanisms that made group distinctive in the first place**. Group researchers historically
focused on those; the need to compete with individual treatment shifted attention to cohesion,
climate and symptom change.

That is an unusually candid observation about how funding and comparison pressures shape what
gets studied.

**Where the evidence stands.** Equivalence with individual therapy is well established across a
25-year matched meta-analytic comparison, with a relationship mechanism as strong as individual
therapy's. Reviewers conclude the evidence should encourage clinicians to increase their use of
group therapy and acquire competence in it.

## What it does not do

**It is not cheaper care that works worse** — that is precisely the assumption the matched
comparison refutes. It *is* cheaper, with a favourable cost-benefit ratio. Both things are true.

**It is not group-shaped individual therapy.** Taking turns receiving the therapist's attention
while others watch is a failure mode, not the format. In a well-run group, members work with each
other.

**It does not suit every presentation.** Acute crisis, active psychosis and severe social phobia
at the point of entry may need individual work first — though social anxiety is also one of the
conditions group treats well, once someone can get through the door.

**And it is not confidential in the way individual therapy is.** Groups have confidentiality
agreements, and those agreements depend on other members honouring them. That is a real
difference and worth naming rather than glossing.

## What the sessions actually involve

**A structured group** resembles a class. Handouts, a whiteboard, a topic for the week, homework
review. Personal disclosure is limited and voluntary. Many people find this format far less
exposing than expected — you can attend eight weeks of a skills group and say very little.

**A process group** is the demanding one. There is no agenda. The therapist says almost nothing
for long stretches. The silence at the start of an early session is, by universal report,
excruciating.

What happens then is the mechanism: someone speaks, someone else reacts, and the therapist draws
attention to what just occurred between them. *You went quiet when she said that. What happened?*
The group becomes a live sample of how each member relates to people generally — which is
information you cannot get from describing your relationships to one therapist in private.

**The first three sessions are the hardest.** Dropout, where it happens, clusters early. Most
people who stay past session four stay to the end.

Typical courses: 8 to 16 weeks for structured groups, considerably longer or open-ended for
process groups.

## Is this available to me?

Group is often the **most available** thing in mental health care, and frequently the shortest
wait.

**Where to look:** community mental health, county behavioural health, hospital outpatient
programmes, university counselling centres, and private practices running specific groups.

**Ask what kind of group it is** — structured, process, or support. They are different products.

**Ask about the therapist's group training.** Running a group well is a specific skill, not
individual therapy with more chairs. The American Group Psychotherapy Association offers a
**Certified Group Psychotherapist (CGP)** credential, which is the meaningful signal.

**Ask about screening.** Good groups screen members individually before entry, for fit and for
safety.

Billed as psychotherapy, usually at a substantially lower per-session rate than individual.

## How to ask for it

> *"I'd like to be considered for a group. Is it structured or process-based, how many people,
> how long does it run, and does the therapist have group-specific training?"*

And if you are offered group **instead of** individual therapy you were waiting for, the honest
position is that the matched evidence shows equivalence — so this is not a downgrade. It is
reasonable to take it, and also reasonable to ask to stay on the individual list if you have a
specific reason to prefer it.

## Why people avoid it, and what to do with that

The reluctance is nearly universal and worth taking seriously rather than arguing with.

**"I don't want to hear other people's problems."** In practice this is the thing most members
report as unexpectedly valuable — the altruism and universality factors. Being useful to someone
else is a documented mechanism, not a side effect.

**"I don't want to talk in front of strangers."** In structured groups you barely have to. In
process groups you can say so, out loud, in session one — and a competent therapist will treat
that as a legitimate starting point rather than resistance to be overcome.

**"It's not real therapy."** The matched 25-year comparison is the answer to this, and it is a
better answer than most beliefs in mental health care receive.

**"Someone will repeat what I say."** This one is not irrational. Ask how confidentiality is
handled and what happens if it is breached.

## When this isn't enough

If you are in acute crisis, actively suicidal, or in an unsafe situation, individual and crisis
care come first.

If a group is not working, the useful question is which *kind* of group — a process group can be
overwhelming for someone who would do well in a structured one, and vice versa.

And if you have a condition with a specific protocol — [OCD](/clinic/erp-for-ocd),
[PTSD](/clinic/prolonged-exposure-ptsd), [insomnia](/clinic/cbt-i-insomnia) — ask whether the
group delivers that protocol. Several of them are delivered in groups very effectively. A general
support group is a different offer.
