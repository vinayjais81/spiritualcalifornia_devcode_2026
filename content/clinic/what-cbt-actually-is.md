CBT is the most widely delivered psychotherapy in the world and the most widely misdescribed. It
is not positive thinking. It is not being told your feelings are irrational. It is not a
worksheet dispensary.

It is also not one thing. **CBT is a family**, and several of its members have their own articles
in this series: [CBT-I](/clinic/cbt-i-insomnia) for insomnia,
[ERP](/clinic/erp-for-ocd) for OCD, [prolonged exposure](/clinic/prolonged-exposure-ptsd) and
[cognitive processing therapy](/clinic/cognitive-processing-therapy) for PTSD, and
[behavioural activation](/clinic/behavioural-activation) for depression. Saying "I'm doing CBT"
is roughly as specific as saying "I'm having surgery."

## What it actually is

The core proposition: thoughts, feelings and behaviour influence one another, and the most
accessible lever is usually not the feeling.

**The cognitive half** examines the automatic interpretations that run beneath a reaction. Not
*is this thought positive* but *is this thought accurate, and is it the only available reading?*
The characteristic move is asking for evidence — including evidence against.

**The behavioural half** changes what you do: approaching what you have been avoiding, scheduling
activity, testing a prediction by running an experiment rather than arguing about it.

Three features distinguish CBT from most talk therapy:

- **It is structured.** Sessions have agendas. Treatment has a length.
- **It is present-focused.** The past explains how a pattern formed; the work is on what maintains
  it now.
- **It is homework-driven.** Most of the change happens between sessions. This is not a
  supplement to the therapy — for most CBT protocols it *is* the therapy.

## What the research shows

CBT has the largest evidence base of any psychotherapy. A review of 16 methodologically rigorous
meta-analyses found
[large effect sizes for CBT in unipolar depression, generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder
with or without agoraphobia, social phobia, PTSD, and childhood depressive and anxiety
disorders](https://www.academia.edu/97048081/The_effects_of_cognitive_behavioral_therapy_are_not_systematically_falling_A_revision_of_Johnsen_and_Friborg_2015_)
— with smaller effects for marital distress, anger and childhood somatic disorders.

That breadth is the reason CBT dominates training programmes and guidelines. But the most
instructive thing in this literature is an argument.

### The falling-effects controversy

In 2015, Johnsen and Friborg published a meta-analysis in *Psychological Bulletin* covering 70
studies from 1977 to 2014. The average effect sizes were enormous — **g = 1.58 on the Beck
Depression Inventory** (95% CI 1.43-1.74) and **1.69 on the Hamilton Rating Scale** (1.48-1.89).

Then came the finding that made news: metaregression indicated that
[the effects of CBT had **declined linearly and steadily since its introduction**](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26890388/)
— on patient self-report (p < .001), on clinician ratings (p < .01), and on remission rates
(p < .01). The decline held in both pre/post and controlled designs. Modern trials, the authors
concluded, were providing less relief than the seminal ones.

**Two re-analyses pushed back, and they are more persuasive.**

The first argued the model was wrong: fitting a curve rather than a straight line significantly
improved it, and once you did,
[significant decline appeared only between 1977 and 1995 (p = .001-.009) and not between 1995 and
2014 (p = .987-.785)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/313950617_The_effects_of_cognitive-behavioral_therapy_for_depression_are_not_falling_A_re-analysis_of_Johnsen_and_Friborg_2015).
The decline was driven by 22 studies published before 1995; the 48 published after showed none.

The second, by Cristea and colleagues, found **an additional 30 eligible randomized trials** the
original had missed and redid the analysis. Publication year
[was not a significant predictor in any analysis that used between-group effect sizes and included
all eligible
trials](https://www.nationalelfservice.net/treatment/cbt/battle-of-the-meta-analyses-is-cbt-becoming-less-effective-over-time/).

**Why this matters more than it looks.** Early effect sizes in almost any field are inflated —
the phenomenon Ioannidis called the **winner's curse**, where the first study to find an effect
reports a larger one than any replication will. Small early trials, enthusiastic developers,
selected samples. What appears to be a treatment "losing power" is usually a field's estimate
converging on the truth.

CBT did not get worse. Our measurement of it got better.

**Where the evidence stands, with one caveat:** the modern effect size is smaller than the headline figures from
the 1980s, and that anyone quoting g = 1.58 is quoting a number the field has since refined.

### One finding worth sitting with

The same meta-analysis reported that
[**experienced psychologists achieved better results (g = 1.55) than less experienced student
therapists (g = 0.98)**](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26890388/), and that women profited more
than men.

The therapist-experience gap is interesting because it cuts against the simplest reading of the
[common factors literature](/clinic/common-factors-dodo-bird), and also against the simplest
reading of the [COBRA trial](/clinic/behavioural-activation), where junior workers delivering
behavioural activation matched experienced therapists delivering CBT. The honest position is that
these findings coexist and the field has not fully reconciled them.

## What it does not do

**It is not positive thinking.** If the evidence supports a painful conclusion, CBT does not ask
you to replace it with a cheerful one. It asks whether the conclusion is as total, as permanent
and as personal as it feels.

**It does not dismiss the past.** It declines to treat the past as the primary lever.

**It does not work without the homework.** This is the single most common reason CBT fails, and
it is worth knowing before you start rather than concluding at week eight that the treatment does
not suit you.

**It is not equally suited to everything.** The evidence is strongest for depression, anxiety
disorders, PTSD and OCD, and weaker for marital distress, anger and complex presentations.

**And "CBT" alone is not a specific enough answer.** If you have insomnia, you need CBT-I. If you
have OCD, you need ERP. If you have PTSD, you need a trauma-focused protocol. A general CBT
therapist may not deliver those, and the difference is not cosmetic — for OCD, generic
CBT-flavoured talk therapy can actively make things worse.

## What the sessions actually involve

**Sessions 1-2:** assessment, and building a shared formulation — a diagram, often literally drawn,
of how your thoughts, feelings, behaviours and physical sensations feed each other. You should
leave with it.

**Then the work**, which is repetitive by design. Each session has an agenda set at the start,
reviews last week's homework, introduces or practises a technique, and sets the next assignment.

Typical techniques: **thought records** (situation, automatic thought, evidence for and against,
alternative view); **behavioural experiments** (predict what will happen, do it, compare);
**activity scheduling**; **graded exposure**.

Homework every week. Fifteen minutes to an hour.

Standard courses run 12 to 20 sessions. Considerably shorter for specific protocols; considerably
longer for complex presentations.

**One thing to expect:** the early sessions often feel too simple. Writing down a thought and
asking for evidence can feel insulting when your problem is enormous. The technique's power is in
repetition, not in the insight of any single instance.

## Is this available to me?

CBT is the most widely available therapy in the US, and most licensed therapists claim
competence in it — which is precisely the problem. Claiming CBT and delivering protocol-faithful
CBT are different things.

**Look for specific training** rather than a general claim. Certification through the Academy of
Cognitive and Behavioral Therapies is the substantive credential. Association for Behavioral and
Cognitive Therapies membership is a reasonable secondary signal.

**Ask about the protocol for your condition**, not about CBT generally.

**Digital and guided self-help formats** have real evidence for some conditions — see our
CBT-I article for the FDA-cleared prescription route, now Medicare-reimbursable.

Standard psychotherapy coverage applies.

## How to ask for it

> *"I'd like CBT for [condition]. Do you deliver a specific protocol for it, and roughly how many
> sessions? What does the homework involve?"*

If the answer to the second question is vague, you may be being offered supportive therapy with a
CBT label. That may still help — the [alliance literature](/clinic/common-factors-dodo-bird)
suggests it might — but it is not what the trials above tested.

## When this isn't enough

If a full course produces nothing, the options are real: a different protocol, a different
modality, medication, or combination treatment. Depression and anxiety both have multiple
evidence-based routes and CBT is not the only one — [IPT and behavioural
activation](/clinic/behavioural-activation) carry their own evidence.

If you are doing the homework and nothing is shifting, say so early. A structured therapy should
be able to tell you by session six or eight whether the plan needs to change.
