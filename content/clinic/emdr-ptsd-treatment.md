EMDR is the most-searched therapy name in this entire series, and the one most often described
in ways that make clinicians wince. It is also — and this is the part that gets lost — one of
only three trauma-focused psychotherapies that survived the most recent US military guideline
review with a strong recommendation intact.

It is simultaneously the treatment that a major professional body rated *below* its
cognitive-behavioural rivals two years later.

Both of those things are true, both reviews were competent, and the disagreement between them is
the most useful thing to understand about EMDR.

## What it actually is

Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing was developed by Francine Shapiro in the late
1980s. Treatment follows a standardized eight-phase protocol. The distinctive phase asks you to
hold a distressing memory in mind — the image, the belief attached to it, the bodily sensation —
while tracking the therapist's fingers moving side to side, or following a light bar, or
receiving alternating taps or tones. This is called **bilateral stimulation**.

Sets last twenty to thirty seconds. Between sets you report what came up, without editing, and
the therapist follows wherever attention has gone rather than steering it back.

What EMDR is *not*: hypnosis, and not a requirement to describe the traumatic event in narrative
detail. That second point is why many people choose it. You hold the memory; you do not
necessarily have to tell it.

## What the research shows

### The 2023 VA/DoD guideline, and what it changed

The US Department of Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense published version 4.0 of their
Clinical Practice Guideline for PTSD and Acute Stress Disorder in 2023, and it was a stricter
review than its predecessor. The work group evaluated each psychotherapy individually rather
than as a class, and applied GRADE more rigorously.

The result was a cull. Of seven trauma-focused therapies strongly recommended in the 2017
edition, **only three retained that status: Cognitive Processing Therapy, Prolonged Exposure, and
EMDR** ([U.S.
Medicine](https://www.usmedicine.com/clinical-topics/ptsd/critics-strongly-condemn-new-va-dod-guideline-on-ptsd-treatment/)).
Ehlers' cognitive therapy and Written Exposure Therapy were downgraded to a weaker suggestion;
brief eclectic psychotherapy and Narrative Exposure Therapy dropped to insufficient evidence.

EMDR carries a
[**"Strong For" strength of recommendation** in that guideline](https://health.mil/Reference-Center/Publications/2025/05/19/EMDR-PTSD-Evidence-Brief),
and the Military Health System relies on those guidelines to define best practice.

Three other things from the same document are worth knowing, because they rarely travel:

- **Psychotherapy is recommended over medication** for PTSD, on the grounds of larger and more
  persistent improvement ([ISTSS
  summary](https://istss.org/using-the-2023-va-dod-clinical-practice-guideline-for-management-of-posttraumatic-stress-disorder-and-acute-stress-disorder-ariel-j-lang-jessica-l-hamblen-paul-holtzheimer-ursula-kelly-sonya-b/)).
- Where medication is used, the recommended options are paroxetine, sertraline and venlafaxine.
- The guideline contains two recommendations **against** specific treatments, meaning strongly
  contraindicated: **benzodiazepines, and cannabis and its derivatives**.

That last point deserves more attention than it gets. Both are widely used by people with PTSD,
and the most recent US military guideline advises against both.

### The 2025 APA guideline, and the disagreement

Two years later, the American Psychological Association published its own updated clinical
practice guideline for PTSD in adults — and did not place EMDR in the same evidence category as
trauma-focused CBT approaches.

A critique published in June 2026 argues this was a methodological artefact rather than a finding
about EMDR. The APA's review
[defined its population as including only studies where 100% of participants met full PTSD
criteria](https://spj.science.org/doi/10.34133/jemdr.0034), a narrower inclusion rule than NICE
or ISTSS applied, and — the critique notes — none of the meta-analyses used examined follow-up
periods, despite the APA's own emphasis on maintenance of treatment gains.

This is not unique to the APA. A synopsis of the 2023 VA/DoD guideline in the *Annals of Internal
Medicine* compared five major guidelines and recorded that
[one did not recommend EMDR for military personnel, and one gave EMDR a weaker rating than other
therapies](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M23-2757).

**How to read that honestly:** EMDR is recommended by every major guideline body. Where they
differ is on whether it belongs in the same top tier as CPT and prolonged exposure, and the
divergence traces to inclusion criteria and analytic choices rather than to conflicting trial
results. That is a genuine methodological dispute, not a scandal.

### The eye movements themselves

The longstanding scientific question about EMDR is whether the bilateral stimulation adds
anything, or whether the treatment works because it contains exposure and cognitive processing —
the same ingredients as therapies that use no eye movements at all.

This has been argued for three decades and is not settled. We are not going to pretend otherwise,
and we would note that our [EFT tapping article](/journal/eft-tapping-examined) covers a
structurally identical dispute in a much weaker field.

What is worth saying is that **the guidelines recommend the protocol as delivered**. Whichever
component is doing the work, the package has been tested against controls repeatedly and it
performs. If your goal is recovery rather than mechanism, the open question matters less than it
appears to.

**Where the evidence stands.** Recommended by every major guideline body reviewed here, strongly recommended by the
most recent US military guideline, with a live and legitimate dispute about its relative
standing.

## What it does not do

**It does not erase memories.** The memory remains. What typically changes is the intensity of
the physical and emotional response attached to it.

**It is not a single-session fix**, despite anecdotes. Protocols typically run eight to twelve
sessions for single-incident trauma, and considerably longer for complex or repeated trauma.

**It is not established for everything it is offered for.** EMDR is marketed for anxiety,
depression, phobias, performance and chronic pain. The guideline-strength evidence is for PTSD.

**And it is not a gentler alternative to exposure therapies in the way it is often sold.** It
involves deliberately contacting distressing material. Sessions can be intense, and the
between-session period can be difficult.

## What the sessions actually involve

The first two or three sessions involve no reprocessing at all. You take a history, and the
therapist teaches stabilization techniques — a "calm place" exercise, grounding methods — that
you will use if a set becomes overwhelming. A therapist who skips this phase is not following
the protocol.

When reprocessing begins, you identify a target memory, an image that represents the worst part,
the negative belief attached to it ("I am not safe"), a preferred belief, and where you feel it
in your body. You rate the distress from 0 to 10.

Then the sets begin. Twenty to thirty seconds of following the light bar or the therapist's
fingers, then: *what came up?* You say whatever arrived — an image, a smell, an unrelated
memory, nothing. The therapist says *go with that*, and another set begins.

What people commonly report is that the material moves on its own, often somewhere unexpected,
and that this is disconcerting the first time. Distress ratings usually fall across a session,
though not always, and not always in a straight line.

Sessions run 60 to 90 minutes.

## Is this available to me?

**Coverage.** EMDR is delivered by licensed therapists and is generally billable as
psychotherapy under standard CPT codes, so if your plan covers therapy it typically covers EMDR.
Ask the practice to confirm.

**Veterans.** The VA provides EMDR as one of its recommended trauma-focused therapies. Ask at
your VA medical centre or Vet Center.

**Telehealth.** EMDR can be delivered remotely using on-screen bilateral stimulation, which
became routine after 2020 and remains widely available. Interstate practice depends on
licensure — see our forthcoming article on finding a therapist.

**Waiting times** for EMDR-trained clinicians in California are frequently longer than for
general therapy. Ask to be put on a cancellation list.

## How to ask for it

To a GP or psychiatrist:

> *"I think I may have PTSD. I'd like a referral for a trauma-focused psychotherapy — EMDR,
> cognitive processing therapy or prolonged exposure. The VA/DoD guideline recommends
> psychotherapy over medication for PTSD, and I'd like to start there."*

To a therapist you are considering:

- **"What is your EMDR training?"** Look for training accredited by **EMDRIA** — the EMDR
  International Association — and ask whether they have completed basic training only, or are
  EMDRIA-Certified, which requires additional consultation hours.
- **"What is your underlying licence?"** EMDR training is open to licensed mental health
  professionals; confirm the licence and check it with the California board.
- **"How many sessions before we'd review whether it's working?"**
- **"What do you do if I become overwhelmed during a set?"** A clear protocol answer is what you
  want.

## When this isn't enough

If EMDR is not helping after a reasonable course, the answer is not necessarily more EMDR. CPT
and prolonged exposure carry equally strong recommendations, and switching modality is a normal
clinical decision rather than a failure.

If you are in crisis, actively suicidal, or in an unsafe living situation, trauma reprocessing is
generally not the first step — stabilization and safety are. A competent trauma therapist will
say so.

And if you have been offered a body-based or energy-based trauma treatment as an alternative to
EMDR, it is worth knowing where those sit by comparison. Our articles on [Somatic
Experiencing](/journal/somatic-experiencing) and [Internal Family
Systems](/journal/internal-family-systems) set out their evidence bases honestly; both are
considerably weaker than what is described here.
