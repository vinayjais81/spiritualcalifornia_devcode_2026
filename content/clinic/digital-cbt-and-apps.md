There are tens of thousands of mental health apps. A few dozen have been through randomized
controlled trials. The gap between those two numbers is the entire subject of this article.

## What it actually is

Three quite different things get called the same thing, and the distinction predicts the
evidence better than any brand name does.

**Guided digital CBT** — a structured programme, usually 6 to 12 modules, with a real human
supporting you: brief messages, a weekly check-in, sometimes a phone call. Support may be
asynchronous and still count.

**Unguided (self-guided) digital CBT** — the same content, no human. You work through it alone.

**Wellness and meditation apps** — mood tracking, breathing exercises, meditation libraries.
Mostly not CBT, mostly not trialled as treatments, and frequently marketed as though they were.

A fourth category now matters: **prescription digital therapeutics**, which are FDA-cleared and
require a prescription. Our [CBT-I article](/clinic/cbt-i-insomnia) covers the insomnia products
— Somryst and SleepioRx — which are reimbursed under Medicare's Digital Mental Health Treatment
codes as of January 2025. That is a genuinely different regulatory category from an app store
download.

## What the research shows

### The efficacy numbers are real but modest

Linardon and colleagues meta-analyzed **176 randomized controlled trials** of mental health
smartphone apps for depression and anxiety symptoms in *World Psychiatry* (2024) — the largest
synthesis available.

A separate meta-analysis of **92 RCTs and 16,728 participants** found apps significantly improved
clinical outcomes against controls at
[**g = 0.43**](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-025-01567-5) — a small-to-moderate effect.

For **guided** digital CBT specifically, the picture is stronger. A 2022 meta-analysis including
3,800 participants reported a pooled
[**Hedges' g = 0.78 for digital CBT in reducing anxiety symptoms**](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.08.25333326.full.pdf),
and a randomized trial found digital CBT **non-inferior to face-to-face CBT** for generalized
anxiety disorder and social anxiety disorder.

That non-inferiority finding is the important one. For mild-to-moderate depression and anxiety,
therapist-guided digital CBT performs comparably to sitting in a room with a therapist.

### Then the adherence problem, which is severe

Dropout rates for digital CBT programmes
[**range from 25% to 50%**](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.08.25333326.full.pdf).

A 2025 meta-analysis in *JAMA Psychiatry* examined uptake, adherence and attrition across
**79 trials, 92 app conditions and 78 control conditions** — a study designed specifically to
measure the thing efficacy trials tend to report last.

And a striking finding about the state of the field: across 92 RCTs, engagement data were
reported in only 76% of studies, with **25 distinct engagement metrics** used. The authors
concluded that with a quarter of studies not reporting engagement at all,
[establishing overall engagement with mental health apps remains
unfeasible](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-025-01567-5).

We cannot currently say how much of an app people use, because the field has not agreed how to
measure it.

### The guidance question, which just got more complicated

The settled view was that guided digital interventions outperform unguided ones. It is now less
settled.

A 2025 *Lancet Digital Health* meta-analysis of **standalone smartphone apps** found
[non-significant differences for guidance, with considerable
heterogeneity](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/landig/article/PIIS2589-7500(25)00105-0/fulltext?rss=yes)
— and noted directly that this **contrasts with previous research**, offering a plausible
explanation: earlier meta-reviews largely examined *internet-based* rather than *app-based*
interventions.

The same review made an observation this journal keeps encountering. For sleep apps, comparisons
against non-active resources — mostly control apps — yielded larger effect sizes with reduced
heterogeneity, which
[highlights how the choice of control condition can influence both estimated efficacy and
consistency of
results](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/landig/article/PIIS2589-7500(25)00105-0/fulltext?rss=yes).

The comparator decides the answer. Again.

**Where the evidence stands.** Guided digital CBT is well supported and non-inferior to
face-to-face therapy for mild-to-moderate presentations. Standalone apps produce a real but
smaller effect, with considerable heterogeneity, moderate study quality, and an adherence problem
the field cannot yet measure consistently. The *Lancet* reviewers' own recommendation is the
sensible one: standalone apps **might** be offered for depression, anxiety and sleep symptoms
**if no evidence-based first-line intervention is available.**

## What it does not do

**It does not replace treatment for moderate-to-severe conditions.** The non-inferiority evidence
is for mild-to-moderate presentations.

**Downloads are not evidence.** The overwhelming majority of mental health apps have never been
tested. An app being popular, well designed, or endorsed by someone you have heard of tells you
nothing about whether it works.

**"Based on CBT" is not CBT.** Many apps borrow language and a thought-record screen without
delivering a structured protocol. Our [CBT article](/clinic/what-cbt-actually-is) covers what the
actual treatment involves.

**And persuasive design does not appear to help.** The 92-RCT analysis examined persuasive design
principles — streaks, reminders, gamification — and found
[**no significant association with either efficacy or
engagement**](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-025-01567-5). The features that make an app
feel compelling are not the features that make it work.

**A note on privacy.** Mental health apps collect unusually sensitive data, and privacy practices
across the sector are inconsistent. Read what is shared with third parties before you enter
anything you would not want disclosed.

## What using one actually involves

**Guided digital CBT** typically runs 6 to 12 weekly modules. Each has reading or video, an
exercise, and homework — a thought record, an activity schedule, an exposure task. You submit it,
and a supporter responds within a few days.

The support is often brief — a few paragraphs. It is also, on most of the evidence, doing real
work: the human presence changes completion rates even when the content is identical.

**Unguided programmes** are the same content with nobody at the other end. This is where the
dropout figures concentrate.

**Realistically:** week one is easy and interesting. Week three is when most people stop. If you
are going to use one of these, the single most useful thing is to decide in advance what your
week-three plan is — a fixed time, a reminder from a person rather than the app, or a paid
programme you are reluctant to waste.

## Is this available to me?

**Prescription digital therapeutics** require a prescription and are reimbursed in some cases —
see our [CBT-I article](/clinic/cbt-i-insomnia) for the clearest current example.

**Guided programmes** are offered through some employers, universities, health plans and
integrated behavioural health services. Ask your GP or EAP directly; many people have access they
do not know about.

**Public health systems** in several countries offer nationally assessed digital CBT. In the US
the picture is fragmented, and coverage varies by plan.

**Free and low-cost options** exist. Given the evidence, an untested paid app is not obviously
better than an untested free one.

## How to choose one — the questions that matter

- **"Has this specific product been tested in a randomized controlled trial?"** Not the approach —
  the product. Reputable ones publish their trials and will link them.
- **"Is there human support?"** If yes, who, how often, and are they clinically trained?
- **"Is it a structured programme or a library?"** Programmes have an order and an end. Libraries
  have neither, and the evidence is on programmes.
- **"Is it FDA-cleared?"** Very few are. That is a meaningful distinction, not marketing.
- **"What happens to my data?"**

**Two red flags.** Any app claiming to treat a serious condition without regulatory clearance.
And any app whose evidence page cites research on *digital CBT in general* rather than on itself —
a move this journal has flagged in several other articles, including
[sensorimotor psychotherapy](/clinic/sensorimotor-psychotherapy), where evidence for a broad
category gets cited as evidence for one specific product inside it.

## Who this suits

Digital CBT is worth trying if:

- Your symptoms are **mild to moderate**
- You are **waiting for therapy** — this is arguably its best use, and a reasonable thing to ask
  for while on a list
- **Access is the binding constraint** — rural, no transport, no coverage, no time
- You **prefer working alone**, and will realistically do homework without someone expecting it

It suits poorly anyone with moderate-to-severe symptoms, anyone in crisis, and — honestly —
anyone who has abandoned several self-directed programmes already. The dropout data is not a
character judgment, but it is information about what tends to work for whom.

## When this isn't enough

If you have worked through a programme and nothing has shifted, that is a reason to escalate
rather than to try a different app. See our article on
[finding a therapist](/clinic/finding-a-therapist).

If you have a condition with a specific protocol — [OCD](/clinic/erp-for-ocd),
[PTSD](/clinic/prolonged-exposure-ptsd), [insomnia](/clinic/cbt-i-insomnia) — ask whether the
digital programme delivers *that* protocol. For insomnia the answer is genuinely yes, with
regulatory clearance behind it. For the others it is usually no.

**And no app is a crisis service.** If you are in crisis or having thoughts of harming yourself,
contact your doctor, a crisis line, or emergency services.
