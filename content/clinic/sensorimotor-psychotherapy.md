Sensorimotor Psychotherapy sits in a category this journal has visited several times: a
theoretically coherent, widely taught, well-regarded approach with a strikingly thin randomized
evidence base.

We reach a similar conclusion in several other articles, including [Somatic
Experiencing](/journal/somatic-experiencing). SP has less evidence still.

## What it actually is

Developed by Pat Ogden in the 1980s, sensorimotor psychotherapy is described as
[a body-centred talking therapy addressing both the cognitive-emotional aspects and the bodily and
autonomic symptoms of traumatic stress and attachment-related disorders, **without requiring
hands-on
interventions**](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/advances-in-psychiatric-treatment/article/sensorimotor-approaches-to-trauma-treatment/D89DECA6078BBCF28669E8F76090197C).

That last point matters practically: SP is a psychotherapy, not bodywork. No one touches you.

It draws on psychodynamic psychotherapy, gestalt therapy, cognitive-behavioural methods and the
**Hakomi method** of body psychotherapy.

**What makes it distinctive is where attention goes.** A session begins like any therapy, with the
client's narrative. But rather than talking *about* the experience, the therapist directs
attention to the **procedurally learned patterns** the recollection evokes — posture, gesture,
breath, the impulse to curl or push or freeze — and works with those as they appear in the room.

Ogden's argument is that talking has limited direct impact on procedural action tendencies as they
occur in the present moment, and that treatment must address the here-and-now experience of the
traumatic past rather than its narrative content.

The underlying theoretical claim is developmental and hierarchical: that lower, subcortical brain
systems mature before higher ones and that higher-level functioning depends on them — so
regulating autonomic arousal must precede narrative processing or exposure.

## What the research shows

Very little, and we are going to be precise about what exists.

### The pilot study

The most-cited SP research is a pilot by Langmuir, Kirsh and Classen: **ten women** with a history
of childhood abuse completed **20 weekly sessions** of SP-informed group therapy, assessed at
pre-treatment, post-treatment and six-month follow-up.

Its own authors' framing is worth quoting from the Sensorimotor Psychotherapy Institute's
resources page:
[**"To date, there has been no empirical research to support the efficacy of this treatment
approach."**](https://sensorimotorpsychotherapy.org/resources/) That was the state of the field
when the study was written.

**And here is a discrepancy readers should know about.** The Institute's summary reports that
participants demonstrated significant improvement in **body awareness, dissociation, and
receptivity to soothing.**

An independent review of body-oriented psychotherapy reads the same study differently: twenty
weeks of group therapy produced significant improvement in body awareness, maintained at six
months, but
[**no significant change in somatic aspects of dissociation, dissociative experiences, or
receptivity to
soothing**](https://pacja.org.au/article/71153-the-effectiveness-of-body-oriented-psychotherapy-a-review-of-the-literature/attachment/148188.pdf).

We are not in a position to adjudicate that, and we are not suggesting bad faith on either side —
studies report multiple measures and summaries condense. But it is the difference between "helps
with dissociation" and "helps with body awareness only," which is a substantial difference for
someone choosing a trauma treatment. If a practitioner cites this study to you, it is reasonable
to ask which outcomes they mean.

### What has followed

A **pilot randomized controlled trial** of a body-oriented group therapy for complex trauma
survivors — an adaptation of sensorimotor psychotherapy — was published by Classen and colleagues
in 2020. A pilot RCT is a meaningful step up from an uncontrolled study of ten people, and it
remains a pilot.

The broader category has been reviewed: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis of
**body- and movement-oriented interventions for PTSD** covering 29 studies found moderate
improvements in PTSD symptoms alongside benefits for depressive symptoms and sleep quality — with
the authors noting that study quality varied and calling for stronger designs and longer
follow-up.

That review is about the *category*, not about SP. Yoga, dance/movement therapy, body-oriented
group therapy and other approaches sit inside it. It should not be cited as evidence for
sensorimotor psychotherapy specifically, and it sometimes is.

**Where the evidence stands.** A coherent, thoughtfully constructed model with one small uncontrolled pilot, one
pilot RCT of a group adaptation, and supportive evidence for the broader family of body-oriented
approaches that does not isolate this one.

## What it does not do

**It has not been shown to treat PTSD.** No adequately powered randomized trial of individual
sensorimotor psychotherapy against an active comparator exists.

**It is not a first-line trauma treatment**, and should not be presented as equivalent to
[EMDR](/clinic/emdr-ptsd-treatment), [prolonged
exposure](/clinic/prolonged-exposure-ptsd) or [cognitive processing
therapy](/clinic/cognitive-processing-therapy), all of which carry strong guideline
recommendations built on trials with hundreds of participants.

**Its neuroscience framing outruns its outcome evidence.** The hierarchical brain model is a
reasonable organizing metaphor. It is not a demonstrated mechanism, and citing neuroscience is not
the same as demonstrating that a treatment works.

**And "trauma stored in the body" remains unestablished.** Our journal's article on
[fascia](/journal/fascia-and-chinese-medicine) covers this claim directly: bodily sensation
accompanies emotional states, which is well established; the idea that traumatic memory is held in
tissue and can be discharged through it is not.

## What the sessions actually involve

You sit and talk, at first, as in any therapy. Then the therapist begins tracking and naming what
your body is doing while you speak — *I notice your shoulders came up as you said that; would you
be willing to stay with that for a moment?*

The work is deliberately slow and in small increments. Rather than following the story to its
conclusion, the therapist repeatedly interrupts it to attend to what is happening physically.

**Experiments** are characteristic: trying a small movement — pushing against a hand, straightening
the spine, turning the head — and observing what changes emotionally. The claim is that completing
a defensive action that was interrupted at the time alters the pattern.

There is no touch and no requirement to narrate the traumatic event in detail, which is a genuine
part of the appeal for people who cannot face exposure-based work.

Sessions are typically weekly and open-ended.

## Is this available to me?

SP is well disseminated through the Sensorimotor Psychotherapy Institute, which runs a multi-level
training programme, and trained practitioners are reasonably findable in California.

**Ask about the underlying licence.** As with [Somatic
Experiencing](/journal/somatic-experiencing), SP training is open to practitioners from various
backgrounds. For trauma work, an independently licensed clinician — LMFT, LCSW, psychologist,
psychiatrist — is what you want, with SP as an additional training rather than the sole
qualification. Ask for the licence number and check it with the California board.

**Ask what else they are trained in.** A therapist who also knows a guideline-recommended trauma
protocol can tell you honestly when SP is not the right tool.

Billed as psychotherapy where the practitioner is licensed.

## How to ask for it

> *"I'm interested in sensorimotor psychotherapy. Before we start — are you independently
> licensed, and what does the research base look like as you understand it?"*

The second half of that question is a competence filter, and a good practitioner will answer it
straightforwardly: promising model, very limited trials, many clients find it helpful. A
practitioner who tells you SP is an established evidence-based trauma treatment is overstating
what exists.

## Who this suits

Given the evidence, our honest framing: SP is a reasonable option for someone who has tried or
cannot tolerate the guideline-recommended treatments, and who finds a body-oriented approach
intuitively right.

It may suit you if:

- **Exposure-based work has been intolerable** or you have dropped out of it
- Your difficulty presents **physically** — chronic tension, freeze responses, startle,
  disconnection from the body
- You want a **slow, non-narrative** approach

Our practical recommendation is the same one we gave for Somatic Experiencing: **if you have PTSD
and have not tried a first-line trauma treatment, try one first.** The evidence gap is not
marginal. If first-line treatment has failed you or you cannot face it, this is a defensible next
option, chosen with clear eyes.

## When this isn't enough

Set a review point in advance — six or eight sessions — and assess honestly whether anything has
shifted. Open-ended body-oriented work can continue for years without a checkpoint, and the
absence of outcome data means there is no external benchmark to compare against.

If you become destabilized, that is a signal to slow down and possibly to reconsider. Trauma work
without an underlying clinical licence is a real risk, and this is the modality where we would
hold that line most firmly.

And if you are in crisis or in an unsafe situation, stabilization and safety come before any
trauma processing.
